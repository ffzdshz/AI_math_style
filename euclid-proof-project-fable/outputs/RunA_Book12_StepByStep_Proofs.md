## Proposition 12.1
**Statement:** Similar polygons inscribed in circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters.

**Step 1.** Let ABCDE and FGHKL be similar polygons inscribed in the circles ABCDE and FGHKL, the vertices corresponding in the order A, B, C, D, E and F, G, H, K, L, so that BA and GF are corresponding sides; and let BM and GN be diameters of the two circles. Since the polygons are inscribed, every vertex of each lies on the circumference of its circle (D4.3). I say that polygon ABCDE : polygon FGHKL = square on BM : square on GN.

**Step 2.** Join BE, AM, GL, FN (P1).

**Step 3.** Since the polygons are similar, their angles are severally equal and the sides about the equal angles are proportional (D6.1); hence the angle BAE is equal to the angle GFL, and BA : AE = GF : FL.

**Step 4.** The triangles BAE and GFL therefore have one angle equal to one angle and the sides about these equal angles proportional; hence they are equiangular, and the angles subtended by corresponding sides are equal (6.6). In particular the angle AEB, subtended by BA, is equal to the angle FLG, subtended by GF.

**Step 5.** In the circle ABCDE the angles AEB and AMB are angles in the same segment, both standing upon the circumference AB (E and M lying on the same side of AB); therefore the angle AEB is equal to the angle AMB (3.21). For the same reason, in the circle FGHKL the angle FLG is equal to the angle FNG (3.21). Hence, by Step 4, the angle AMB is equal to the angle FNG (CN1).

**Step 6.** Since BM is a diameter, the angle BAM is an angle in the semicircle on BM and is therefore right (3.31); likewise the angle GFN is right (3.31). Hence the angle BAM is equal to the angle GFN (P4).

**Step 7.** In the triangles ABM and FGN the three angles of each are together equal to two right angles (1.32); the angles BAM, AMB together are equal to the angles GFN, FNG together (Steps 5 and 6, CN2); therefore the remaining angle ABM is equal to the remaining angle FGN (CN3). Thus the triangles ABM and FGN are equiangular.

**Step 8.** In equiangular triangles the sides about the equal angles are proportional, corresponding sides being those which subtend equal angles (6.4). About the equal angles ABM and FGN, the sides BA and GF subtend the equal angles AMB and FNG, and the sides BM and GN subtend the equal right angles BAM and GFN; therefore BA : BM = GF : GN.

**Step 9.** Alternately, BA : GF = BM : GN (5.16); that is, the four straight lines BM, GN, BA, GF are proportional: BM : GN = BA : GF.

**Step 10.** Upon BM and GN stand the squares on BM and GN, which are similar and similarly described figures; upon BA and GF stand the polygons ABCDE and FGHKL, which are similar and similarly described figures, BA and GF being corresponding sides. Since the four straight lines BM, GN, BA, GF are proportional, the rectilineal figures similar and similarly described upon them are also proportional (6.22): square on BM : square on GN = polygon ABCDE : polygon FGHKL.

**Step 11.** Therefore the polygon ABCDE is to the polygon FGHKL as the square on BM is to the square on GN; that is, similar polygons inscribed in circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.2
**Statement:** Circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters.

Let ABCD and EFGH be circles, and let BD and FH be diameters of them. I say that, as the circle ABCD is to the circle EFGH, so is the square on BD to the square on FH.

**Step 1.** (Lemma, first part.) Let XY be any straight line fitted into a circle whose centre is Z [3.1], and let W be a point on one of the circumferences cut off by XY such that WX = WY; call the figure bounded by XY and the circumference XWY the segment XWY. Join WX, WY [P1]. Since WX = WY, the angle WXY is equal to the angle WYX [1.5]. Join ZW, and through W draw UV at right angles to ZW [1.11]; since ZW produced is a diameter, UV touches the circle at W and falls outside the circle [3.16]. The angle UWX, which the chord WX makes with the tangent UV, is equal to the angle in the alternate segment, that is, to the angle WYX [3.32]; therefore the angle UWX is equal to the angle WXY [CN1]. But these are alternate angles made by WX falling on the two straight lines UV, XY; therefore UV is parallel to XY [1.27].

**Step 2.** (Lemma, second part.) Through X draw XU parallel to ZW, and through Y draw YV parallel to XU [1.31], meeting UV in U and V. Then XUVY is a parallelogram, and the parallelogram XUVY and the triangle XWY are on the same base XY and in the same parallels XY, UV; therefore the parallelogram XUVY is double of the triangle XWY [1.41]. Again, since UV falls outside the circle [3.16], the circumference XWY lies within the parallelogram XUVY, so that the segment XWY is a part of the parallelogram and is less than it [CN5]. Therefore the triangle XWY is greater than half the segment XWY. This holds for any chord XY and any such point W.

**Step 3.** Let S be the area to which the circle ABCD has the same ratio as the square on BD has to the square on FH (the existence of this fourth proportional being assumed), so that, as the square on BD is to the square on FH, so is the circle ABCD to the area S. I say first that S is not less than the circle EFGH.

**Step 4.** For, if possible, let S be less than the circle EFGH. Let Z be the centre of the circle EFGH [3.1]; through Z draw EG at right angles to FH [1.11], and join EF, FG, GH, HE [P1]; then EFGH is the square inscribed in the circle [4.6], and its sides are all equal [D1.22]. Since EF = EH, the triangle EFH is, by Steps 1–2, greater than half the segment FEH; and since GF = GH, the triangle FGH is greater than half the segment FGH. The two triangles make up the square EFGH, and the two segments make up the whole circle; therefore the inscribed square EFGH is greater than half the circle EFGH.

**Step 5.** Bisect the circumferences EF, FG, GH, HE at the points K, L, M, N [3.30], and join EK, KF, FL, LG, GM, MH, HN, NE [P1]. Since the circumference EK is equal to the circumference KF, EK = KF [3.29]; therefore, by Steps 1–2, the triangle EKF is greater than half the segment EKF. In the same way each of the triangles FLG, GMH, HNE is greater than half the segment about it; hence the four triangles together are greater than half of the four segments which remain when the square is subtracted from the circle.

**Step 6.** Now the circle EFGH and the excess by which the circle EFGH exceeds the area S are two unequal magnitudes, of which the circle is the greater. From the circle there has been subtracted the square, which is greater than its half; from the remainder, the four triangles, which are greater than half of it; and, by bisecting the remaining circumferences and joining the points of bisection, this can be continued as often as we please, each time subtracting triangles greater than half of the remaining segments (Steps 1–2, 5). Therefore there will at length be left some segments of the circle which together are less than the excess by which the circle EFGH exceeds S [10.1]. Let such segments have been left, and, to fix the lettering, let them be the segments on EK, KF, FL, LG, GM, MH, HN, NE, so that the remaining polygon is EKFLGMHN (if further bisections are required, the argument is verbatim the same for the polygon with more sides). Then, since the polygon EKFLGMHN together with these segments makes up the circle EFGH, and the area S together with its excess also makes up the circle EFGH, while the segments are less than the excess, the polygon EKFLGMHN is greater than the area S.

**Step 7.** In the circle ABCD, through its centre [3.1] draw the diameter AC at right angles to BD [1.11] and join AB, BC, CD, DA, so that ABCD is the inscribed square [4.6]; bisect the circumferences AB, BC, CD, DA at O, P, Q, R [3.30], join AO, OB, BP, PC, CQ, QD, DR, RA [P1], and bisect further exactly as many times as was done in the circle EFGH, so as to obtain the polygon AOBPCQDR with the same number of sides as EKFLGMHN. In each circle the sides of the inscribed square are equal [D1.22], so the four circumferences they cut off are equal [3.28], and the parts obtained by bisecting them equally often are equal. Hence each polygon is equilateral, its sides subtending equal circumferences [3.29], and equiangular, its angles standing upon equal circumferences [3.27]. Since the polygons have the same number n of sides, and the interior angles of each are together equal to 2n − 4 right angles [1.32], each angle of either polygon is the n-th part of the same sum; so the angles of the polygon AOBPCQDR are severally equal to the angles of the polygon EKFLGMHN. And, all the sides of the one being equal to one another and all the sides of the other likewise, the sides about the equal angles are proportional [5.7]. Therefore the polygon AOBPCQDR is similar to the polygon EKFLGMHN [D6.1].

**Step 8.** Therefore, as the square on BD is to the square on FH, so is the polygon AOBPCQDR to the polygon EKFLGMHN [12.1]. But, as the square on BD is to the square on FH, so is the circle ABCD to the area S; therefore, as the circle ABCD is to the area S, so is the polygon AOBPCQDR to the polygon EKFLGMHN [5.11]. Now the circle ABCD is greater than the polygon AOBPCQDR inscribed in it, the polygon being a part of the circle [CN5]; therefore the area S is greater than the polygon EKFLGMHN [5.14]. But it was proved (Step 6) that the area S is less than the polygon EKFLGMHN: which is impossible. Therefore it is not true that, as the square on BD is to the square on FH, so is the circle ABCD to some area less than the circle EFGH.

**Step 9.** In exactly the same way, interchanging the two circles, it is proved that neither is it true that, as the square on FH is to the square on BD, so is the circle EFGH to some area less than the circle ABCD.

**Step 10.** I say next that S is not greater than the circle EFGH. For, if possible, let S be greater than the circle EFGH. Since, as the square on BD is to the square on FH, so is the circle ABCD to S, inversely, as the square on FH is to the square on BD, so is the area S to the circle ABCD [5.7]. Let T be the area to which the circle EFGH has the same ratio as the area S has to the circle ABCD (again assuming the fourth proportional), so that, as S is to the circle ABCD, so is the circle EFGH to T. Since S is greater than the circle EFGH, the circle ABCD is greater than T [5.14]. Also, as the square on FH is to the square on BD, so is the circle EFGH to T [5.11]; that is, as the square on FH is to the square on BD, so is the circle EFGH to an area T less than the circle ABCD: which was proved impossible in Step 9. Therefore S is not greater than the circle EFGH.

**Step 11.** Since S is neither less nor greater than the circle EFGH, S is equal to the circle EFGH. Therefore, as the circle ABCD is to S, so is the circle ABCD to the circle EFGH [5.7]; and hence, as the square on BD is to the square on FH, so is the circle ABCD to the circle EFGH [5.11]. Therefore circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters. Q.E.D.

Remarks on path choice: (i) In Step 8 the inequality S > polygon EKFLGMHN follows from 5.14 applied directly to the proportion circle ABCD : S = polygon AOBPCQDR : polygon EKFLGMHN (first term greater than third), so alternation (5.16) is not needed. (ii) Tangents at points of the circumference (Step 1) are drawn as perpendiculars to the radius by 1.11 and the porism to 3.16, since 3.17 concerns a point outside the circle. (iii) The existence of a fourth proportional to two squares and a circle (Steps 3 and 10) is assumed, no proposition constructing a fourth proportional to non-rectilineal areas being available; this assumption is inherent in the method.

## Proposition 12.3
**Statement:** Any pyramid which has a triangular base is divided into two pyramids equal and similar to one another, similar to the whole and having triangular bases, and into two equal prisms; and the two prisms are greater than the half of the whole pyramid.

**Step 1.** Let ABC–D be a pyramid with triangular base ABC and vertex D (throughout, "pyramid XYZ–V" denotes the pyramid with base XYZ and vertex V). Bisect the six edges AB, BC, CA, AD, DB, DC at E, F, G, H, K, L respectively [1.10], and join HE, EG, GH, HK, KL, LH, KF, FG [P1].

**Step 2.** Since AE = EB and AH = HD, the sides AB, AD of triangle ABD are cut proportionally at E and H; hence EH is parallel to BD [6.2]. Likewise DH = HA and DK = KB give HK parallel to AB [6.2]. Thus EBKH is a parallelogram, so HK = EB and EH = BK [1.34]. In the same way, in triangle ACD, HG is parallel to DC and HL parallel to AC [6.2], so GCLH is a parallelogram with HL = GC and HG = CL [1.34]; in triangle ABC, EG is parallel to BC and GF parallel to AB [6.2], so EBFG is a parallelogram with EG = BF and GF = EB [1.34]; and in triangle DBC, KL is parallel to BC and KF parallel to DC [6.2], so KFCL is a parallelogram with KL = FC and KF = CL [1.34].

**Step 3.** Since E, F, G, H, K, L are the midpoints, the equalities of Step 2 give [CN1]: HK = GF = EB = AE, each half of AB; HL = GC = AG, each half of AC; KL = FC = BF = EG, each half of BC; EH = BK = KD, each half of BD; HG = CL = LD = KF, each half of CD; and AH = HD, half of AD.

**Step 4.** (Face AEH and face HKD.) Since HK is parallel to AB and AD falls on them, the exterior angle KHD is equal to the interior and opposite angle EAH [1.29]. Also AE = HK and AH = HD [Step 3]. Therefore EH = KD, and triangle AEH is equal and similar to triangle HKD [1.4].

**Step 5.** (Face AGH and face HLD.) Similarly, since HL is parallel to AC, angle LHD is equal to angle GAH [1.29]; AG = HL and AH = HD [Step 3]; therefore GH = LD, and triangle AGH is equal and similar to triangle HLD [1.4].

**Step 6.** (Face EHG and face KDL.) The straight lines HE, HG meeting at H are parallel respectively to DK, DL meeting at D (for HE is parallel to BD and HG to DC), and they are not in the same plane; hence angle EHG is equal to angle KDL [11.10]. Also HE = DK and HG = DL [Step 3]. Therefore EG = KL, and triangle EHG is equal and similar to triangle KDL [1.4].

**Step 7.** (Face AEG and face HKL.) The straight lines HK, HL meeting at H are parallel respectively to AB, AC meeting at A, and are not in the same plane; hence angle KHL is equal to angle EAG [11.10]. Also HK = AE and HL = AG [Step 3]. Therefore triangle HKL is equal and similar to triangle AEG [1.4].

**Step 8.** The pyramid AEG–H is contained by the four triangles AEG, AEH, AGH, EHG, and the pyramid HKL–D by the four triangles HKL, HKD, HLD, KDL [D11.12]. By Steps 4–7 these are planes equal in multitude and respectively equal and similar. Therefore pyramid AEG–H is equal and similar to pyramid HKL–D [D11.10]; and each of them has a triangular base.

**Step 9.** (Similarity of the faces to those of the whole.) In triangles DHK and DAB the sides DH, DK, HK are respectively halves of DA, DB, AB [Step 3]; hence DH : DK = DA : DB, DK : KH = DB : BA and DH : HK = DA : AB [5.15], that is, the two triangles have their sides proportional. Therefore they are equiangular, the equal angles being subtended by the corresponding sides [6.5]; and since their angles are severally equal and the sides about the equal angles are proportional, triangle DHK is similar to triangle DAB [D6.1]. In exactly the same way, since its sides are halves of the corresponding sides, triangle DKL is similar to triangle DBC, triangle DLH to triangle DCA, and triangle HKL (whose sides HK, KL, LH are halves of AB, BC, CA) to triangle ABC [5.15, 6.5, D6.1].

**Step 10.** Hence pyramid HKL–D is contained by four planes similar respectively to the four planes ABC, ABD, ACD, BCD containing pyramid ABC–D, and so is similar to the whole pyramid [D11.9]. The same reasoning applies to pyramid AEG–H, whose faces AEG, AEH, AGH, EHG have sides respectively halves of those of ABC, ABD, ACD, BCD [Step 3]; so it also is similar to the whole pyramid [5.15, 6.5, D6.1, D11.9].

**Step 11.** (The common face HKFG.) HK and GF are each parallel to AB [Step 2] and are not in one plane with it; hence HK is parallel to GF [11.9]. Likewise HG and KF are each parallel to DC; hence HG is parallel to KF [11.9]. Therefore HKFG is a parallelogram.

**Step 12.** (The first prism.) The straight lines HE, HG meeting at H are parallel respectively to KB, KF meeting at K [Steps 2, 11], and are not in the same plane; hence the planes EHG and BKF are parallel [11.15], and angle EHG is equal to angle BKF [11.10]. Also HE = KB and HG = KF [Step 3]; therefore triangle EHG is equal and similar to triangle BKF [1.4]. Thus the solid contained by the two triangles BKF, EHG, which are equal, similar and parallel, and by the three parallelograms EBFG, EBKH, HKFG is a prism [D11.13]; call it the first prism.

**Step 13.** (The second prism.) The straight lines KH, KL meeting at K are parallel respectively to FG, FC meeting at F [Steps 2, 11], and are not in the same plane; hence the planes HKL and GFC are parallel [11.15], and angle HKL is equal to angle GFC [11.10]. Also KH = FG and KL = FC [Step 3]; therefore triangle HKL is equal and similar to triangle GFC [1.4]. Thus the solid contained by the two triangles GFC, HKL and the three parallelograms KFCL, LCGH, HKFG is a prism [D11.13]; call it the second prism.

**Step 14.** The plane EHG cuts off from the pyramid ABC–D the pyramid AEG–H, the plane HKL cuts off the pyramid HKL–D, and the plane of the parallelogram HKFG divides the solid that remains into the first and the second prism. Thus the whole pyramid is divided into the two pyramids and the two prisms.

**Step 15.** (The two prisms are equal.) Triangles GBF and GFC are on the equal bases BF, FC and in the same parallels BC, EG; hence they are equal [1.38]. Parallelogram EBFG and triangle GBF have the same base BF and are in the same parallels BF, EG; hence the parallelogram is double of the triangle [1.41]. Therefore parallelogram EBFG is double of triangle GFC.

**Step 16.** The first prism has the parallelogram EBFG as base and the straight line HK opposite; the second prism has the triangle GFC as base and the triangle HKL opposite. Since HK lies in the plane HKL, which is parallel to the plane ABC [Step 13], the two prisms are of equal height. And the parallelogram EBFG is double of the triangle GFC [Step 15]. Therefore the first prism is equal to the second prism [11.39].

**Step 17.** (The two prisms exceed the half.) Join EF and EK [P1]. The pyramid EBF–K is contained by the triangles EBF, EBK, BFK, EFK [D11.12]; it is a part of the first prism, being cut off from it by the plane EFK, since its vertices E, B, F, K are all vertices of that prism. Therefore the first prism is greater than the pyramid EBF–K [CN5].

**Step 18.** Since EG is parallel to BC and AB falls on them, angle EBF is equal to angle AEG [1.29]; also EB = AE and BF = EG [Step 3]; therefore EF = AG, and triangle EBF is equal and similar to triangle AEG [1.4]. Since EH is parallel to BD, angle EBK is equal to angle AEH [1.29]; also EB = AE and BK = EH; therefore EK = AH, and triangle EBK is equal and similar to triangle AEH [1.4].

**Step 19.** The straight lines BF, BK meeting at B are parallel respectively to EG, EH meeting at E, and are not in the same plane; hence angle FBK is equal to angle GEH [11.10]; with BF = EG and BK = EH, it follows that FK = GH and triangle BFK is equal and similar to triangle EGH [1.4]. Finally, in triangles EFK and AGH, EF = AG, EK = AH and FK = GH; hence angle FEK is equal to angle GAH [1.8], and triangle EFK is equal and similar to triangle AGH [1.4].

**Step 20.** Therefore the pyramid EBF–K is contained by four planes respectively equal and similar to the four planes containing the pyramid AEG–H, and so the two pyramids are equal [D11.10]. Hence, by Step 17, the first prism is greater than the pyramid AEG–H.

**Step 21.** But the second prism is equal to the first prism [Step 16], and the pyramid HKL–D is equal to the pyramid AEG–H [Step 8]; therefore the second prism is also greater than the pyramid HKL–D. Consequently the two prisms together are greater than the two pyramids together.

**Step 22.** The whole pyramid ABC–D consists of the two pyramids and the two prisms [Step 14]. Since the two prisms are greater than the two pyramids, twice the two prisms exceed the two prisms together with the two pyramids, that is, the whole pyramid. Therefore the two prisms are greater than the half of the whole pyramid.

Therefore any pyramid with a triangular base is divided into two pyramids equal and similar to one another, similar to the whole and having triangular bases, and into two equal prisms; and the two prisms are greater than the half of the whole pyramid. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.4
**Statement:** If there be two pyramids of the same height which have triangular bases, and cach of them be divided into two pyramids equal to one another and similar to the whole, and into two equal prisms, then, as the base of the one pyramid is to the base of the other pyramid, so will all the prisms in the one pyramid be to all the prisms, being equal in multitude, in the other pyramid.

**Statement:** If there be two pyramids of the same height which have triangular bases, and each of them be divided into two pyramids equal to one another and similar to the whole, and into two equal prisms, then, as the base of the one pyramid is to the base of the other pyramid, so will all the prisms in the one pyramid be to all the prisms, being equal in multitude, in the other pyramid.

**Step 1.** Let ABCG and DEFH be two pyramids of the same height, having the triangular bases ABC, DEF and the vertices G, H. From G and H let GZ and HZ' be drawn perpendicular to the planes ABC and DEF respectively (11.11); these perpendiculars are the heights of the pyramids (D6.4), so that by hypothesis GZ = HZ'. Divide each pyramid as in 12.3. In ABCG bisect AB, BC, CA at K, O, L and GA, GB, GC at P, M, N; then ABCG is divided into the two pyramids AKLP and GPMN, equal to one another and similar to the whole, and into two equal prisms, namely the prism I whose base is the parallelogram KBOL and whose opposite edge is PM, and the prism II whose base is the triangle LOC and whose opposite face is the triangle PMN (12.3). Likewise in DEFH bisect DE, EF, FD at Q, V, R and HD, HE, HF at S, T, U; then DEFH is divided into two pyramids and into two equal prisms, the prism I' with base the parallelogram QEVR and opposite edge ST, and the prism II' with base the triangle RVF and opposite face the triangle STU (12.3). I say that, as the base ABC is to the base DEF, so are the prisms I and II together to the prisms I' and II' together.

**Step 2.** Since CL = LA and CO = OB, the ratios CL : LA and CO : OB are each the ratio of a magnitude to an equal magnitude and are therefore the same ratio (D5.5); thus the sides CA, CB of the triangle ABC are cut proportionally at L and O, and LO is parallel to AB (6.2). For the same reason RV is parallel to DE.

**Step 3.** Since LO is parallel to AB, the angle CLO is equal to the angle CAB and the angle COL to the angle CBA (1.29), and the angle at C is common; so the triangle LOC is equiangular with the triangle ABC, and the sides about the equal angles are proportional (6.4). Therefore the triangle LOC is similar to the triangle ABC (D6.1), C corresponding to C, O to B and L to A, so that LOC and ABC are similarly described upon CO and CB. In the same way the triangle RVF is similar to the triangle DEF and similarly described upon FV and FE.

**Step 4.** BC is double of CO, and EF is double of FV. Since parts have the same ratio as the same multiples of them taken in corresponding order, CO : FV = BC : EF (5.15); and alternately CO : BC = FV : EF (5.16).

**Step 5.** The four straight lines CO, BC, FV, EF are thus proportional; on CO and BC are described the similar and similarly situated triangles LOC and ABC, and on FV and EF the similar and similarly situated triangles RVF and DEF. Therefore triangle LOC : triangle ABC = triangle RVF : triangle DEF (6.22), and alternately triangle LOC : triangle RVF = triangle ABC : triangle DEF (5.16).

**Step 6.** [Lemma: the prisms II and II' are to one another as the triangles LOC and RVF.] First, the prisms II and II' are of the same height. The triangles LOC and PMN are the two opposite faces of the prism II, so the plane PMN is parallel to the plane ABC (D11.13). The two straight lines GC and GZ drawn from G are cut by the parallel planes PMN and ABC, GC at N and C, and GZ at the point Y in which it meets the plane PMN and at Z; therefore GN : NC = GY : YZ (11.17). But GN = NC; therefore GY = YZ (D5.5). Hence YZ, the perpendicular from the point Y of the face PMN to the plane ABC, which is the height of the prism II, is half of GZ. In the same way the height Y'Z' of the prism II', cut off from HZ' by the plane STU, is half of HZ'. But the solid BL is equal to the solid ER; hence the solid BL is to the solid ER as the solid ER is to the solid ER [5.7]; and the solid ER is to the solid ER as the pyramid DEFH is to the pyramid DEFH [5.15]. Therefore the pyramid ABCG is to the pyramid DEFH as the pyramid DEFH is to the pyramid DEFH [5.11].

**Step 14.** The pyramids ABCG, DEFH thus have the same ratio to the same magnitude, the pyramid DEFH; therefore the pyramid ABCG is equal to the pyramid DEFH [5.9].

**Step 15.** Therefore in equal pyramids which have triangular bases the bases are reciprocally proportional to the heights; and those pyramids in which the bases are reciprocally proportional to the heights are equal. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.10
**Statement:** Any cone is a third part of the cylinder which has the same base with it and equal height.

**Step 1.** Let the cone have for its base the circle ABCD, with centre O, and for its vertex the point L, so that LO is its axis and is at right angles to the plane of the circle [D11.18]; and let the cylinder have the same circle ABCD for its base and the same height as the cone [D11.21], so that its other base lies in the plane drawn through L parallel to the plane of the circle. I say that the cylinder is triple of the cone, that is, that the cone is a third part of the cylinder. For, if the cylinder is not triple of the cone, it is either greater than triple or less than triple of the cone. Before treating the two cases, some constructions are made in the plane of the circle (Steps 2–7).

**Step 2.** Draw two diameters AC, BD of the circle at right angles to one another and join AB, BC, CD, DA; then ABCD is a square inscribed in the circle [4.6]. Through A, B, C, D draw the tangents to the circle; they form a square PQRS circumscribed about the circle, P being the corner between the tangents at A and B, Q that between the tangents at B and C, and so on [4.7]. In that construction the tangent at A is shown to be parallel to BD and the tangent at B parallel to AC, so that OAPB is a parallelogram; likewise OBQC, OCRD, ODSA are parallelograms.

**Step 3.** The parallelogram OAPB is bisected by its diameter AB, so that the triangle OAB is half of OAPB [1.34]; in the same way the triangles OBC, OCD, ODA are halves of OBQC, OCRD, ODSA. Now the four triangles OAB, OBC, OCD, ODA make up the square ABCD, and the four parallelograms OAPB, OBQC, OCRD, ODSA make up the square PQRS; therefore the square ABCD is half of the square PQRS. Moreover the sides of PQRS touch the circle and do not cut it [D3.2], so the circle ABCD lies wholly within the square PQRS.

**Step 4.** Bisect the arcs AB, BC, CD, DA at the points E, F, G, H [3.30] and join AE, EB, BF, FC, CG, GD, DH, HA [P1]. By the construction of 3.30, E lies on the straight line drawn at right angles to AB from its middle point N, so that AN = NB and the angles ANE, BNE are right. Since NE cuts the chord AB into two equal parts and at right angles, the centre O lies on the straight line NE [3.1, Por.]; hence the straight line through E and O is a diameter of the circle having E as an extremity.

**Step 5.** Through E draw MK parallel to AB [1.31]. The straight line NE falls on the parallels AB, MK, so the interior angles on the same side, ANE and NEM, are together equal to two right angles [1.29]; as the angle ANE is right, the angle NEM is right. Thus MK is drawn at right angles to the diameter EO from its extremity E, and therefore MK touches the circle at E [3.16, Por.] and does not cut it [D3.2]. Through A and B draw AM, BK parallel to NE [1.31], meeting MK at M and K. Then ANEM and NBKE are parallelograms (each having its opposite sides parallel), and together they make up the quadrilateral ABKM. Since MK touches the circle at E without cutting it, the arc AEB lies between the chord AB and the line MK; so the segment AEB of the circle cut off by the chord AB lies wholly within the quadrilateral ABKM.

**Step 6.** The parallelogram ANEM is bisected by its diameter AE and the parallelogram NBKE by its diameter EB [1.34]; hence the triangle ANE equals the triangle AME, and the triangle NBE equals the triangle EKB. Adding, the triangle AEB, which is made up of ANE and NBE, equals the triangles AME and EKB together [CN2]; and these two triangles together with AEB make up the whole quadrilateral ABKM. Therefore the quadrilateral ABKM is double of the triangle AEB. Making the same construction on BC, CD, DA, we obtain quadrilaterals on BC, CD, DA which are respectively double of the triangles BFC, CGD, DHA and which enclose the segments of the circle about BC, CD, DA. The same holds whenever any arc is bisected and the triangle on its chord is formed, as will be done repeatedly below.

**Step 7.** Consider the polygon AEBFCGDH inscribed in the circle (what follows applies equally to any polygon inscribed in the circle by continued bisection of arcs). Join OA, OE, OB, OF, OC, OG, OD, OH [P1]; these divide the polygon into the eight triangles OAE, OEB, OBF, OFC, OCG, OGD, ODH, OHA. Let a prism be set up on the polygon with height equal to that of the cylinder, its lateral faces lying in the planes through the sides of the polygon parallel to the axis LO and its upper face in the plane of the upper base of the cylinder; and let a pyramid be set up on the polygon with vertex L. The planes through the axis LO and the points A, E, B, F, C, G, D, H divide the prism into eight prisms on the eight triangles, each of height equal to the cylinder, and divide the pyramid into eight pyramids on the same triangles, each with vertex L and therefore of the same height. Each of these prisms is triple of the pyramid which has the same triangular base and equal height [12.7, Por.]; therefore all the prisms together, which make up the prism on the polygon, are triple of all the pyramids together, which make up the pyramid on the polygon [5.1]. That is, the prism on the polygon AEBFCGDH with height equal to the cylinder is triple of the pyramid on the same polygon with vertex L. Further, the base of the prism lies within the circle and its height equals that of the cylinder, so the prism is enclosed by the cylinder and is less than it [CN5]; and the base of the pyramid lies within the circle and its vertex is L, so the pyramid is enclosed by the cone and is less than it [CN5].

**Step 8.** First suppose, if possible, that the cylinder is greater than triple of the cone, and let X be the excess by which the cylinder exceeds triple of the cone. On the square ABCD and on the square PQRS set up parallelepipedal solids of height equal to the cylinder, their lateral edges parallel to the axis and their upper faces in the plane of the upper base of the cylinder. Parallelepipedal solids of the same height are to one another as their bases [11.32]; and the square ABCD is half of the square PQRS (Step 3); therefore the solid on ABCD is half of the solid on PQRS. But the circle lies within PQRS (Step 3) and the heights are the same, so the cylinder is enclosed by the solid on PQRS and is less than it [CN5]. Hence the solid on ABCD, the prism on the square ABCD of height equal to the cylinder, is greater than half of the cylinder.

**Step 9.** When the prism on ABCD is taken away from the cylinder, there remain the four segments of the cylinder about AB, BC, CD, DA, namely the parts of the cylinder cut off by the planes through AB, BC, CD, DA parallel to the axis. On the triangles AEB, BFC, CGD, DHA set up prisms of height equal to the cylinder, and on the parallelograms ANEM, NBKE (and on the corresponding parallelograms on BC, CD, DA) set up parallelepipedal solids of the same height. The solid on ANEM is cut by the plane through AE and the diagonal of the opposite face, and is bisected by that plane [11.28]; therefore the prism on ANE equals the prism on AME. In the same way the solid on NBKE is bisected by the plane through EB and the diagonal of the opposite face, so that the prism on NBE equals the prism on EKB. Adding, the prism on AEB, which is made up of the prisms on ANE and NBE, equals the prisms on AME and EKB together [CN2]; and these together with the prism on AEB make up the whole solid on ABKM, that is, the solid composed of the solids on ANEM and NBKE. Therefore the prism on AEB is half of the solid on ABKM.

**Step 10.** The segment of the circle about AB lies within ABKM (Step 5) and the heights are equal, so the segment of the cylinder about AB is enclosed by the solid on ABKM and is less than it [CN5]; hence the prism on AEB, being half of the solid on ABKM, is greater than half of the segment of the cylinder about AB. Likewise the prisms on BFC, CGD, DHA are greater than half of the segments of the cylinder about BC, CD, DA respectively; so the four prisms together are greater than half of the four segments together, that is, greater than half of what was left of the cylinder.

**Step 11.** Thus from the cylinder there has been taken a magnitude greater than its half, and from the remainder a magnitude greater than its half. Bisecting the arcs that are left, joining the chords, setting up on the triangles so formed prisms of height equal to the cylinder (each of which is greater than half of the segment of the cylinder about it, by the argument of Steps 9–10), and continuing in this way, we shall at last leave some segments of the cylinder which together are less than the excess X [10.1]. Let the process be stopped at such a stage, and for definiteness let the polygon then inscribed be AEBFCGDH (the reasoning is the same for the polygon of any stage), so that the segments left are those about AE, EB, BF, FC, CG, GD, DH, HA. What remains of the cylinder when these segments are taken away is the prism on the polygon AEBFCGDH with height equal to the cylinder. Since the cylinder exceeds triple of the cone by X, and the segments taken away are together less than X, this prism is greater than triple of the cone.

**Step 12.** But the prism on AEBFCGDH with height equal to the cylinder is triple of the pyramid on AEBFCGDH with vertex L (Step 7). Therefore triple of that pyramid is greater than triple of the cone, and consequently the pyramid on AEBFCGDH with vertex L is greater than the cone. But this pyramid is enclosed by the cone and is less than it (Step 7) [CN5]: which is impossible. Therefore the cylinder is not greater than triple of the cone.

**Step 13.** Next suppose, if possible, that the cylinder is less than triple of the cone. Then the cone is greater than a third part of the cylinder; let Y be the excess by which the cone exceeds a third part of the cylinder. On the square ABCD and on the square PQRS set up pyramids with vertex L. These are pyramids of the same height (their common vertex L being at the same distance from the plane of their bases) with polygonal bases, and so are to one another as their bases [12.6]; since the square ABCD is half of PQRS (Step 3), the pyramid on ABCD is half of the pyramid on PQRS. But the circle lies within PQRS (Step 3), so the cone is enclosed by the pyramid on PQRS with vertex L and is less than it [CN5]. Hence the pyramid on ABCD with vertex L is greater than half of the cone.

**Step 14.** When this pyramid is taken away from the cone, there remain the four segments of the cone about AB, BC, CD, DA, namely the parts of the cone cut off by the planes through L and AB, BC, CD, DA. On the triangles AEB, BFC, CGD, DHA and on the quadrilateral ABKM (and the corresponding quadrilaterals on BC, CD, DA) set up pyramids with vertex L. The pyramids on AEB and on ABKM have the same height and are to one another as their bases [12.6]; since ABKM is double of AEB (Step 6), the pyramid on ABKM is double of the pyramid on AEB. But the segment of the circle about AB lies within ABKM (Step 5), so the segment of the cone about AB is enclosed by the pyramid on ABKM with vertex L and is less than it [CN5]. Hence the pyramid on AEB with vertex L is greater than half of the segment of the cone about AB. Likewise the pyramids on BFC, CGD, DHA are greater than half of the segments of the cone about BC, CD, DA; so the four pyramids together are greater than half of what was left of the cone.

**Step 15.** Thus from the cone there has been taken more than its half, and from the remainder more than its half. Bisecting the arcs that are left, joining the chords, setting up on the triangles so formed pyramids with vertex L (each greater than half of the segment of the cone about it, by the argument of Step 14), and continuing in this way, we shall at last leave some segments of the cone which together are less than the excess Y [10.1]. Let the polygon then inscribed be AEBFCGDH, so that the segments left are those about AE, EB, BF, FC, CG, GD, DH, HA. What remains of the cone when these segments are taken away is the pyramid on AEBFCGDH with vertex L. Since the cone exceeds a third part of the cylinder by Y, and the segments taken away are together less than Y, this pyramid is greater than a third part of the cylinder.

**Step 16.** But the pyramid on AEBFCGDH with vertex L is a third part of the prism on AEBFCGDH with height equal to the cylinder (Step 7). Therefore a third part of that prism is greater than a third part of the cylinder, and consequently the prism is greater than the cylinder. But the prism is enclosed by the cylinder and is less than it (Step 7) [CN5]: which is impossible. Therefore the cylinder is not less than triple of the cone.

**Step 17.** Since the cylinder is neither greater nor less than triple of the cone, it is triple of the cone. Therefore the cone is a third part of the cylinder which has the same base with it and equal height. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.11
**Statement:** Cones and cylinders which are of the same height are to one another as their bases.

**Step 1.** Let there be cones and cylinders of the same height, whose bases are the circles ABCD, EFGH [D11.20, D11.23]; let K, M be the centres of these circles, KL, MN the axes of the cones [D11.19], L, N their vertices, and AC, EG diameters of the bases; call the cones AL and EN, and let the cylinders be those standing on the bases ABCD, EFGH with the same height. I say that, as the circle ABCD is to the circle EFGH, so is the cone AL to the cone EN, and so is the cylinder on ABCD to the cylinder on EFGH.

**Step 2.** Suppose the circle ABCD is not to the circle EFGH as the cone AL is to the cone EN. Then let it be contrived that, as the circle ABCD is to the circle EFGH, so is the cone AL to some solid O, where O is not equal to the cone EN; O is therefore either less or greater than the cone EN.

**Step 3.** First let O be less than the cone EN, and let the solid X be the excess by which the cone EN exceeds O, so that the cone EN is equal to O and X together.

**Step 4.** Inscribe in the circle EFGH the square EFGH [4.6]; by that construction its diagonals EG, FH are diameters of the circle. Since the angle EFG is right, the square on EG is equal to the squares on EF, FG together [1.47], and since EF is equal to FG this is double the square EFGH. Hence the square EFGH is half the circumscribed square. The circle lies within the circumscribed square, because each side of that square touches the circle without cutting it [D3.2]; so the circle is less than the circumscribed square [CN5]. Therefore the square EFGH is greater than half the circle EFGH.

**Step 5.** On the square EFGH set up a pyramid with vertex N, and on the circumscribed square set up a pyramid with vertex N. Their bases lie in the plane of the circle EFGH and they have the same vertex, so they have the same height, namely the perpendicular MN from N to that plane [D6.4]. Therefore the pyramid on EFGH is to the pyramid on the circumscribed square as the square EFGH is to the circumscribed square [12.6]; hence the pyramid on EFGH is half the pyramid on the circumscribed square. The cone EN lies within the pyramid on the circumscribed square, since its base lies within that square and it has the same vertex N; so the cone is less than that pyramid [CN5]. Therefore the pyramid on EPF is to the pyramid on the parallelogram as the triangle EPF is to the parallelogram [12.6], so that the pyramid on EPF is half the pyramid on the parallelogram. Therefore the pyramid with base EPF and vertex N is greater than half the segment of the cone standing on EPF; and similarly for the pyramids with vertex N on the triangles FQG, GRH, HSE.

Therefore, as the cone AL is to the solid O, so is the pyramid with base AUBVCWDZ and vertex L to the pyramid with base EPFQGRHS and vertex N [5.11].

Since the same multiples of magnitudes have the same ratio as the magnitudes themselves taken in corresponding order, as the cone AL is to the cone EN, so is the cylinder on ABCD to the cylinder on EFGH [5.15]. Therefore also, as the circle ABCD is to the circle EFGH, so is the cylinder on ABCD to the cylinder on EFGH [5.11].

**Step 18.** Therefore cones and cylinders which are of the same height are to one another as their bases. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.12
**Statement:** Similar cones and cylinders are to one another in the triplicate ratio of the diameters in their bases.

**Step 1.** Let there be similar cones whose bases are the circles ABCD, EFGH with centres K, M and diameters BD, FH, whose axes are KL, MN, and whose vertices are L, N; call them the cones ABCDL and EFGHN. Since the cones are similar, their axes and the diameters of their bases are proportional (D11.24): BD : FH = KL : MN. I say that the cone ABCDL has to the cone EFGHN the ratio triplicate of that which BD has to FH.

**Step 2.** Each cone is generated by a right-angled triangle turned about one of the sides containing the right angle; the side which remains fixed is the axis, and the other side about the right angle describes the base (D11.18, D11.19, D11.20). As the triangle turns, the moving side, which is always at right angles to the axis, takes the position of every radius of the base. Hence the axis KL is at right angles to every radius of the circle ABCD drawn from K, and MN to every radius of the circle EFGH drawn from M: the angles which KL makes with KA, KB, … and MN with ME, MF, … are right, and all these right angles are equal to one another (P4).

**Step 3.** Since K is the centre, KB = KD (D1.15), so that BD is double KB; likewise FH is double MF. Parts have the same ratio as the same multiples of them taken in corresponding order (5.15), so KB : MF = BD : FH. But BD : FH = KL : MN (Step 1); therefore KB : MF = KL : MN (5.11), and, alternately, KB : KL = MF : MN (5.16). Every radius KX of the circle ABCD is equal to KB and every radius MY of the circle EFGH is equal to MF (D1.15), so KX : KL = KB : KL and MY : MN = MF : MN (5.7); hence for any radii KX, MY we have KX : KL = MY : MN (5.11).

**Step 4.** Suppose that the cone ABCDL has not to the cone EFGHN the ratio triplicate of BD : FH. Then the cone ABCDL has that triplicate ratio to some solid O which is either less or greater than the cone EFGHN. First let O be less than the cone EFGHN.

**Step 5.** Inscribe in the circle EFGH the square EFGH (4.6) and circumscribe about the circle a square (4.7); by the construction of 4.7 each side of the circumscribed square is equal to the diameter FH, so the circumscribed square is the square on FH. In the inscribed square the angle FEH is right and EF = EH (D1.22), so the square on FH is equal to the squares on EF, EH together (1.47), that is, to double the square EFGH. Therefore the inscribed square EFGH is half of the circumscribed square.

**Step 6.** Set up on the square EFGH the pyramid with vertex N, and on the circumscribed square the pyramid with the same vertex N. These pyramids are of the same height, so they are to one another as their bases (12.6); hence the pyramid on the inscribed square is half of the pyramid on the circumscribed square. ## Proposition 12.13
**Statement:** If a cylinder be cut by a plane which is parallel to its opposite planes, then, as the cylinder is to the cylinder, so will the axis be to the axis.

**Step 1.** Let AD be a cylinder whose axis is EF and whose bases are the circles AB and CD, with centres E and F; thus AD is generated by the revolution about EF of the rectangle EACF, whose sides EA, FC are radii of the bases and whose side AC is opposite to EF and parallel to it [D11.21, D11.22, D11.23]. Let the cylinder be cut by the plane GH parallel to its opposite planes AB, CD; let this plane meet the axis EF at K and the side AC at G, and let it divide AD into the cylinder BG, whose axis is EK, and the cylinder GD, whose axis is KF. I say that, as the cylinder BG is to the cylinder GD, so is the axis EK to the axis KF.

**Step 2.** First, the axis of any such cylinder is at right angles to its bases. For, since the generating parallelogram is rectangular, the revolving side EA makes a right angle with the fixed side EF in every position [D11.21]; therefore EF makes right angles with every straight line drawn from E to the circumference of the base AB, that is, with all the straight lines in the plane of AB which meet it, and so EF is at right angles to the plane of AB [D11.3]. Similarly FE is at right angles to the plane of CD. Consequently the height of the cylinder, the perpendicular between its bases, is its axis; and any cylinders whose axes are equal are of the same height.

**Step 3.** Next, let any plane parallel to the plane AB be drawn through a point X of the axis EF, or of EF produced [P2], the rectangle EACF being produced as far as necessary. The plane of the rectangle contains X and the radius EA; hence the two parallel planes are cut by it in parallel straight lines [11.16], so that the plane through X cuts the plane of the rectangle in a straight line XY through X parallel to EA, Y lying on AC or on AC produced. Since XY is parallel to EA and XE to YA, the figure EXYA is a parallelogram; therefore XY is equal to EA [1.34]. And since XY is parallel to EA and EA is at right angles to EF, the angle YXE is also right [1.29]. Hence, as the rectangle revolves about the axis, the point Y describes in the plane through X a circle with centre X and radius XY, which is equal to the circle AB, since their radii are equal [D3.1]; and the rectangle is divided by XY into two rectangles which, by the same revolution, generate two cylinders having this circle for a common base and having for axes the two parts into which X divides the axis [D11.21, D11.22, D11.23]. In particular, the plane GH cuts the cylinder AD in the circle GH, with centre K and radius KG equal to EA, which is the common base of the cylinders BG and GD.

**Step 4.** Produce the axis EF in both directions to L and M [P2]; on EL set out any number of straight lines EN, NL, each equal to EK, and on FM any number of straight lines FO, OM, each equal to KF [1.3]; and through the points L, N, O, M let planes be drawn parallel to the plane AB. By Step 3 these planes cut the plane of the rectangle EACF, produced, in the straight lines LP, NR, OT, MV, each parallel and equal to EA and at right angles to LM, the points P, R, T, V lying on AC produced. Therefore LMVP is a rectangle; by its revolution about LM it generates a cylinder PW, whose axis is LM and whose bases are the circles PQ, VW described by P and V about the centres L and M [D11.21, D11.22, D11.23], and of this cylinder the cylinder AD, generated by the part EACF of the same rectangle, is a part. The planes of the circles RS, AB, GH, CD, TU through N, E, K, F, O cut the rectangle LMVP in NR, EA, KG, FC, OT (FC being a side of the rectangle EACF), dividing it into the rectangles LNRP, NEAR, EKGA, KFCG, FOTC, OMVT; hence [Step 3] they divide the cylinder PW into the cylinders QR, RB, BG, GD, DT, TW, whose axes are LN, NE, EK, KF, FO, OM respectively, and whose bases are the circles PQ, RS, AB, GH, CD, TU, VW.

**Step 5.** The radii LP, NR, KG, OT, MV of the circles PQ, RS, GH, TU, VW are each equal to EA [Step 3, 1.34], and the radius FC of the circle CD is equal to EA, being the opposite side of the rectangle EACF [1.34]; therefore all these radii are equal to one another [CN1], and the circles PQ, RS, AB, GH, CD, TU, VW are all equal to one another [D3.1].

**Step 6.** I say further that any two cylinders which have equal axes and equal bases are equal to one another. For let two cylinders have equal axes and equal bases. Since their axes are equal, they are of the same height [Step 2]; therefore, as the first cylinder is to the second, so is the base of the first to the base of the second [12.11]. Now, of four magnitudes in the same ratio, if any equimultiples be taken of the first and third and any equimultiples of the second and fourth, the former alike exceed, are alike equal to, or alike fall short of the latter [D5.5]; taking, then, each of the four magnitudes once, since the base of the first cylinder is equal to the base of the second, the first cylinder is equal to the second cylinder.

**Step 7.** The cylinders QR, RB, BG have the axes LN, NE, EK, which are equal to one another, and their bases are equal circles [Step 5]; therefore they are equal to one another [Step 6]. Hence the cylinder PG, whose axis is LK and which is made up of the cylinders QR, RB, BG, is the same multiple of the cylinder BG that the axis LK, made up of LN, NE, EK, is of the axis EK [D5.2]. For the same reason the cylinders GD, DT, TW are equal to one another, and the cylinder GM, whose axis is KM and which is made up of GD, DT, TW, is the same multiple of the cylinder GD that the axis KM is of the axis KF [D5.2].

**Step 8.** I say that the cylinder PG is greater than, equal to, or less than the cylinder GM according as the axis LK is greater than, equal to, or less than the axis KM. (a) If LK is equal to KM, the cylinders PG and GM have equal axes, and their bases PQ, GH and GH, VW are equal circles [Step 5]; therefore PG is equal to GM [Step 6]. (b) If LK is greater than KM, cut off from KL the straight line KZ equal to KM [1.3], and through Z draw a plane parallel to the plane AB; by Step 3 this plane divides the cylinder PG into two cylinders, one of which, ZG, has the axis ZK and has for bases the circle GH and a circle equal to it. Since the axes ZK, KM are equal and the bases are equal, the cylinder ZG is equal to the cylinder GM [Step 6]; but the cylinder PG is greater than its part ZG [CN5]; therefore PG is greater than GM. (c) If LK is less than KM, then, cutting off from KM a straight line equal to KL, we show in the same way that GM is greater than PG, that is, PG is less than GM.

**Step 9.** Now there are four magnitudes, the cylinder BG, the cylinder GD, the axis EK and the axis KF; of the first and third, BG and EK, equimultiples have been taken, namely the cylinder PG and the axis LK, and of the second and fourth, GD and KF, equimultiples have been taken, namely the cylinder GM and the axis KM [Step 7]; and it has been proved that, if the multiple LK exceeds the multiple KM, the multiple PG exceeds the multiple GM, if equal, equal, and if less, less [Step 8]. Since the number of the straight lines EN, NL and the number of the straight lines FO, OM were any whatever, these equimultiples are any equimultiples whatever. Therefore, as the cylinder BG is to the cylinder GD, so is the axis EK to the axis KF [D5.5]. Therefore, if a cylinder be cut by a plane which is parallel to its opposite planes, then, as the cylinder is to the cylinder, so is the axis to the axis. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.14

**Statement:** Cones and cylinders which are on equal bases are to one another as their heights.

**Step 10.** Therefore the cylinders EB, FD on the equal bases AB, CD are to one another as their heights GH, KL, and so also are the cones ABG, CDK on the same bases. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 12.18

**Statement:** Spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters.

Let ABC and DEF be two spheres, and let BC and EF be their respective diameters. I say that the sphere ABC has to the sphere DEF the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF.

**Step 1.** Suppose that the sphere ABC has not to the sphere DEF the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF. Then the sphere ABC has that ratio either to some sphere less than DEF or to some sphere greater than DEF. We show that each alternative is impossible.

**Step 2.** (The lesser sphere.) First let the sphere ABC have to a sphere GHK, less than the sphere DEF, the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF. Let the sphere GHK be so placed that it is about the same centre as the sphere DEF.

**Step 3.** Since DEF and GHK are two spheres about the same centre and DEF is the greater, let there be inscribed in the greater sphere DEF a polyhedral solid which does not touch the lesser sphere GHK at its surface [12.17]. Because the solid does not touch the lesser sphere, the lesser sphere GHK lies wholly within the polyhedral solid.

**Step 4.** Let there also be inscribed in the sphere ABC a polyhedral solid similar to the polyhedral solid in the sphere DEF, by carrying out in ABC the same construction with the same number of divisions. Then, by the Porism to 12.17, the polyhedral solid in the sphere ABC has to the polyhedral solid in the sphere DEF the ratio triplicate of that which the diameter BC has to the diameter EF [12.17].

Ratios which are the same with the same ratio are the same with one another; therefore, as the sphere ABC is to the sphere GHK, so is the polyhedral solid in ABC to the polyhedral solid in DEF [5.11].

**Step 6.** The polyhedral solid inscribed in the sphere ABC is a part of the sphere ABC; therefore the sphere ABC is greater than the polyhedral solid in it [CN5].

**Step 7.** In the proportion of Step 5 the first magnitude, the sphere ABC, is greater than the third, the polyhedral solid in ABC; therefore the second, the sphere GHK, is greater than the fourth, the polyhedral solid in DEF [5.14].

**Step 8.** But the sphere GHK lies wholly within the polyhedral solid in DEF (Step 3), so that it is a part of that solid; therefore the sphere GHK is less than the polyhedral solid in the sphere DEF [CN5]. Thus the sphere GHK is both greater and less than the polyhedral solid in DEF, which is impossible. Therefore the sphere ABC has not to any sphere less than the sphere DEF the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF.

**Step 9.** (The greater sphere.) Next, suppose that the sphere ABC has to a sphere LMN, greater than the sphere DEF, the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF. By the definition of triplicate ratio, this means that there are four magnitudes BC, EF, G, H in continued proportion, BC : EF = EF : G = G : H, such that the sphere ABC is to the sphere LMN as BC is to H [D5.10].

**Step 10.** Inversely, the sphere LMN is to the sphere ABC as H is to BC [5.7 Por.]. Inverting likewise each of the proportions BC : EF = EF : G = G : H, we obtain H : G = G : EF = EF : BC [5.7 Por.]; so H, G, EF, BC are four magnitudes in continued proportion, and H has to BC the ratio triplicate of that which H has to G, that is, of that which EF has to BC [D5.10]. Therefore the sphere LMN has to the sphere ABC the ratio triplicate of that which EF has to BC.

**Step 11.** Let it be contrived that, as the sphere LMN is to the sphere ABC, so is the sphere DEF to some sphere X. In this proportion the first magnitude, the sphere LMN, is greater than the third, the sphere DEF; therefore the second, the sphere ABC, is greater than the fourth, the sphere X [5.14]. Thus X is a sphere less than the sphere ABC.

**Step 12.** Now, as the sphere DEF is to the sphere X, so is the sphere LMN to the sphere ABC, and this is the ratio triplicate of that which EF has to BC (Step 10); therefore the sphere DEF has to the sphere X the ratio triplicate of that which EF has to BC [5.11]. That is, the sphere DEF has to a sphere X, less than the sphere ABC, the ratio triplicate of that which its diameter EF has to the diameter BC of ABC. But this is impossible, by the argument of Steps 2–8 applied to the spheres DEF and ABC in place of ABC and DEF, with X placed about the centre of ABC. Therefore the sphere ABC has not to any sphere greater than the sphere DEF the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF.

**Step 13.** Since, therefore, the sphere ABC has the ratio triplicate of that which BC has to EF neither to a sphere less than DEF nor to a sphere greater than DEF, it has that ratio to the sphere DEF itself. Therefore spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters. Q.E.D.
