## Proposition 5.1
**Statement:** If a magnitude be the same multiple of a magnitude that a part subtracted is of a part subtracted, the remainder will also be the same multiple of the remainder that the whole is of the whole.

**Step 1.** Let the magnitude AB be the same multiple of the magnitude CD that AE, a part subtracted from AB, is of CF, a part subtracted from CD; and let the remainders be EB and FD. I say that the remainder EB is the same multiple of the remainder FD that the whole AB is of the whole CD.

**Step 2.** Since AB is a multiple of CD, AB is measured by CD [D5.2]; therefore AB can be divided into magnitudes AG, GH, ..., KB, each equal to CD, and their number is the number of times that AB is a multiple of CD.

**Step 3.** Likewise, since AE is a multiple of CF, AE is measured by CF [D5.2]; therefore AE can be divided into magnitudes AL, LM, ..., NE, each equal to CF. And since AB is the same multiple of CD that AE is of CF, the magnitudes AL, LM, ..., NE are as many in number as the magnitudes AG, GH, ..., KB.

**Step 4.** Since CF is a part of CD, the whole CD is greater than CF [CN5], and CD is made up of CF together with the remainder FD. Now each of AG, GH, ..., KB is equal to CD; therefore from each of them let a magnitude equal to CF be cut off, namely AP from AG, GQ from GH, ..., KR from KB. Then, since AG is equal to CD and AP is equal to CF, if the equals AP, CF be subtracted from the equals AG, CD, the remainder PG is equal to the remainder FD [CN3]. In the same way each of QH, ..., RB is equal to FD.

**Step 5.** Now each of AP, GQ, ..., KR is equal to CF, and each of AL, LM, ..., NE is equal to CF; therefore AP is equal to AL, GQ to LM, and so on, each to each [CN1]. And these magnitudes are equal in number (Step 3). Therefore, adding equals to equals, the sum of AP, GQ, ..., KR is equal to the sum of AL, LM, ..., NE [CN2]; that is, the sum of AP, GQ, ..., KR is equal to AE.

**Step 6.** But the whole AB is made up of the sum of AP, GQ, ..., KR together with the sum of PG, QH, ..., RB; and the whole AB is also made up of AE together with EB. If from the same AB there be subtracted the equals, namely the sum of AP, GQ, ..., KR on the one hand and AE on the other, the remainders are equal [CN3]; therefore the sum of PG, QH, ..., RB is equal to EB.

**Step 7.** Therefore EB is made up of the magnitudes PG, QH, ..., RB, each of which is equal to FD, and which are as many in number as AG, GH, ..., KB. Hence FD measures EB, so that EB is a multiple of FD [D5.2], and the number of times is the same as the number of times that AB is a multiple of CD.

**Step 8.** Therefore the remainder EB is the same multiple of the remainder FD that the whole AB is of the whole CD. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.2
**Statement:** If a first magnitude be the same multiple of a second that a third is of a fourth, and a fifth also be the same multiple of the second that a sixth is of the fourth, the sum of the first and fifth will also be the same multiple of the second that the sum of the third and sixth is of the fourth.

**Step 1.** Let the first magnitude AB be the same multiple of the second magnitude C that the third magnitude DE is of the fourth magnitude F, and let the fifth magnitude BG be the same multiple of C that the sixth magnitude EH is of F. Let AB and BG be placed together so that the sum of the first and fifth is the whole magnitude AG, and let DE and EH likewise be placed together so that the sum of the third and sixth is the whole magnitude DH. It is required to prove that AG is the same multiple of C that DH is of F.

**Step 2.** Since AB is a multiple of C, AB is measured by C (D5.2); that is, AB is made up of a certain number of magnitudes each equal to C, exactly and with nothing left over. Since DE is the same multiple of F that AB is of C, DE is measured by F (D5.2) and is made up of the same number of magnitudes each equal to F. Let this common number be m: AB is composed of m magnitudes each equal to C, and DE is composed of m magnitudes each equal to F.

**Step 3.** In the same way, since BG is the same multiple of C that EH is of F, BG is measured by C and EH by F (D5.2), the number of measuring magnitudes being the same in each. Let this common number be n: BG is composed of n magnitudes each equal to C, and EH is composed of n magnitudes each equal to F.

**Step 4.** The whole AG is composed of AB together with BG. The m magnitudes equal to C which make up AB, taken together with the n magnitudes equal to C which make up BG, therefore make up the whole AG exactly, with nothing left over. Hence AG is composed of m + n magnitudes each equal to C; that is, C measures AG, and AG is that multiple of C which is m + n times C (D5.2).

**Step 5.** Likewise the whole DH is composed of DE together with EH. The m magnitudes equal to F which make up DE, taken together with the n magnitudes equal to F which make up EH, make up the whole DH exactly, with nothing left over. Hence DH is composed of m + n magnitudes each equal to F; that is, F measures DH, and DH is that multiple of F which is m + n times F (D5.2).

**Step 6.** Therefore as many magnitudes as there are in AG equal to C, so many also are there in DH equal to F, the number being m + n in each case. Consequently, whatever multiple AG is of C, that same multiple is DH of F (D5.2).

**Step 7.** Therefore, if a first magnitude be the same multiple of a second that a third is of a fourth, and a fifth also be the same multiple of the second that a sixth is of the fourth, the sum of the first and fifth is also the same multiple of the second that the sum of the third and sixth is of the fourth. Q.E.D.

Path note: the proof proceeds directly from the definition of a multiple (D5.2) by counting the measuring magnitudes; 5.1, which concerns subtraction of parts, is not required.

## Proposition 5.3
**Statement:** If a first magnitude be the same multiple of a second that a third is of a fourth, and if equimultiples be taken of the first and third, then also ex aequali the magnitudes taken will be equimultiples respectively, the one of the second and the other of the fourth.

**Step 1.** Let a first magnitude A be the same multiple of a second B that a third C is of a fourth D, and let equimultiples EF and GH be taken of A and C respectively. We must show that EF is the same multiple of B that GH is of D.

**Step 2.** Since EF is the same multiple of A that GH is of C, EF is measured by A and GH is measured by C, and as many times the one as the other (D5.2). Divide EF into the magnitudes EK, KF, each equal to A, and GH into the magnitudes GL, LH, each equal to C; then the number of the magnitudes EK, KF in EF is equal to the number of the magnitudes GL, LH in GH.

**Step 3.** Since A is the same multiple of B that C is of D, and EK is equal to A while GL is equal to C, EK is measured by B exactly as many times as GL is measured by D (D5.2); that is, EK is the same multiple of B that GL is of D.

**Step 4.** For the same reason KF is the same multiple of B that LH is of D.

**Step 5.** Thus a first magnitude EK is the same multiple of a second B that a third GL is of a fourth D, and a fifth KF is also the same multiple of B that a sixth LH is of D. Therefore the sum of the first and fifth, EK together with KF, that is EF, is the same multiple of B that the sum of the third and sixth, GL together with LH, that is GH, is of D (5.2).

**Step 6.** If EF and GH contain more than two parts each, the same reasoning applies: each further part of EF is the same multiple of B that the corresponding part of GH is of D (Step 3), and adding it to the sum already formed keeps the sum of the parts of EF the same multiple of B that the sum of the corresponding parts of GH is of D (5.2), until all the parts are exhausted. Therefore EF is the same multiple of B that GH is of D.

**Step 7.** Therefore, if a first magnitude be the same multiple of a second that a third is of a fourth, and if equimultiples be taken of the first and third, then also ex aequali the magnitudes taken are equimultiples respectively, the one of the second and the other of the fourth. Q.E.D.

**Path note:** The only natural route is to split the equimultiples into parts equal to A and C and sum them with 5.2; no alternative preceding theorem serves.

## Proposition 5.4
**Statement:** If a first magnitude have to a second the same ratio as a third to a fourth, any equimultiples whatever of the first and third will also have the same ratio to any equimultiples whatever of the second and fourth respectively, taken in corresponding order.

**Step 1.** Let a first magnitude A have to a second B the same ratio as a third C has to a fourth D; and let equimultiples E, F be taken of A, C respectively (E the same multiple of A that F is of C), and any other equimultiples G, H of B, D respectively (G the same multiple of B that H is of D). It is required to prove that, as E is to G, so is F to H.

**Step 2.** Take any equimultiples whatever K, L of E, F respectively, and any other equimultiples whatever M, N of G, H respectively.

**Step 3.** Since E is the same multiple of A that F is of C, and K, L are equimultiples of E, F, therefore ex aequali K is the same multiple of A that L is of C [5.3]. That is, K, L are equimultiples of A, C.

**Step 4.** For the same reason, since G is the same multiple of B that H is of D, and M, N are equimultiples of G, H, therefore M is the same multiple of B that N is of D [5.3]. That is, M, N are equimultiples of B, D.

**Step 5.** Now, since A is to B as C is to D, and K, L have been taken as equimultiples of A, C, while M, N have been taken as other equimultiples of B, D, therefore, by the definition of magnitudes in the same ratio, if K is in excess of M, L is also in excess of N; if K is equal to M, L is equal to N; and if K is less than M, L is less than N [D5.5].

**Step 6.** But K, L are any equimultiples whatever of E, F, and M, N are any other equimultiples whatever of G, H. Hence whatever equimultiples be taken of E and F, and whatever equimultiples of G and H, the former alike exceed, are alike equal to, or alike fall short of the latter, taken in corresponding order.

**Step 7.** Therefore, by the definition of same ratio, as E is to G, so is F to H [D5.5]. That is, the equimultiples E, F of the first and third have the same ratio to the equimultiples G, H of the second and fourth respectively, taken in corresponding order. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.5
**Statement:** If a magnitude be the same multiple of a magnitude that a part subtracted is of a part subtracted, the remainder will also be the same multiple of the remainder that the whole is of the whole.

**Step 1.** Let the magnitude AB be the same multiple of the magnitude CD that the part AE, subtracted from AB, is of the part CF, subtracted from CD; let EB be the remainder of AB and FD the remainder of CD. Thus AB is made up of a certain number of magnitudes each equal to CD, and AE is made up of that same number of magnitudes each equal to CF [D5.2]. I say that the remainder EB is the same multiple of the remainder FD that the whole AB is of the whole CD.

**Step 2.** Let a magnitude GH be taken which is the same multiple of FD that AE is of CF, namely by adding FD to itself as many times as CF has been added to itself to make up AE [D5.2].

**Step 3.** Then the two magnitudes AE, GH are equimultiples, respectively, of the two magnitudes CF, FD. Therefore, whatever multiple AE is of CF, the sum of AE and GH is that same multiple of the sum of CF and FD [5.1]. But the sum of CF and FD is the whole CD. Therefore the sum of AE and GH is the same multiple of CD that AE is of CF.

**Step 4.** But, by hypothesis, AB is also the same multiple of CD that AE is of CF. Hence AB and the sum of AE and GH are each made up of the same number of magnitudes, every one of which is equal to CD [D5.2]. The magnitudes which make up the one are therefore severally equal to the magnitudes which make up the other [CN1], and, equals being added to equals, the wholes are equal [CN2]: that is, AB is equal to the sum of AE and GH.

**Step 5.** Now AB is also the sum of AE and EB, since EB is the remainder when AE is subtracted from AB. Therefore the sum of AE and EB is equal to the sum of AE and GH. Let the common magnitude AE be subtracted from each; then the remainder EB is equal to the remainder GH [CN3].

**Step 6.** But GH is the same multiple of FD that AE is of CF (Step 2), and EB is equal to GH; therefore EB is measured by FD the same number of times that GH is, that is, EB is the same multiple of FD that AE is of CF [D5.2].

**Step 7.** And AE is the same multiple of CF that AB is of CD (hypothesis). Therefore the remainder EB is the same multiple of the remainder FD that the whole AB is of the whole CD. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.6
**Statement:** If two magnitudes be equimultiples of two magnitudes, and any magnitudes subtracted from them be equimultiples of the same, the remainders also are either equal to the same or equimultiples of them.

**Step 1.** Let AB, CD be equimultiples of E, F respectively, so that AB is the same multiple of E that CD is of F; and let AG, CH, subtracted from AB, CD respectively, be equimultiples of E, F, so that AG is the same multiple of E that CH is of F. I say that the remainders GB, HD are either equal to E, F respectively or are equimultiples of them.

**Step 2.** Since AB is a multiple of E, it is measured by E, that is, it is made up of a certain number of magnitudes each equal to E [D5.2]; and AG, being a multiple of E, is likewise made up of magnitudes each equal to E. Since AG is a part of AB, AG is less than AB [CN5], so AG is made up of fewer of these magnitudes than AB is; hence, when AG is taken away from AB, the remainder GB is made up of the magnitudes equal to E that remain [CN3]. Therefore GB is either equal to E (if only one remains) or a multiple of E (if more than one remain). We treat these two cases in turn.

**Step 3.** (Case 1) Let GB be equal to E. I say that HD is also equal to F. Take CK equal to F, placed on the side of C away from H, so that KC and CH together make up KH.

**Step 4.** Since AG is the same multiple of E that CH is of F, and GB is equal to E while CK is equal to F, the sum AB of AG and GB is the same multiple of E that the sum KH of CH and CK is of F [5.2].

**Step 5.** But, by hypothesis, AB is the same multiple of E that CD is of F. Therefore KH and CD are each the same multiple of F; each is equal to F taken the same number of times; hence KH is equal to CD [CN1].

**Step 6.** From the equals KH, CD subtract the common magnitude CH; the remainders KC, HD are equal [CN3].

**Step 7.** But KC is equal to F (Step 3). Therefore HD is also equal to F [CN1]. Thus, when GB is equal to E, HD is equal to F.

**Step 8.** (Case 2) Let GB be a multiple of E. I say that HD is the same multiple of F that GB is of E. Take CK, on the side of C away from H, the same multiple of F that GB is of E, so that KC and CH together make up KH.

**Step 9.** Since AG is the same multiple of E that CH is of F, and GB is the same multiple of E that CK is of F, the sum AB of AG and GB is the same multiple of E that the sum KH of CH and CK is of F [5.2].

**Step 10.** But AB is the same multiple of E that CD is of F. Therefore KH and CD are each the same multiple of F; each is equal to F taken the same number of times; hence KH is equal to CD [CN1].

**Step 11.** From the equals KH, CD subtract the common magnitude CH; the remainders KC, HD are equal [CN3].

**Step 12.** But KC is the same multiple of F that GB is of E (Step 8); and HD, being equal to KC, is measured by F the same number of times that KC is, so HD is the same multiple of F that GB is of E [D5.2].

**Step 13.** Therefore, in either case, the remainders GB, HD are either equal to E, F respectively (Case 1) or equimultiples of E, F (Case 2). Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.7
**Statement:** Equal magnitudes have to the same the same ratio, as also has the same to equal magnitudes.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be equal magnitudes, and let C be any other magnitude of the same kind. I say that A has to C the same ratio as B has to C, and also that C has to A the same ratio as C has to B.

**Step 2.** Let D, E be any equimultiples whatever of A, B respectively, and let F be any other multiple whatever of C (D5.2).

**Step 3.** Since D is the same multiple of A that E is of B, D is composed of as many magnitudes equal to A as E is composed of magnitudes equal to B (D5.2). But A is equal to B; hence each of the equal parts composing D is equal to the corresponding part composing E, and adding these equals to equals one by one, the wholes are equal: D = E (CN2).

**Step 4.** Since D = E, D and E stand in the same relation of size to F. If D exceeds F, then E, being equal to D, also exceeds F; if D is equal to F, then E is equal to F, since things equal to the same thing are equal to one another (CN1); and if D falls short of F, then E, being equal to D, also falls short of F.

**Step 5.** Now D, E are equimultiples of A, B taken at will, and F is any multiple whatever of C; and it has been shown that D and E alike exceed, are alike equal to, or alike fall short of F. Therefore, by the definition of magnitudes in the same ratio (D5.5), A has to C the same ratio as B has to C.

**Step 6.** Again, with the same equimultiples D, E of A, B and the same multiple F of C: since D = E, if F exceeds D it also exceeds E; if F is equal to D it is also equal to E (CN1); and if F falls short of D it also falls short of E.

**Step 7.** Here F is any multiple whatever of C, while D, E are any equimultiples whatever of A, B; and F alike exceeds, is alike equal to, or alike falls short of D and E. Therefore, by D5.5, C has to A the same ratio as C has to B.

**Step 8.** Therefore equal magnitudes have to the same magnitude the same ratio, and the same magnitude has to equal magnitudes the same ratio. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.8
**Statement:** Of unequal magnitudes, the greater has to the same a greater ratio than the less has; and the same has to the less a greater ratio than it has to the greater.

**Step 1.** Let AB, C be unequal magnitudes, of which AB is the greater, and let D be another magnitude of the same kind, so that AB, C, D have ratios to one another. It is to be proved that AB has to D a greater ratio than C has to D, and that D has to C a greater ratio than D has to AB.

**Step 2.** Since AB is greater than C, let EB, a part of AB, be made equal to C; then the remainder AE is the excess by which AB exceeds C, and the whole AB is made up of AE, EB.

**Step 3.** AE and D are magnitudes having a ratio to one another; therefore AE, being multiplied, will at some time exceed D [D5.4]. Let AE be multiplied until the multiple taken is greater than D, and let FG be this multiple of AE. Let GH be the same multiple of EB, and K the same multiple of C, that FG is of AE.

**Step 4.** Since FG is the same multiple of AE that GH is of EB, the whole FH, made up of FG, GH, is the same multiple of the whole AB, made up of AE, EB, that FG is of AE [5.1]. But K is also that same multiple of C; therefore FH, K are equimultiples of AB, C.

**Step 5.** Since GH, K are the same multiple of EB, C respectively, GH is made up of as many magnitudes equal to EB as K is of magnitudes equal to C; and EB is equal to C. Therefore, equals being added to equals the same number of times, GH is equal to K [CN2].

**Step 6.** D and K are magnitudes having a ratio to one another; therefore D, being multiplied, will at some time exceed K [D5.4]. Let the multiples of D be taken in succession, D itself, then the double of D, then the triple of D, and so on, each being made up of the preceding multiple together with D, until a multiple of D is reached which is the first that is greater than K; let this multiple be N. Then N is greater than K.

**Step 7.** FH is greater than N. For, if N is D itself, then, since FG is greater than D and the whole FH is greater than its part FG [CN5], FH is greater than D, that is, than N. If N is not D itself, let M be the multiple of D immediately preceding N, so that N is made up of M and D together. Since N is the first multiple of D greater than K, M is not greater than K; and GH is equal to K [Step 5]; therefore GH is not less than M. Also FG is greater than D, so that FG exceeds D by some magnitude. Therefore FG, GH together contain D, M together and exceed them by that magnitude, so that the whole FH is greater than N, its part [CN5]. Hence in either case FH is greater than N.

**Step 8.** Now FH, K are equimultiples of AB, C [Step 4], and N is a multiple of D. The multiple FH of AB exceeds the multiple N of D [Step 7], but the multiple K of C does not exceed the same multiple N of D, since N is greater than K [Step 6]. Therefore AB has to D a greater ratio than C has to D [D5.7].

**Step 9.** Again, N, N are equimultiples of D, D, and K, FH are equimultiples of C, AB [Step 4]. The multiple N of D exceeds the multiple K of C [Step 6], but the same multiple N of D does not exceed the multiple FH of AB, since FH is greater than N [Step 7]. Therefore D has to C a greater ratio than D has to AB [D5.7].

**Step 10.** Therefore, of unequal magnitudes, the greater has to the same a greater ratio than the less has, and the same has to the less a greater ratio than it has to the greater. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.9
**Statement:** Magnitudes which have the same ratio to the same are equal to one another; and magnitudes to which the same has the same ratio are equal.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be magnitudes which have the same ratio to a magnitude C, so that A : C = B : C. We show that A = B.

**Step 2.** Suppose, if possible, that A and B are unequal. Then one of them is the greater; let A be greater than B (the argument with B greater than A is identical with the letters interchanged).

**Step 3.** Since A is greater than B, by 5.8 (first part) the greater A has to C a greater ratio than the less B has to C; that is, A : C is greater than B : C.

**Step 4.** By D5.7, "A : C greater than B : C" means that there exist equimultiples mA, mB of A, B and an equimultiple nC of C such that mA exceeds nC while mB does not exceed nC.

**Step 5.** But by hypothesis A : C = B : C, so by D5.5, for every choice of equimultiples mA, mB of A, B and every equimultiple nC of C, the multiples mA and mB must alike exceed, alike equal, or alike fall short of nC. The equimultiples of Step 4 violate this, since mA exceeds nC while mB does not. This is a contradiction.

**Step 6.** Therefore A is not greater than B, and by the same argument B is not greater than A. Hence A = B. This proves the first part.

**Step 7.** Now let a magnitude C have the same ratio to A and to B, so that C : A = C : B. We show that A = B.

**Step 8.** Suppose, if possible, that A and B are unequal, and let A be greater than B (the other case is identical with the letters interchanged).

**Step 9.** Since A is greater than B, by 5.8 (second part) the same C has to the less B a greater ratio than it has to the greater A; that is, C : B is greater than C : A.

**Step 10.** By D5.7, "C : B greater than C : A" means that there exist an equimultiple mC of C and equimultiples nB, nA of B, A such that mC exceeds nB while mC does not exceed nA.

**Step 11.** But by hypothesis C : A = C : B, so by D5.5, for every equimultiple mC of C and every choice of equimultiples nA, nB of A, B, the multiple mC must alike exceed, alike equal, or alike fall short of nA and nB. The equimultiples of Step 10 violate this, since mC exceeds nB but does not exceed nA. This is a contradiction.

**Step 12.** Therefore A is not greater than B, and by the same argument B is not greater than A. Hence A = B. This proves the second part.

**Step 13.** Therefore magnitudes which have the same ratio to the same are equal to one another, and magnitudes to which the same has the same ratio are equal. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.10
**Statement:** Of magnitudes which have a ratio to the same, that which has a greater ratio is greater; and that to which the same has a greater ratio is less.

**Statement:** Of magnitudes which have a ratio to the same, that which has a greater ratio is greater; and that to which the same has a greater ratio is less.

Let A and B be two magnitudes each having a ratio to the same magnitude C.

**Part I.** Let A have to C a greater ratio than B has to C. I say that A is greater than B.

**Step 1.** Since A has to C a greater ratio than B has to C, by D5.7 there exist equimultiples mA, mB of A and B, and a multiple nC of C, such that mA exceeds nC while mB does not exceed nC.

**Step 2.** A is not equal to B. For suppose A = B. Then by 5.7 (equal magnitudes have to the same the same ratio), A has to C the same ratio as B has to C. But then, by D5.5, since mA and mB are equimultiples of A and B and nC is a multiple of C, from mA > nC it would follow that mB > nC. This contradicts Step 1, in which mB does not exceed nC. Hence A is not equal to B.

**Step 3.** A is not less than B. For suppose A < B. Then by 5.8 (of unequal magnitudes, the greater has to the same a greater ratio than the less has), B has to C a greater ratio than A has to C. But by hypothesis A has to C a greater ratio than B has to C; and by D5.7 the ratio A:C is then the greater of the two ratios A:C and B:C, so it cannot at the same time be the less of them. Hence A is not less than B.

**Step 4.** Since A is neither equal to B nor less than B, A is greater than B. This proves the first part.

**Part II.** Now let C have to B a greater ratio than C has to A. I say that B is less than A.

**Step 5.** Since C has to B a greater ratio than C has to A, by D5.7 there exist a multiple mC of C (the same multiple taken as antecedent in both ratios) and equimultiples nB, nA of B and A, such that mC exceeds nB while mC does not exceed nA.

**Step 6.** B is not equal to A. For suppose B = A. Then by 5.7 (the same has to equal magnitudes the same ratio), C has to B the same ratio as C has to A. ## Proposition 5.14
**Statement:** If a first magnitude have to a second the same ratio as a third has to a fourth, and the first be greater than the third, the second will also be greater than the fourth; if equal, equal; and if less, less.

**Step 1.** Let A, B, C, D be four magnitudes such that A has to B the same ratio as C has to D; write A:B = C:D. Observe that by D5.5 this relation is symmetric between the two pairs: the defining condition (that any equimultiples whatever of A and C alike exceed, are alike equal to, or alike fall short of any equimultiples whatever of B and D, taken in corresponding order) is unchanged when the pair A, B and the pair C, D are interchanged. Hence from A:B = C:D we also have C:D = A:B, and this will be used freely below.

**Step 2.** *Case 1: A > C.* Since A is greater than C, and B is a further magnitude, by 5.8 (of unequal magnitudes, the greater has to the same a greater ratio than the less has) A has to B a greater ratio than C has to B: A:B > C:B.

**Step 3.** Now C:D = A:B (Step 1) and A:B > C:B (Step 2). Apply 5.13 with C, D as first and second, A, B as third and fourth, and C, B as fifth and sixth: the first has to the second the same ratio as the third to the fourth, and the third has to the fourth a greater ratio than the fifth to the sixth; therefore the first has to the second a greater ratio than the fifth to the sixth. That is, C:D > C:B.

**Step 4.** Thus the same magnitude C has to D a greater ratio than it has to B. By 5.10 (that to which the same has a greater ratio is less), D is less than B. Therefore B > D, which is the first assertion.

**Step 5.** *Case 2: A = C.* Since A is equal to C, by 5.7 (equal magnitudes have to the same the same ratio) A has to B the same ratio as C has to B: A:B = C:B.

**Step 6.** Now C:D is the same with the ratio A:B (Step 1), and C:B is also the same with the ratio A:B (Step 5). By 5.11 (ratios which are the same with the same ratio are also the same with one another), C:D = C:B.

**Step 7.** Thus the same magnitude C has to D the same ratio that it has to B. By 5.9 (magnitudes to which the same has the same ratio are equal), D = B. Therefore B = D, which is the second assertion.

**Step 8.** *Case 3: A < C.* Then C is greater than A, and C:D = A:B (Step 1). **Step 9.** Hence, if a first magnitude has to a second the same ratio as a third has to a fourth, then when the first is greater than the third the second is greater than the fourth; when equal, equal; and when less, less. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.15
**Statement:** Parts have the same ratio as the same multiples of them taken in corresponding order.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be two magnitudes, and let C be the same multiple of A that D is of B; that is, A is a part of C and B is the same part of D, so that A measures C and B measures D the same number of times (D5.1, D5.2). We must show that A : B = C : D.

**Step 2.** Since C is a multiple of A, A measures C (D5.2), so C is made up of a certain number of magnitudes each equal to A. Let these be C1, C2, ..., Cn, so that C = C1 + C2 + ... + Cn with each Ci = A. Likewise D is made up of magnitudes each equal to B; and because C is the same multiple of A that D is of B, the number of these is also n: D = D1 + D2 + ... + Dn with each Di = B.

**Step 3.** Take any one pair Ci, Di. Since Ci = A, the equal magnitudes Ci and A have to the same magnitude B the same ratio, so Ci : B = A : B (5.7). Since Di = B, the same magnitude Ci has to the equal magnitudes Di and B the same ratio, so Ci : Di = Ci : B (5.7). Ratios which are the same with the same ratio are the same with one another; therefore Ci : Di = A : B (5.11).

**Step 4.** Step 3 holds for each i = 1, ..., n. Hence C1 : D1 = A : B, C2 : D2 = A : B, ..., Cn : Dn = A : B, and so, by 5.11 again, C1 : D1 = C2 : D2 = ... = Cn : Dn. Thus the magnitudes C1, D1, C2, D2, ..., Cn, Dn are proportional (D5.6), with antecedents C1, ..., Cn and consequents D1, ..., Dn.

**Step 5.** Since any number of magnitudes are proportional, as one of the antecedents is to one of the consequents, so are all the antecedents to all the consequents (5.12). Therefore C1 : D1 = (C1 + C2 + ... + Cn) : (D1 + D2 + ... + Dn).

**Step 6.** But C1 + C2 + ... + Cn = C and D1 + D2 + ... + Dn = D (Step 2). Therefore C1 : D1 = C : D.

**Step 7.** From Step 3, C1 : D1 = A : B; from Step 6, C1 : D1 = C : D. Since ratios which are the same with the same ratio are also the same with one another (5.11), A : B = C : D.

Therefore parts have the same ratio as the same multiples of them taken in corresponding order. Q.E.D.

**Step 2.** Since the four magnitudes AB, CD, AE, CF are proportional, AB : CD = AE : CF, they are also proportional alternately [5.16]; therefore AB : AE = CD : CF.

**Step 3.** Now AB is made up of the two magnitudes EB and AE, and CD is made up of FD and CF. Hence the proportion AB : AE = CD : CF says that EB together with AE is to AE as FD together with CF is to CF; that is, the magnitudes EB, AE, FD, CF are proportional componendo. Therefore they are also proportional separando [5.17]: the excess EB, by which AB exceeds AE, is to AE as the excess FD, by which CD exceeds CF, is to CF; that is, EB : AE = FD : CF.

**Step 4.** Since EB : AE = FD : CF, these four magnitudes are also proportional alternately [5.16]; therefore EB : FD = AE : CF. (The remainders EB and FD are of the same kind, being parts of the wholes AB and CD, which have a ratio to one another; so the ratio EB : FD exists.)

**Step 5.** But by hypothesis AE : CF = AB : CD. Thus the ratio EB : FD and the ratio AB : CD are each the same as the ratio AE : CF. Ratios which are the same with the same ratio are also the same with one another [5.11]; therefore EB : FD = AB : CD.

**Step 6.** Therefore the remainder EB is to the remainder FD as the whole AB is to the whole CD. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.20
**Statement:** If there be three magnitudes, and others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two are in the same ratio, and if ex aequali the first be greater than the third, the fourth will also be greater than the sixth; if equal, equal; and, if less, less.

Let A, B, C be three magnitudes, and D, E, F three others equal to them in multitude, such that A : B = D : E and B : C = E : F. I say that if A > C then D > F; if A = C then D = F; and if A < C then D < F.

**Step 1.** Since B : C = E : F, inversely C : B = F : E [5.7, Porism].

**Step 2.** First let A > C. Since A > C and B is another magnitude, the greater has to the same a greater ratio than the less has; therefore A : B > C : B [5.8].

**Step 3.** Since D : E = A : B (hypothesis) and A : B > C : B (Step 2), therefore D : E > C : B [5.13].

**Step 4.** Since D : E > C : B, there are equimultiples mD, mC of D, C and equimultiples nE, nB of E, B such that mD > nE but mC does not exceed nB [D5.7].

**Step 5.** Since C : B = F : E (Step 1), and mC, mF are equimultiples of C, F while nB, nE are equimultiples of B, E, the fact that mC does not exceed nB implies that mF does not exceed nE [D5.5].

**Step 6.** Thus mD > nE while mF does not exceed nE; therefore D : E > F : E [D5.7].

**Step 7.** D and F have ratios to the same magnitude E, and D : E > F : E; therefore D > F [5.10].

**Step 8.** Next let A = C. Equal magnitudes have to the same the same ratio; therefore A : B = C : B [5.7].

**Step 9.** Since D : E = A : B and A : B = C : B, therefore D : E = C : B [5.11]; and since C : B = F : E (Step 1), therefore D : E = F : E [5.11].

**Step 10.** D and F have the same ratio to the same magnitude E; therefore D = F [5.9].

**Step 11.** Lastly let A < C. Then C > A, so C : B > A : B [5.8].

**Step 12.** Since F : E = C : B (Step 1) and C : B > A : B, therefore F : E > A : B [5.13].

**Step 13.** Since F : E > A : B, there are equimultiples mF, mA of F, A and equimultiples nE, nB of E, B such that mF > nE but mA does not exceed nB [D5.7]; since A : B = D : E, mA not exceeding nB implies mD does not exceed nE [D5.5]; thus mF > nE while mD does not exceed nE, so F : E > D : E [D5.7].

**Step 14.** D and F have ratios to the same magnitude E, and F : E > D : E; therefore F > D [5.10], that is, D < F.

Therefore, if A > C then D > F; if equal, equal; and if less, less. Q.E.D.

*Path note.* 5.13 substitutes an equal ratio only on the greater side of a ratio-inequality; substituting F : E for C : B (or D : E for A : B) on the lesser side (Steps 4–6, 13) is carried out directly from D5.5 and D5.7. The route via alternation (5.16) and 5.14 was not taken because A, B, C and D, E, F need not be magnitudes of the same kind. Inversion (Step 1) uses the Porism to 5.7, cited as 5.7.

## Proposition 5.21
**Statement:** If there be three magnitudes, and others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio, and the proportion of them be perturbed, then, if ex aequali the first magnitude is greater than the third, the fourth will also be greater than the sixth; if equal, equal; and if less, less.

**Step 1.** Let A, B, C be three magnitudes, and D, E, F three others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio, the proportion being perturbed (D5.18): as A is to B, so is E to F; and as B is to C, so is D to E. The comparison ex aequali concerns the extreme terms of each set, A with C and D with F (D5.17). We must show that if A > C then D > F; if A = C then D = F; and if A < C then D < F.

**Step 2.** Since as B is to C, so is D to E, therefore inversely (D5.13), as C is to B, so is E to D [5.7, Porism].

**Step 3.** (Case A > C.) Since A is greater than C, and B is another magnitude of the same kind, A has to B a greater ratio than C has to B [5.8].

**Step 4.** Since as E is to F, so is A to B, and A has to B a greater ratio than C has to B, therefore E has to F a greater ratio than C has to B [5.13].

**Step 5.** Since E has to F a greater ratio than C has to B, by D5.7 there are equimultiples mE, mC of E, C and equimultiples nF, nB of F, B such that mE exceeds nF but mC does not exceed nB. Now as C is to B, so is E to D (Step 2); taking the equimultiples mC, mE of C, E and the equimultiples nB, nD of B, D, since mC does not exceed nB, by D5.5 mE does not exceed nD. Thus mE exceeds nF while mE does not exceed nD; therefore, by D5.7, E has to F a greater ratio than E has to D. (This replacement of the lesser ratio C:B by the equal ratio E:D is justified directly from the definitions, since 5.13 as enunciated covers only the replacement of the greater ratio.)

**Step 6.** Since E has to F a greater ratio than E has to D, that to which the same has the greater ratio is less; therefore F is less than D [5.10], that is, D is greater than F.

**Step 7.** (Case A = C.) Since A is equal to C, A has to B the same ratio as C has to B [5.7]. But as A is to B, so is E to F; therefore as E is to F, so is C to B [5.11]. And as C is to B, so is E to D (Step 2); therefore as E is to F, so is E to D [5.11].

**Step 8.** Since E has to F the same ratio as E has to D, magnitudes to which the same has the same ratio are equal; therefore F is equal to D [5.9].

**Step 9.** (Case A < C.) Since C is greater than A, C has to B a greater ratio than A has to B [5.8]. Since as E is to D, so is C to B (Step 2), and C has to B a greater ratio than A has to B, therefore E has to D a greater ratio than A has to B [5.13].

**Step 10.** Since E has to D a greater ratio than A has to B, by D5.7 there are equimultiples mE, mA of E, A and equimultiples nD, nB of D, B such that mE exceeds nD but mA does not exceed nB. Now as A is to B, so is E to F; taking the equimultiples mA, mE of A, E and the equimultiples nB, nF of B, F, since mA does not exceed nB, by D5.5 mE does not exceed nF. Thus mE exceeds nD while mE does not exceed nF; therefore, by D5.7, E has to D a greater ratio than E has to F.

**Step 11.** Since E has to D a greater ratio than E has to F, D is less than F [5.10].

**Step 12.** Therefore, if ex aequali A is greater than C, D is also greater than F; if equal, equal; and if less, less. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.22
**Statement:** If there be any number of magnitudes whatever, and others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio, they will also be in the same ratio ex aequali.

**Step 1.** Let there be any number of magnitudes A, B, C, and others D, E, F equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio, so that as A is to B, so is D to E, and as B is to C, so is E to F. I say that they will also be in the same ratio ex aequali, that is, as A is to C, so is D to F [D5.17].

**Step 2.** Of A and D take equimultiples G and H; of B and E take other, chance, equimultiples K and L; and of C and F take further other, chance, equimultiples M and N.

**Step 3.** Since, as A is to B, so is D to E, and of A and D the equimultiples G and H have been taken, and of B and E other, chance, equimultiples K and L, therefore, as G is to K, so is H to L [5.4].

**Step 4.** For the same reason, since, as B is to C, so is E to F, and of B and E the equimultiples K and L have been taken, and of C and F other, chance, equimultiples M and N, therefore, as K is to M, so is L to N [5.4].

**Step 5.** Thus G, K, M are three magnitudes, and H, L, N are others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio. Therefore, ex aequali, if G is in excess of M, H is also in excess of N; if equal, equal; and if less, less [5.20].

**Step 6.** But G and H are equimultiples of A and D, and M and N are other, chance, equimultiples of C and F. Hence, whatever equimultiples be taken of A and D, and whatever other equimultiples of C and F, the former alike exceed, are alike equal to, or alike fall short of, the latter, taken in corresponding order. Therefore, as A is to C, so is D to F [D5.5].

**Step 7.** If there be more than three magnitudes on each side, let the first set be A, B, C, and so on, and the second D, E, F, and so on, taken two and two together in the same ratio. By what has been proved, as A is to C, so is D to F. Then A, C and the fourth magnitude of the first set, together with D, F and the fourth magnitude of the second set, are three and three magnitudes which taken two and two are in the same ratio; hence, by the same reasoning, as A is to the fourth of the first set, so is D to the fourth of the second. Proceeding in this way through all the magnitudes, as the first is to the last among the first magnitudes, so is the first to the last among the second magnitudes.

**Step 8.** Therefore, if there be any number of magnitudes whatever, and others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio, they will also be in the same ratio ex aequali [D5.17]. Q.E.D.

**Step 3.** Since G, H are equimultiples of A, B, and parts have the same ratio as the same multiples of them taken in corresponding order (5.15), therefore as A is to B, so is G to H. For the same reason, since M, N are equimultiples of E, F, as E is to F, so is M to N (5.15).

**Step 4.** But by hypothesis as A is to B, so is E to F. Therefore, since ratios which are the same with the same ratio are also the same with one another (5.11), as G is to H, so is M to N.

**Step 5.** Again, since as B is to C, so is D to E, therefore alternately (5.16) as B is to D, so is C to E.

**Step 7.** Again, since L, M are equimultiples of C, E, therefore by 5.15 as C is to E, so is L to M. But as C is to E, so is H to K (Step 6); therefore by 5.11, as H is to K, so is L to M.

Therefore by D5.5, as A is to C, so is D to F.

**Step 11.** Therefore, if there be three magnitudes, and others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two together are in the same ratio, and the proportion of them be perturbed, they will also be in the same ratio ex aequali. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.24
**Statement:** If a first magnitude have to a second the same ratio as a third has to a fourth, and also a fifth have to the second the same ratio as a sixth to the fourth, the first and fifth added together will have to the second the same ratio as the third and sixth have to the fourth.

**Step 1.** Let AB be the first magnitude, C the second, DE the third, F the fourth, BG the fifth and EH the sixth, so that AB has to C the same ratio as DE has to F, and BG has to C the same ratio as EH has to F. Let BG be added to AB so that AG is the sum of the first and fifth, and let EH be added to DE so that DH is the sum of the third and sixth. It is required to prove that AG : C = DH : F.

**Step 2.** Since BG : C = EH : F, therefore, taking the ratio inversely (D5.13), C : BG = F : EH; for by Definition D5.5 the equimultiples of C, F alike exceed, equal, or fall short of the equimultiples of BG, EH exactly when the latter alike fall short of, equal, or exceed the former, so that magnitudes which are proportional are also proportional inversely [5.7, Porism].

**Step 3.** We now have three magnitudes AB, C, BG and three others DE, F, EH equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two are in the same ratio: AB : C = DE : F (given) and C : BG = F : EH (Step 2). Therefore ex aequali (D5.17), AB : BG = DE : EH [5.22].

**Step 4.** The proportion AB : BG = DE : EH is a proportion separando, since AB is the excess by which AG exceeds BG, and DE is the excess by which DH exceeds EH (D5.15). Magnitudes that are proportional separando are also proportional componendo (D5.14); therefore AG : BG = DH : EH [5.18].

**Step 5.** Again we have three magnitudes AG, BG, C and three others DH, EH, F equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two are in the same ratio: AG : BG = DH : EH (Step 4) and BG : C = EH : F (given). Therefore ex aequali (D5.17), AG : C = DH : F [5.22].

**Step 6.** Hence the first and fifth added together (AG) have to the second (C) the same ratio as the third and sixth added together (DH) have to the fourth (F). Q.E.D.

## Proposition 5.25
**Statement:** If four magnitudes be proportional, the greatest and the least are greater than the remaining two.

**Step 1.** Let the four magnitudes AB, CD, E, F be proportional, so that AB : CD = E : F, and let AB be the greatest of the four and F the least. We must show that AB together with F is greater than CD together with E.

**Step 2.** Since AB is the greatest, AB > E; and since F is the least, CD > F. From the greater AB cut off AG equal to E, and from the greater CD cut off CH equal to F [1.3]. Then G lies between A and B and H lies between C and D, so that AB is made up of AG and GB, and CD is made up of CH and HD.

**Step 3.** Since E = AG, the equal magnitudes E and AG have the same ratio to F [5.7]; and since F = CH, the same magnitude AG has the same ratio to the equal magnitudes F and CH [5.7]. Hence E : F = AG : CH [5.11]. But AB : CD = E : F by hypothesis; therefore AB : CD = AG : CH [5.11].

**Step 4.** Thus the whole AB is to the whole CD as the part AG subtracted is to the part CH subtracted. Therefore the remainder GB is to the remainder HD as the whole AB is to the whole CD [5.19]; that is, AB : CD = GB : HD.

**Step 5.** Since AB : CD = GB : HD, alternately AB : GB = CD : HD [5.16]. In this proportion the first magnitude AB is greater than the third magnitude CD, because AB is the greatest of the four; therefore the second is greater than the fourth, that is, GB > HD [5.14].

**Step 6.** Since AG = E and F = CH, adding equals to equals, AG together with F is equal to CH together with E [CN2].

**Step 7.** Now AB together with F is made up of GB, AG and F; and CD together with E is made up of HD, CH and E. Since GB > HD, GB exceeds HD by some magnitude K, so that GB is equal to HD together with K. Adding AG together with F to GB, and the equal magnitude CH together with E to HD together with K [Step 6], the sums are equal [CN2]: AB together with F is equal to CD together with E together with K. Hence CD together with E is a part of AB together with F, and the whole is greater than the part [CN5]. Therefore AB and F together are greater than CD and E together.

**Step 8.** Therefore, if four magnitudes be proportional, the greatest and the least are greater than the remaining two. Q.E.D.
