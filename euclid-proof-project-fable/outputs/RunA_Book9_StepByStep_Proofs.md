## Proposition 9.1
**Statement:** If two similar plane numbers by multiplying one another make some number, the product will be square.

**Step 1.** Let A, B be two similar plane numbers, and let A by multiplying B make C. I say that C is square.

**Step 2.** Let A by multiplying itself make D. Then D is a square number, being equal multiplied by equal [D7.18].

**Step 3.** Since A by multiplying itself has made D, and by multiplying B has made C, therefore, as A is to B, so is D to C [7.17].

**Step 4.** Since A, B are similar plane numbers, one mean proportional number falls between A and B [8.18].

**Step 5.** Since as A is to B, so is D to C, and one mean proportional number falls between A and B, therefore one mean proportional number also falls between D and C [8.8].

**Step 6.** Let E be that mean proportional; then D, E, C are three numbers in continued proportion, and the first, D, is square. Therefore the third, C, is also square [8.22].

**Step 7.** Therefore, if two similar plane numbers by multiplying one another make some number, the product is square. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.2
**Statement:** If two numbers by multiplying one another make a square number, they are similar plane numbers.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be two numbers, and let A by multiplying B make the square number C. I say that A and B are similar plane numbers.

**Step 2.** Let A by multiplying itself make D. Then D is a square number, being equal multiplied by equal [D7.18].

**Step 3.** Since the number A by multiplying the two numbers A and B has made D and C respectively, the numbers so produced have the same ratio as the numbers multiplied: as A is to B, so is D to C [7.17].

**Step 4.** Now D and C are square numbers. Each of them is a plane number, having been produced by two numbers multiplying one another [D7.16], and each is contained by two equal sides [D7.18]: D by A and A, and C by some number E and E, since C is square. The sides are therefore proportional, for as A is to A, so is E to E, each being the ratio of a number to itself. Hence D and C are similar plane numbers [D7.21].

**Step 5.** Since D and C are similar plane numbers, between D and C there falls one mean proportional number [8.18].

**Step 6.** But as D is to C, so is A to B (Step 3). Now when between two numbers there falls one number in continued proportion with them, one number also falls in continued proportion between any two numbers which have the same ratio as they [8.8]. Therefore between A and B there also falls one mean proportional number.

**Step 7.** But if one mean proportional number falls between two numbers, the numbers are similar plane numbers [8.20]. Therefore A and B are similar plane numbers.

**Step 8.** Therefore, if two numbers by multiplying one another make a square number, they are similar plane numbers. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.3
**Statement:** If a cube number by multiplying itself make some number, the product will be cube.

**Step 1.** Let A be a cube number, and let A by multiplying itself make the number B. I say that B is cube.

**Step 2.** Since A is cube, it is a number contained by three equal numbers, being equal multiplied by equal and again by equal [D7.19]. Let C be the side of A: then C by multiplying itself makes some number D, and C by multiplying D makes A.

**Step 3.** Since C by multiplying itself has made D, D is C added to itself as many times as there are units in C [D7.15]; therefore C measures D according to the units in C. But the unit also measures C according to the units in C. Hence the unit is the same part of C that C is of D, and therefore, as the unit is to C, so is C to D [D7.20].

**Step 4.** Again, since C by multiplying D has made A, A is D added to itself as many times as there are units in C [D7.15]; therefore D measures A according to the units in C. But the unit measures C according to the units in C. Hence the unit is the same part of C that D is of A, and therefore, as the unit is to C, so is D to A [D7.20].

**Step 5.** By Steps 3 and 4, as the unit is to C, so is C to D, and so is D to A. Therefore the unit, C, D, A are in continued proportion; that is, between the two numbers, the unit and A, there fall two numbers, C and D, in continued proportion with them.

**Step 6.** Since A by multiplying itself has made B, B is A added to itself as many times as there are units in A [D7.15]; therefore A measures B according to the units in A. But the unit measures A according to the units in A. Hence the unit is the same part of A that A is of B, and therefore, as the unit is to A, so is A to B [D7.20].

**Step 7.** Thus A and B are two numbers having the same ratio as the unit and A (Step 6), and between the unit and A there fall two numbers in continued proportion with them (Step 5). Therefore between A and B there also fall two numbers in continued proportion [8.8]. Let them be G and H, so that A, G, H, B are four numbers in continued proportion.

**Step 8.** Now A, G, H, B are four numbers in continued proportion, and the first of them, A, is cube (Step 1). Therefore the fourth, B, is also cube [8.23].

**Step 9.** Therefore, if a cube number by multiplying itself make some number, the product is cube. Q.E.D.

*Path note:* the route through the unit and 8.8 is the most direct and is the one taken. An equivalent but longer route avoids the unit by applying 7.17 to A multiplying C, D and A (obtaining A : G = G : H = H : B from C : D = D : A) before invoking 8.23; it is not used.

## Proposition 9.4
**Statement:** If a cube number by multiplying a cube number make some number, the product will be cube.

**Step 1.** Let A be a cube number, let B be a cube number, and let A by multiplying B make C. I say that C is cube.

**Step 2.** Let A by multiplying itself make D. Since A is a cube number and, by multiplying itself, has made D, the product D is cube. [9.3]

**Step 3.** Since the one number A, by multiplying the two numbers A and B, has made D and C respectively, the numbers so produced have the same ratio as the numbers multiplied; therefore, as A is to B, so is D to C. [7.17]

**Step 4.** Now A and B are both cube numbers; therefore the ratio of A to B is the ratio which a cube number has to a cube number. And D has to C the same ratio that A has to B (Step 3); therefore D and C have to one another the ratio which a cube number has to a cube number.

**Step 5.** But the first of them, D, is cube (Step 2). Therefore the second, C, is also cube. [8.25]

**Step 6.** Therefore, if a cube number by multiplying a cube number make some number, the product will be cube. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.5
**Statement:** If a cube number by multiplying any number make a cube number, the multiplied number will also be cube.

**Step 1.** Let A be a cube number, and let A by multiplying the number B make C, and let C be a cube number. I say that B is also a cube number.

**Step 2.** Let A by multiplying itself make D. Since A is a cube number and by multiplying itself has made D, D is a cube number [9.3].

**Step 3.** Since the number A by multiplying the two numbers A and B has made D and C respectively, the products have the same ratio as the numbers multiplied; therefore, as D is to C, so is A to B [7.17].

**Step 4.** Now D and C are both cube numbers, so each is contained by three equal numbers [D7.19]; hence the sides of D are proportional to the sides of C, and D, C are similar solid numbers [D7.21].

**Step 5.** Between two similar solid numbers there fall two mean proportional numbers; therefore two mean proportional numbers fall between D and C [8.19].

**Step 6.** But as D is to C, so is A to B (Step 3); and when numbers fall in continued proportion between two numbers, just as many fall in continued proportion between numbers having the same ratio as they. Therefore two mean proportional numbers also fall between A and B [8.8].

**Step 7.** Thus A, the two mean proportional numbers, and B are four numbers in continued proportion, and the first of them, A, is cube; therefore the fourth, B, is also cube [8.23].

**Step 8.** Therefore, if a cube number by multiplying any number make a cube number, the multiplied number is also cube. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.7
**Statement:** If a composite number by multiplying any number make some number, the product will be solid.

**Step 1.** Let A be a composite number, and let A by multiplying any number B make C. I say that C is solid.

**Step 2.** Since A is composite, it is measured by some number [D7.13]. Let D be a number measuring A, and let E be the number having as many units as the number of times D measures A.

**Step 3.** Since D measures A according to the units in E, A is produced by adding D to itself as many times as there are units in E; therefore E by multiplying D has made A [D7.15].

**Step 4.** Now A by multiplying B has made C, and A is itself the product of D and E; therefore the product of D and E by multiplying B has made C. Thus the three numbers D, E, B, having multiplied one another, have made C.

**Step 5.** Therefore C is a solid number, and its sides are D, E, B [D7.17]. Hence, if a composite number by multiplying any number make some number, the product is solid. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.8
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, the third from the unit will be square, as will also those which successively leave out one; the fourth will be cube, as will also all those which leave out two; and the seventh will be at once cube and square, as will also those which leave out five.

**Step 1.** Let the unit and the numbers A, B, C, D, E, F (and as many more, G, H, K, L, ..., as we please) be in continued proportion beginning from the unit, so that unit : A = A : B = B : C = C : D = D : E = E : F = ... . Thus A is the second from the unit, B the third, C the fourth, D the fifth, E the sixth, and F the seventh.

**Step 2.** Since unit : A = A : B, by D7.20 the unit is the same part of A that A is of B; that is, the unit measures A the same number of times that A measures B. But the unit measures A according to the units in A. Therefore A measures B according to the units in A.

**Step 3.** Hence B is made by adding A to itself as many times as there are units in A; that is, A by multiplying itself has made B (D7.15). Therefore B is equal multiplied by equal, and so B is square (D7.18). Thus the third from the unit is square.

**Step 4.** Now B, C, D are three numbers in continued proportion, and the first of them, B, is square; therefore the third, D, is also square (8.22). Again D, E, F are three numbers in continued proportion and D is square; therefore F is also square (8.22). Proceeding in the same way with each successive triple (F, G, H), (H, K, L), ..., all those numbers which successively leave out one, namely the fifth, seventh, ninth, ... from the unit, are square.

**Step 5.** Next, since unit : A = B : C (both being ratios of the same continued proportion), by D7.20 the unit measures A the same number of times that B measures C. But the unit measures A according to the units in A; therefore B measures C according to the units in A, so that A by multiplying B has made C (D7.15).

**Step 6.** But A by multiplying itself made B (Step 3), and A by multiplying B has made C (Step 5). Therefore C is A multiplied by A and again by A, that is, C is contained by the three equal numbers A, A, A; hence C is cube (D7.19). Thus the fourth from the unit is cube.

**Step 7.** Now C, D, E, F are four numbers in continued proportion, and the first of them, C, is cube; therefore the fourth of them, F, is also cube (8.23). Again F, G, H, K are four numbers in continued proportion and F is cube; therefore K is cube (8.23). Proceeding in the same way with each successive quadruple, all those numbers which leave out two, namely the seventh, tenth, thirteenth, ... from the unit, are cube.

**Step 8.** The seventh from the unit, F, has been shown to be square (Step 4) and also to be cube (Step 7); therefore F is at once cube and square.

**Step 9.** Those numbers which leave out five after F are the thirteenth, nineteenth, ... from the unit. Each of these is reached from the third by successively leaving out one (its place from the unit is odd), so it is square by Step 4; and each is reached from the fourth by successively leaving out two (its place exceeds the fourth by a multiple of three), so it is cube by Step 7. Therefore each of them is at once cube and square.

**Step 10.** Therefore, if as many numbers as we please beginning from a unit be in continued proportion, the third from the unit is square, as are those which successively leave out one; the fourth is cube, as are all those which leave out two; and the seventh is at once cube and square, as are those which leave out five. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.9
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, and the number after the unit be square, all the rest will also be square.

**Step 1.** Let there be as many numbers as we please, A, B, C, D, E, F, beginning from an unit and in continued proportion, so that the unit is to A as A is to B, as B is to C, as C is to D, as D is to E, as E is to F; and let A, the number after the unit, be square. I say that all the rest, B, C, D, E, F, are also square.

**Step 2.** Since the numbers A, B, C, D, E, F beginning from the unit are in continued proportion, the third from the unit, B, is square, and so also are those which successively leave out one, namely D, the fifth from the unit, and F, the seventh [9.8]. Thus every number occupying an odd place after the unit is square.

**Step 3.** It remains to prove that C, E, and generally every number occupying an even place after the unit, are also square. Since the unit, A, B, C are in continued proportion, A is to B as B is to C; therefore the three numbers A, B, C are in continued proportion, and A, the first of them, is square by hypothesis; therefore C, the third of them, is also square [8.22].

**Step 4.** Again, since B is to C as C is to D, and C is to D as D is to E, the three numbers C, D, E are in continued proportion, and C, the first of them, has been proved square in Step 3; therefore E, the third of them, is also square [8.22].

**Step 5.** Proceeding in the same way, any number of the sequence occupying an even place after the unit is the third term of a continued proportion of three numbers whose first term is the number two places before it, which itself occupies an even place. Since A, the number in the second place, is square, and the property of being square is carried forward two places at a time by 8.22, every number occupying an even place after the unit is square. Together with Step 2, which covers every number occupying an odd place after the unit, this shows that all the numbers B, C, D, E, F following A are square.

**Step 6.** Therefore, if as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, and the number after the unit be square, all the rest will also be square. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.10
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, and the number after the unit be not square, neither will any other be square except the third from the unit and all those which leave out one.

**Step 1.** Let as many numbers as we please, A, B, C, D, E, F, ..., beginning from an unit, be in continued proportion, so that as the unit is to A, so is A to B, B to C, C to D, D to E, E to F, and so on; and let A, the number after the unit, not be square. I say that no number of the series is square except B, the third from the unit, and D, F, ..., the numbers which successively leave out one after it; that is, A, C, E, ... are not square.

**Step 2.** Since the numbers beginning from the unit are in continued proportion, the third from the unit, B, is square, and so are those which successively leave out one, namely D, F, ... [9.8]. It remains to show that A, C, E, ... are not square.

**Step 3.** A is not square, by hypothesis.

**Step 4.** Suppose, if possible, that C is square. B is also square [Step 2]; therefore C has to B the ratio which a square number, C, has to a square number, B.

**Step 5.** Since as A is to B, so is B to C, inversely, as B is to A, so is C to B [D7.20]. Therefore B has to A the ratio which the square number C has to the square number B; that is, B and A have to one another the ratio which a square number has to a square number.

**Step 6.** But the first of them, B, is square [Step 2]; therefore the second, A, is also square [8.24]. This is contrary to the hypothesis [Step 3]. Therefore C is not square.

**Step 7.** Next suppose, if possible, that E is square. D is square [Step 2]; therefore E has to D the ratio which a square number has to a square number. Since as C is to D, so is D to E, inversely, as D is to C, so is E to D [D7.20]; therefore D has to C the ratio which a square number has to a square number, and D, the first, is square; therefore C is also square [8.24], which is contrary to Step 6. Therefore E is not square.

**Step 8.** The same argument applies to every subsequent number of the series which is neither the third from the unit nor one of those which leave out one. For let G be any such number; the number immediately before it, F, is square [Step 2], and the number two places before it, E, is of the same class as G. If G were square, then, since as E is to F, so is F to G, inversely F would be to E as G is to F [D7.20], so that F and E would have to one another the ratio which a square number has to a square number; and F being square, E would also be square [8.24]. Thus if any number of this class were square, the number two places before it would be square, and, repeating the argument, ultimately A would be square, which is contrary to the hypothesis. Therefore none of A, C, E, G, ... is square.

**Step 9.** Therefore, if as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, and the number after the unit be not square, neither will any other be square except the third from the unit and all those which leave out one. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.11
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, the less measures the greater according to some one of the numbers which have place among the proportional numbers.

**Step 1.** Let A be the unit, and let B, C, D, ..., K, L be as many numbers as we please, beginning from the unit A and in continued proportion, so that, as the unit A is to B, so is B to C, so is C to D, and so on, until, as K is to L. Thus the ratio of any term of the series to the term next after it is the same ratio as that of any other term to the term next after it [D7.20]. Let M and N be any two of the numbers B, C, ..., L, M standing earlier in the series than N. I say that M measures N according to some one of the numbers B, C, ..., L.

**Step 2.** First let Q be any one of the numbers C, D, ..., L, that is, any term of the series other than B, and let P be the term standing immediately before Q, so that P is one of B, C, ..., K. Then, as the unit A is to B, so is P to Q (Step 1). Now the unit A measures the number B, since B is a multitude composed of units [D7.2]; and since, as A is to B, so is P to Q, the unit A is the same part of B that P is of Q [D7.20]; that is, the unit A measures B the same number of times that P measures Q.

**Step 3.** Since, then, the unit A measures the number B, and the number P measures the number Q the same number of times, therefore, alternately, the unit A measures P the same number of times that B measures Q [7.15]. But the unit A measures P according to the units in P [D7.2]. Therefore B measures Q according to the units in P, that is, B measures Q according to the number P, the term standing immediately before Q. This holds for every term Q of the series after B.

**Step 4.** Now return to the two numbers M and N. Let Q be that term of the series which stands as many places after B as N stands after M. Since N stands at least one place after M, Q stands at least one place after B, so that Q is not B; and since M stands at least one place after the unit A, N stands fewer places after M than after A, so that Q stands not later than N. Hence Q is one of the numbers C, D, ..., L, and Q has a term P standing immediately before it, P being one of B, C, ..., K.

**Step 5.** Consider the row of numbers B, C, ..., Q (from B up to Q inclusive) and the row of numbers from M up to N inclusive. By the choice of Q (Step 4) these two rows are equal in multitude. Moreover, taken two and two they are in the same ratio: as B is to C, so is M to the term next after M; as C is to D, so is that term to the term next after it; and so on, until, as P is to Q, so is the term before N to N; for each of these is the ratio of a term of the series to the term next after it, and all such ratios are the same (Step 1). Therefore, ex aequali, as B is to Q, so is M to N [7.14]. (When N is the term next after M, the rows are B, C and M, N, and this proportion is simply the given proportion, Q being C and P being B.)

**Step 6.** By Step 3, B measures Q according to P; thus B is a part of Q. Since, as B is to Q, so is M to N (Step 5), M is the same part of N that B is of Q [D7.20]; that is, M measures N the same number of times that B measures Q. Therefore M measures N according to the number P.

**Step 7.** Since M measures N, M is a part of N, the less of the greater [D7.3]; so M is the less and N the greater of the two numbers. And P is one of the numbers B, C, ..., K, which have place among the proportional numbers (Step 4). Therefore the less number M measures the greater number N according to P, some one of the numbers which have place among the proportional numbers. Since M and N were any two of the numbers B, C, ..., L, the same holds for every pair of them. Q.E.D.

*Path note:* the proportion needed for M and N could be obtained in a single stroke as A : P = M : N by applying ex aequali to the rows A, B, ..., P and M, ..., N, which would dispense with 7.15. That route was not taken because 7.14 is enunciated for numbers, whereas the unit is not a number; the alternation required when a unit is one of the terms is supplied separately by 7.15. The proof therefore first settles, by 7.15, that B measures every later term according to the term before it, and then extends this to any pair by ex aequali among numbers only.

## Proposition 9.12
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, by however many prime numbers the last is measured, the next to the unit will also be measured by the same.

**Step 1.** Let as many numbers as we please, A, B, C, D, beginning from the unit be in continued proportion, so that the unit is to A as A is to B, as B is to C, and as C is to D; let D be the last, and let D be measured by any prime number E. I say that E also measures A, the number next to the unit.

**Step 2.** Suppose, if possible, that E does not measure A. Now E is prime, and any prime number is prime to any number which it does not measure [7.29]; therefore E and A are prime to one another.

**Step 3.** Since A is the least of the numbers A, B, C, D and the numbers are in continued proportion beginning from the unit, A measures each greater term according to one of the numbers of the sequence [9.11], and by the Porism to 9.11 that number occupies, reckoned from the term standing immediately before the measured term, the same (first) place that A occupies reckoned from the unit; that is, A measures each later term according to the term immediately preceding it. Hence A multiplied by C makes D, A multiplied by B makes C, and A multiplied by A makes B.

**Step 4.** (Descent step.) Let T be any term of the sequence other than A, let S be the term immediately before T, and suppose E measures T; let E measure T according to F, so that E multiplied by F makes T. By Step 3, A multiplied by S also makes T. Therefore the product of A and S is equal to the product of E and F, and consequently, as A is to E, so is F to S [7.19].

## Proposition 9.35
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please be in continued proportion, and there be subtracted from the second and the last numbers equal to the first, then, as the excess of the second is to the first, so will the excess of the last be to all those before it.

**Step 1.** Let A, BC, D, EF be as many numbers as we please in continued proportion, A being the first and EF the last, so that A : BC = BC : D = D : EF; and from the second, BC, and from the last, EF, let BG and FH, each equal to A, be subtracted. It is to be proved that as GC (the excess of BC) is to A, so is EH (the excess of EF) to A + BC + D (all the numbers before EF).

**Step 2.** Since a number equal to A has been subtracted from BC, A is less than BC, and therefore A is either a part or parts of BC [D7.3, D7.4]. Since A : BC = BC : D, BC is the same part or parts of D that A is of BC [D7.20]; hence BC is less than D [D7.3, D7.4]. In the same way, since BC : D = D : EF, D is less than EF. Thus each of the numbers is less than the one following it.

**Step 3.** From EF cut off FK equal to BC and FL equal to D (possible by Step 2, BC and D each being less than EF). Since FH = A < BC = FK < D = FL < EF, the numbers FH, FK, FL are contained in EF in that order, so that the remainders KH = FK − FH, LK = FL − FK, EL = EF − FL are numbers, and EH is composed of EL, LK, KH.

**Step 4.** FH and BG are each equal to A, so FH = BG [CN1]. Since FK = BC and FH = BG, subtracting equals from equals, the remainder KH is equal to the remainder GC [CN3].

**Step 5.** Since BC : D = D : EF, and FK = BC, FL = D, we have FK : FL = FL : EF, that is, FL : EF = FK : FL; in other words, as the whole FL is to the whole EF, so is FK subtracted from FL to FL subtracted from EF. Therefore the remainder LK is to the remainder EL as FL is to EF [7.11], and hence LK : EL = FK : FL.

**Step 6.** Since LK : EL = FK : FL, alternately LK : FK = EL : FL [7.13].

**Step 7.** Since A : BC = BC : D, and FH = A, FK = BC, FL = D, we have FH : FK = FK : FL, that is, FK : FL = FH : FK; in other words, as the whole FK is to the whole FL, so is FH subtracted from FK to FK subtracted from FL. Therefore the remainder KH is to the remainder LK as FK is to FL [7.11], and hence KH : LK = FH : FK.

**Step 8.** Since KH : LK = FH : FK, alternately KH : FH = LK : FK [7.13].

**Step 9.** From Steps 6 and 8, EL : FL = LK : FK = KH : FH. Therefore, as one of the antecedents is to one of the consequents, so are all the antecedents to all the consequents [7.12]: KH : FH = (EL + LK + KH) : (FL + FK + FH).

**Step 10.** But EL + LK + KH = EH (Step 3), FL + FK + FH = D + BC + A, KH = GC (Step 4), and FH = A. Therefore GC : A = EH : (A + BC + D); that is, as the excess of the second is to the first, so is the excess of the last to all the numbers before it.

**Step 11.** If there are more numbers in the continued proportion, cut off from the last number parts equal to each of the numbers before it in the same way; Steps 5–8 applied to each pair of adjacent ratios show that the successive remainders are to the numbers cut off in one and the same ratio, namely that of the excess of the second to the first, and 7.12 then gives, exactly as in Steps 9–10, that this ratio is that of the excess of the last to the sum of all the numbers before it. Q.E.D.

## Proposition 9.36
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be set out continuously in double proportion, until the sum of all becomes prime, and if the sum multiplied into the last make some number, the product will be perfect.

**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be set out continuously in double proportion, until the sum of all becomes prime, and if the sum multiplied into the last make some number, the product will be perfect.

**Step 1.** Let as many numbers as we please, A, B, C, D, be set out beginning from the unit in double proportion, so that A is double the unit (A is the dyad, two units), B is double A, C double B, and D double C; let the sum of the unit, A, B, C, D be E, and let E be prime; and let E multiplying D make FG. I say that FG is perfect. (A, B, C, D stand for any multitude of such numbers; nothing below depends on there being four of them.)

**Step 2.** Let E multiplying A, B, C make HK, L, M respectively (D7.15), just as E multiplying D makes FG.

**Step 3.** Since the one number E multiplying A, B, C, D has made HK, L, M, FG, therefore HK : L = A : B, L : M = B : C, M : FG = C : D (7.17). But A is half of B, B half of C, C half of D; therefore HK is half of L, L half of M, M half of FG (D7.20). Further, HK, the product of E and A, is also the product of A and E (7.16), that is, E added to itself twice, since A is two units (D7.15); so HK = E + E and E is half of HK. Hence E, HK, L, M, FG are continuously proportional, each being half of the next, and they stand in the same ratios as A, B, C, D: A : B = E : HK, B : C = HK : L, C : D = L : M, every one of these ratios being that of a number to its double (D7.20).

**Step 4.** From the second, HK, and from the last, FG, of the continued proportion E, HK, L, M, FG, subtract numbers each equal to the first, E: let HN = E with remainder NK, and FO = E with remainder OG. Then, as the excess of the second is to the first, so is the excess of the last to all those before it: NK : E = OG : (E + HK + L + M) (9.35). But HK = E + E, so the excess NK is equal to E; hence E : E = OG : (E + HK + L + M), and therefore OG = E + HK + L + M (D7.20).

**Step 5.** FG is made up of FO and OG; FO = E, and E is the sum of the unit, A, B, C, D by hypothesis, while OG = E + HK + L + M. Adding equals to equals (CN2), FG = unit + A + B + C + D + E + HK + L + M.

**Step 6.** Each of these nine measures FG. (a) The unit measures every number, a number being a multitude composed of units (D7.2); so it measures FG. (b) FG, the product of E and D, is D added to itself as many times as there are units in E (D7.15), so D measures FG; and FG is also the product of D and E (7.16), E added to itself as many times as there are units in D, so E measures FG. (c) By Step 3, A : B = E : HK, B : C = HK : L, C : D = L : M; therefore, ex aequali, A : D = E : M (7.14). Likewise B : C = E : HK and C : D = HK : L, so, ex aequali, B : D = E : L (7.14); and C : D = E : HK directly. From these three proportions, A × M = D × E, B × L = D × E, C × HK = D × E (7.19); and D × E = E × D = FG (7.16). Hence (CN1) FG is the product of A and M, of B and L, and of C and HK. Consequently, by D7.15 and 7.16, A measures FG (according to the units in M) and M measures FG (according to the units in A); likewise B and L measure FG, and C and HK measure FG. Thus the unit, A, B, C, D, E, HK, L, M are all parts of FG (D7.3), and by Step 5 FG is equal to their sum.

**Step 7.** It remains to show that FG has no other part. Suppose, if possible, that some number O, other than A, B, C, D, E, HK, L, M, measures FG. Being a part of FG, O is less than FG (D7.3), so O measures FG more than once: as many times as O measures FG, so many units let there be in P; then P is a number, not an unit, and P multiplying O makes FG (D7.15). Thus the product of O and P is FG, which is the product of E and D (CN1).

**Step 8.** E measures FG (Step 6b), that is, E measures the product of O and P; and E is prime; therefore E measures O, or E measures P (7.30). Also, since the product of the first and fourth of the four numbers E, O, P, D is equal to the product of the second and third, the four are proportional: E : O = P : D (7.19).

**Step 9.** Suppose E measures P. Alternately, E : P = O : D (7.13); hence O is to D as E is to P, so that O measures D, being the same part of D that E is of P (or O is equal to D, should E be equal to P) (D7.20). Now the unit, A, B, C, D are continuously proportional beginning from an unit, and A, the number after the unit, is prime: for a number is a multitude of units (D7.2), so no number is less than the dyad, and the dyad is therefore measured by an unit alone (D7.11). Hence D is measured by none except those which have a place among the unit, A, B, C, D (9.13); so O, being a number, would be one of A, B, C, D, contrary to hypothesis. Therefore E does not measure P.

**Step 10.** Therefore E measures O (Step 8). Since O is not E, E is a part of O (D7.3): let E measure O according to the units in T, T being a number, so that O is the product of T and E, that is, of E and T (D7.15, 7.16). Since E : O = P : D (Step 8), P is the same part of D that E is of O (D7.20): P measures D according to the units in T, so that D is the product of T and P, that is, of P and T (D7.15, 7.16); thus T measures D according to the units in P. Since P is a number and not an unit, D, being T added to itself as many times as there are units in P, is greater than T (CN5); so T is a number less than D which measures D, a part of it (D7.3). By 9.13, exactly as in Step 9, T has a place among the unit, A, B, C, D; being a number and less than D, T is one of A, B, C. Therefore O, the product of E and T, is the product of E and A, or of E and B, or of E and C — that is, O is HK, or L, or M (Step 2), contrary to hypothesis.

**Step 11.** Both alternatives of Step 8 are impossible; hence no number other than A, B, C, D, E, HK, L, M measures FG, and the parts of FG are exactly the unit, A, B, C, D, E, HK, L, M (Step 6). By Step 5, FG is equal to the sum of these, its own parts; therefore FG is perfect (D7.22). Q.E.D.
