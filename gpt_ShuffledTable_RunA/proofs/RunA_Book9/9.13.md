## Proposition 9.13
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be in continued proportion, and the number after the unit be prime, the greatest will not be measured by any except those which have a place among the proportional numbers.

**Step 1.** Write the given continued proportion as
\[
a_0=1,\quad a_1=p,\quad a_2,\ldots,a_n,
\]
where \(p\) is prime and \(a_n\) is the greatest term. Since \(1:p=a_i:a_{i+1}\), each term after the unit is obtained by multiplying its predecessor by \(p\):
\[
a_{i+1}=p a_i.
\]
[D7.20, D7.15]

**Step 2.** Suppose that a number \(d\) measures \(a_n\) but is not any term of the progression. The unit measures \(d\), since a number is composed of units. Choose the last term \(a_k\) of the progression which measures \(d\), allowing \(a_0=1\). Because \(d\) measures and is unequal to \(a_n\), we have \(d<a_n\); hence \(k<n\). Write
\[
d=a_k q.
\]
Since \(d\ne a_k\), the quotient \(q\) is a number greater than the unit. [D7.2, D7.3, D7.15]

**Step 3.** There is a prime \(r\) measuring \(q\): take \(r=q\) if \(q\) is prime; otherwise \(q\) is composite and VII.31 supplies such a prime. Since \(q\) measures \(d\), and \(d\) measures \(a_n\), repeated multiplication shows that \(r\) also measures \(a_n\). [D7.11, D7.13, 7.31, D7.15]

**Step 4.** Apply IX.12 directly—the natural preceding result here, because it concerns prime divisors of the last term of precisely this kind of progression. It gives that \(r\) measures the term next to the unit, namely \(p\). Since \(p\) is prime and \(r\) is a number, \(r=p\). Thus \(p\) measures \(q\). [9.12, D7.11]

**Step 5.** Write \(q=pt\), where \(t\) is a number or the unit. Then
\[
d=a_k(pt)=(pa_k)t=a_{k+1}t.
\]
Thus \(a_{k+1}\) measures \(d\). This term exists because \(k<n\), and contradicts the choice of \(a_k\) as the last term measuring \(d\). Therefore every number measuring the greatest term has a place among the proportional numbers, as required. [D7.15, D7.20]
