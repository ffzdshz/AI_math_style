## Proposition 9.15
**Statement:** If three numbers in continued proportion be the least of those which have the same ratio with them, any two whatever added together will be prime to the remaining number.

**Step 1.** Let A, B, C be three numbers in continued proportion, least among those having the same ratios. The direct route is to use the coprimality of the extremes: VIII.3 applies exactly to this hypothesis, so A and C have no common measure except the unit (8.3; D7.12). Moreover, A:B = B:C gives
AC = B²
by VII.19 (7.19).

**Step 2.** Write p | X when p measures X. A measure also measures every multiple, and a common measure of two unequal numbers measures their difference: these facts follow by expressing the numbers as repeated sums of their measure (D7.5; D7.15). If two numbers are not prime to one another, they have a common measure d greater than the unit (D7.12). By VII.32, either d itself is prime or some prime p measures d; hence the two numbers have a common prime measure (7.32). This proposition is used because it applies directly to any common measure. It therefore suffices in each case below to exclude a common prime measure.

**Step 3.** Suppose a prime p measures both A+B and C. Since p measures C, it measures AC = B². By VII.30, applied to B multiplied by itself, p measures B (7.30). Subtracting B from A+B, we find that p also measures A. Thus p measures both A and C, contrary to Step 1 and the definition of numbers prime to one another (D7.12). Therefore A+B is prime to C.

**Step 4.** Suppose a prime p measures both B+C and A. Then p measures AC = B², so VII.30 again implies that p measures B (7.30). Subtracting B from B+C shows that p measures C. This again contradicts the coprimality of A and C (D7.12). Therefore B+C is prime to A.

**Step 5.** Suppose a prime p measures both A+C and B. Then p measures B² = AC. By VII.30, p measures A or C (7.30). If it measures A, subtraction from A+C shows that it measures C; if it measures C, subtraction shows that it measures A. Either way, A and C have a common prime measure, contradicting Step 1 (D7.12). Therefore A+C is prime to B.

Consequently, any two of the three numbers added together are prime to the remaining number, as required.
