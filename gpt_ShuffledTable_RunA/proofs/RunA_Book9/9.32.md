## Proposition 9.32
**Statement:** Each of the numbers which are continually doubled beginning from a dyad is even-times even only.

**Step 1.** Let A₀ be the dyad, and let A₁, A₂, … be the numbers obtained by successively doubling it, so that Aᵢ consists of two copies of Aᵢ₋₁. The dyad is even, and every Aᵢ is even because it is divided into two equal parts. Fix any n ≥ 1. As throughout Book VII, a number is a multitude of units; the unit itself is not a numerical factor. [D7.1, D7.2, D7.6]

**Step 2.** Aₙ is measured by Aₙ₋₁ according to the dyad. Both Aₙ₋₁ and the dyad are even. Therefore Aₙ is even-times even. [D7.15, D7.8]

**Step 3.** We use descent through the successive halves, since IX.30 applies directly to a proposed odd measure. Suppose an odd number d measures Aₙ. By IX.30 it also measures Aₙ₋₁. Repeating the same argument, since each intervening number is even, d must measure A₀, the dyad. But an odd number, being a multitude of units and not even, contains at least three units. No positive whole number of copies of it can make the dyad. This is a contradiction. Thus no odd number measures Aₙ. [9.30, D7.2, D7.6, D7.7, D7.15]

**Step 4.** In any multiplication of two numbers producing Aₙ, each factor measures Aₙ: this follows from the definition of multiplication and the equality of the products when the factors are interchanged. Neither factor can therefore be odd, by Step 3; hence both are even. Together with Step 2, this shows that Aₙ is even-times even only. Since Aₙ was any number obtained by continually doubling beginning from the dyad, the proposition follows. [D7.15, 7.16, D7.6, D7.7, D7.8]
