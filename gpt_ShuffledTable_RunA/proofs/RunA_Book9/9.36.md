## Proposition 9.36
**Statement:** If as many numbers as we please beginning from an unit be set out continuously in double proportion, until the sum of all becomes prime, and if the sum multiplied into the last make some number, the product will be perfect.

**Step 1.** Write the given doubling progression as
\[
1,2,2^2,\ldots,2^k=a,
\]
and let
\[
p=1+2+2^2+\cdots+2^k,\qquad N=pa.
\]
By hypothesis, \(p\) is prime. Since the unit alone is not a prime number, \(k\geq1\), so \(a\geq2\). Powers here merely abbreviate repeated doubling, and products denote repeated addition. [D7.1, D7.2, D7.11, D7.15]

**Step 2.** For the sum, use the immediately preceding geometric-progression theorem, 9.35. Apply it to
\[
2,4,\ldots,2a,4a.
\]
The excess of the second term over the first equals the first term. Hence the excess of the last term over the first equals the sum of all preceding terms:
\[
4a-2=2+4+\cdots+2a=2p.
\]
Consequently,
\[
p=2a-1.
\]
[9.35, D7.15, CN3]

**Step 3.** The number \(2\) is prime: no number strictly between the unit and \(2\) can measure it. Thus 9.13 applies directly to the original unit-starting progression. Every divisor of \(a\), including the unit and \(a\) itself, therefore belongs to
\[
1,2,2^2,\ldots,2^k.
\]
This is the natural divisor theorem to use because its hypotheses are exactly those of our doubling progression; no general factorization theorem is needed. [D7.2, D7.11, 9.13]

**Step 4.** Let \(d>1\) be any proper divisor of \(N\), and write \(N=dq\), where \(q>1\). Since the prime \(p\) measures \(dq\), 7.30 says that \(p\) measures \(d\) or \(q\).

If \(d=pe\), then \(peq=pa\), so cancellation gives \(eq=a\). Thus \(e\) measures \(a\), and Step 3 gives \(e=2^j\). Since \(d<N\), we have \(j<k\), so \(d=p2^j\).

If \(q=pf\), cancellation in \(dpf=pa\) gives \(df=a\). Thus \(d\) measures \(a\), and Step 3 gives \(d=2^i\), with \(0\leq i\leq k\).

Accordingly, every proper divisor, with the unit included separately, belongs to one of the two lists
\[
1,2,\ldots,2^k
\quad\text{or}\quad
p,2p,\ldots,2^{k-1}p.
\]
[7.30, 9.13, D7.15]

**Step 5.** Conversely, every listed term is a proper divisor of \(N\): the respective complementary factors are \(p2^{k-i}\) and \(2^{k-j}\). There are no repetitions within either list. Nor can the lists overlap, since \(p=2a-1>a\), while every term of the first list is at most \(a\) and every term of the second is at least \(p\). Hence these lists give exactly all the parts of \(N\), each once. [D7.3, D7.15, CN5]

**Step 6.** The first list sums to \(p\). The second sums to
\[
p(1+2+\cdots+2^{k-1})=p(p-a).
\]
Therefore the sum of all the parts of \(N\) is
\[
p+p(p-a)=p(1+p-a)=p\bigl(1+(2a-1)-a\bigr)=pa=N.
\]
Thus the product equals its own parts and is therefore perfect, as required. [D7.15, CN2, CN3, D7.22]
