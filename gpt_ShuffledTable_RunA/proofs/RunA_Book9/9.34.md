## Proposition 9.34
**Statement:** If a number neither be one of those which are continually doubled from a dyad, nor have its half odd, it is both even-times even and even-times odd.

**Step 1.** Let A be the given number, and let B be its half, understood in the hypothesis as a number. Since B is not odd, it is even. Thus the even number 2 measures A according to the even number B, so A is even-times even. [D7.6, D7.7, D7.8]

**Step 2.** For the other conclusion, use successive halving directly: the hypothesis excluding continual doubling will exclude termination at the unit. Halve A, and continue halving whenever the result is even. This process terminates, since a number is a finite multitude of units and each halving strictly decreases that multitude. If it terminated at the unit, reversing the halvings would produce A by continual doubling beginning with the dyad, contrary to the hypothesis. It therefore terminates at a number C which cannot be divided into two equal parts; hence C is odd. [D7.1, D7.2, D7.6, D7.7]

**Step 3.** Suppose there were k halvings, and let E be obtained by doubling the unit k times. Reversing the halvings expresses A as C added to itself E times, so E multiplying C produces A. Since at least one doubling occurred, E is divisible into two equal parts and is even. By commutativity of multiplication, C multiplying E also produces A; thus E measures A according to C. [D7.15, D7.6, 7.16]

**Step 4.** Consequently A is measured by the even number E according to the odd number C, and is therefore even-times odd. Together with Step 1, this proves that A is both even-times even and even-times odd. [D7.9, D7.8]
