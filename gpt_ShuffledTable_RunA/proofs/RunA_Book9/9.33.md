## Proposition 9.33
**Statement:** If a number have its half odd, it is even-times odd only.

**Step 1.** Let N have an odd half H. Thus N is twice H. By the definition of multiplication and interchange of the factors, the dyad measures N according to H. Since the dyad is even and H is odd, N is even-times odd. [D7.15, 7.16, D7.6, D7.9]

**Step 2.** To establish “only,” we use the direct even-sum route. Suppose, contrariwise, that N is also even-times even. Then an even number E measures N according to an even number K: N is the sum of K copies of E. [D7.8, D7.15]

**Step 3.** Since K is even, divide those copies into two groups containing equally many copies. Their sums are equal, so each sum is the half H of N. Each group consists entirely of even numbers; hence its sum H is even by 9.21. If a group contains just one copy, its sum is E itself and is already even. This route applies without needing to determine the parity of half of K. [D7.6, 9.21]

**Step 4.** But H is odd, and therefore is not divisible into two equal parts, whereas an even number is so divisible. This is a contradiction. Consequently N is not even-times even; together with Step 1, this proves that N is **even-times odd only**. [D7.7, D7.6, D7.8, D7.9]
