## Proposition 9.18
**Statement:** Given two numbers, to investigate whether it is possible to find a third proportional to them.

**Step 1.** Let the given numbers, in their given order, be A and B. We seek a number C such that A:B = B:C. Choose the direct product criterion of VII.19, since it applies immediately to these four terms: the required proportion holds if and only if the product of A and C equals the product of B with itself. [7.19]

**Step 2.** Form S by multiplying B by itself. Investigate whether S consists of a whole number of copies of A: subtract A successively, counting the copies removed, until either S is exhausted or a positive remainder smaller than A remains. This process terminates because S is a finite multitude of units and each subtraction removes units. Multiplication is precisely repeated addition, so this procedure tests whether S is a multiple of A and determines its multiplier. [D7.2, D7.15]

**Step 3.** Suppose S is exhausted and the count of copies is a number C. Then C multiplying A produces S; therefore A multiplying C also produces S. Since S is also the product of B with itself, VII.19 gives A:B = B:C. Thus C is the required third proportional. [D7.15, 7.16, 7.19]

**Step 4.** Otherwise no third proportional number exists. For if such a number C existed, VII.19 would make the product of A and C equal to S. By commutativity and the definition of multiplication, S would consequently consist of exactly C copies of A, contrary to the test. This also excludes the boundary case S = A: the count is then only an unit, whereas a number is a multitude of units. [7.19, 7.16, D7.15, D7.1, D7.2]

**Step 5.** Hence the investigation is complete: multiply B by itself and test whether the resulting number is measured by A a number of times, rather than merely once. If so, that number of times is the third proportional; if not, no third proportional number is possible. [D7.15, 7.19]
