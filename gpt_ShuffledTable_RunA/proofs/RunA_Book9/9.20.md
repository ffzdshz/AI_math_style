## Proposition 9.20
**Statement:** Prime numbers are more than any assigned multitude of prime numbers.

**Step 1.** Let any assigned finite multitude of distinct prime numbers be \(p_1,\ldots,p_r\). Take a number \(M\) measured by every one of them. Such a number is obtained by starting with \(p_1\) and repeatedly applying VII.34 to the preceding common multiple and the next prime. Each earlier prime continues to measure the new common multiple, since a multiple of a multiple consists of repeated groups of the original measure. We use this common-multiple route; minimality is not needed. [7.34, D7.5]

**Step 2.** Add one unit to \(M\), obtaining \(N=M+1\). Since every assigned prime measures \(M\), each is at most \(M\); consequently \(N\) is greater than every assigned prime. [D7.5, CN5]

**Step 3.** If \(N\) is prime, it is therefore a prime outside the assigned multitude. Otherwise \(N\) is composite, and VII.31 supplies a prime \(q\) which measures \(N\). VII.31 is the natural result to invoke here because this branch already assumes that \(N\) is composite. [D7.11, D7.13, 7.31]

**Step 4.** In the composite case, suppose that \(q\) were one of the assigned primes. It would then measure both \(M\) and \(N\). Subtracting the copies of \(q\) composing \(M\) from those composing \(N\) leaves a positive whole number of copies of \(q\); thus \(q\) would measure \(N-M=1\). This is impossible: \(q\), being a number, is a multitude of units, and no positive whole number of copies of it can equal a single unit. Hence \(q\) is not among the assigned primes. [D7.5, CN3, D7.2]

**Step 5.** Thus, in either case, there is a prime distinct from every prime in the assigned multitude. Adjoining it gives a greater multitude of primes. Since the assigned multitude was arbitrary, **prime numbers are more than any assigned multitude of prime numbers**. [Steps 1–4]
