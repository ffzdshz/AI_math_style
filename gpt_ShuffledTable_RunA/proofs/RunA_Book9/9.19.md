## Proposition 9.19
**Statement:** Given three numbers, to investigate when it is possible to find a fourth proportional to them.

**Step 1.** Let the three given numbers, in their prescribed order, be A, B, C. We seek a number D such that A:B = C:D. Choose the direct product route: VII.19 is naturally applicable because it converts precisely this proportionality into equality of two products. Construct P by multiplying B by C. [D7.15, 7.19]

**Step 2.** Suppose a fourth proportional number D exists. By VII.19, the product of A and D equals P. Thus P consists of as many copies of A as there are units in D. Consequently A measures P; moreover, since D is a number—a multitude of units—P is greater than A. These are necessary conditions. [7.19, D7.15, D7.2, D7.5]

**Step 3.** Test these conditions by successively taking copies of A from P. This process terminates, since P consists of finitely many units. If a nonzero remainder smaller than A remains, A does not measure P, so Step 2 excludes a fourth proportional. If P is less than or equal to A, Step 2 likewise excludes a fourth proportional number. Otherwise, if the copies exhaust P, let D consist of as many units as the number of copies taken. At least two copies have been taken, so D is a number, and the product of A and D is P, by the definition of multiplication. Since P is also the product of B and C, VII.19 gives A:B = C:D. [D7.2, D7.15, 7.19]

**Step 4.** Therefore the investigation is complete: a fourth proportional number exists exactly when A measures BC and BC is greater than A; when it exists, construct it by counting how many times A measures BC. The boundary case BC = A yields only a unit, not a number in Euclid’s sense. [D7.5, D7.2, 7.19]
