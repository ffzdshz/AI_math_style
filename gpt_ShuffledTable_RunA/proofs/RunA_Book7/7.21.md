## Proposition 7.21
**Statement:** Numbers prime to one another are the least of those which have the same ratio with them.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be numbers prime to one another. Thus the unit alone is a common measure of A and B. [D7.12]

**Step 2.** Suppose that A and B are not the least numbers having their ratio, and let C and D be the least pair in that ratio, corresponding respectively to A and B. We use VII.20 because its equal-multiplicity conclusion directly supplies a common measure: C measures A the same number of times that D measures B. This multiplicity cannot be one, since then A = C and B = D, contrary to the supposition. It is therefore a multitude of units, hence a number E. [7.20, D7.2]

**Step 3.** Since A consists of E repetitions of C, and B of E repetitions of D, multiplying C by E produces A, and multiplying D by E produces B. Interchanging the multiplying numbers leaves each product unchanged. Consequently, A also consists of C repetitions of E, and B of D repetitions of E. Thus E measures both A and B. [D7.15, 7.16]

**Step 4.** This contradicts the fact that the unit alone is a common measure of A and B, since E is a number, not the unit. Therefore A and B are the least numbers having their ratio. Hence numbers prime to one another are the least of those which have the same ratio with them. [D7.12]
