## Proposition 7.33
**Statement:** Given as many numbers as we please, to find the least of those which have the same ratio with them.

**Step 1.** Let the given numbers be \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\). We use common-measure reduction: numbers prime to one another are already the least numbers having their ratios, so VII.21 supplies the required direction directly. Here “prime to one another” means having only the unit as a common measure. [D7.12, 7.21]

**Step 2.** Find their greatest common measure \(d\). This is a finite construction for any given multitude: examine the unit and the numbers not exceeding the smallest given number, retain those measuring every \(a_i\), and take the greatest. Every common measure occurs in this finite list; if no number measures them all, take \(d\) to be the unit. [D7.1, D7.2, D7.3]

**Step 3.** For each \(i\), let \(b_i\) count how many times \(d\) measures \(a_i\); thus \(a_i=d b_i\). Multiplication by the same number preserves the ratio of the numbers multiplied. Consequently, for every pair of indices,
\[
 b_i:b_j=a_i:a_j.
\]
If \(d\) is the unit, the lists are identical and this conclusion is immediate. [D7.15, 7.17]

**Step 4.** The reduced terms have no common measure other than the unit. For suppose a number \(e\), greater than the unit, measured every \(b_i\). Writing \(b_i=e c_i\), repeated addition gives
\[
 a_i=d(e c_i)=(de)c_i.
\]
Thus \(de\) would measure every given number. But \(de>d\), contrary to the choice of \(d\) as their greatest common measure. Hence the reduced terms are prime to one another. [D7.15, D7.12]

**Step 5.** By VII.21, \(b_1,\ldots,b_n\) are the least of the numbers having their ratios. Step 3 shows that these are precisely the ratios of the given numbers. Therefore the required least proportional numbers have been found. [7.21]
