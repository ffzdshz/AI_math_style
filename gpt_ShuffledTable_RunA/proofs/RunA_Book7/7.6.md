## Proposition 7.6
**Statement:** If a number be parts of a number, and another be the same parts of another, the sum will also be the same parts of the sum that the one is of the one.

**Step 1.** Let A be parts of B, and let C be the same parts of D. Unpacking “the same parts,” let A consist of m equal parts a and B of n such parts, while C consists of m equal parts b and D of n such parts. Here m<n, and m does not measure n: otherwise repetitions of A would make B, contrary to A being parts, rather than a part, of B. The letters m and n record the numbers of equal summands. [D7.2, D7.3, D7.4]

**Step 2.** Each a is the same part of B that each b is of D, namely one of n equal parts. We use VII.5, the immediately preceding single-part addition result, as the direct route from “part” to “parts.” By that proposition, a+b is also one of n equal parts of B+D. Thus B+D consists of n equal copies of a+b. [7.5, D7.3]

**Step 3.** Pair each of the m equal parts of A with one of the m equal parts of C. Each pair has sum a+b; by repeated addition of equals, A+C therefore consists of m equal copies of a+b. [CN2, D7.2]

**Step 4.** Consequently, A+C comprises m of the n equal parts of B+D, just as A comprises m of the n equal parts of B. Moreover, A+C cannot measure B+D: if repetitions of its m equal parts made the n equal parts of B+D, the same repetitions of the m equal parts of A would make B, contradicting Step 1. Hence A+C is the same parts of B+D that A is of B, as required. [D7.2, D7.3, D7.4]
