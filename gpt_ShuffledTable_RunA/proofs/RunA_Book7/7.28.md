## Proposition 7.28
**Statement:** If two numbers be prime to one another, the sum will also be prime to each of them; and, if the sum of two numbers be prime to any one of them, the original numbers will also be prime to one another.

**Step 1.** Let the two numbers be A and B, and let S=A+B. We take the direct route through common measures, rather than ratios. Every number is a multitude of units, so the unit measures each number. Thus, by the definition of numbers prime to one another, it suffices to exclude any common measure greater than the unit. [D7.2, D7.12]

**Step 2.** Suppose A and B are prime to one another. If S and A had a common measure d greater than the unit, each would consist of a whole number of copies of d. Subtracting the copies making up A from those making up S leaves B, still consisting of whole copies of d. Therefore d would measure both A and B, contrary to their being prime to one another. Hence S and A are prime to one another. [D7.5, CN3, D7.12]

**Step 3.** Likewise, if S and B had a common measure d greater than the unit, subtracting the copies of d making up B from those making up S would leave A measured by d. Again d would be a common measure of A and B, a contradiction. Hence S is also prime to B, proving the first assertion. [D7.5, CN3, D7.12]

**Step 4.** Conversely, suppose S is prime to one of the summands; call that summand A, interchanging the names if necessary. If A and B were not prime to one another, they would have a common measure d greater than the unit. Adding the copies of d making up A to those making up B shows that d also measures S=A+B. Thus d would measure both S and A, contrary to the hypothesis. Consequently A and B are prime to one another. This proves the converse and completes both assertions. [D7.5, CN2, D7.12]
