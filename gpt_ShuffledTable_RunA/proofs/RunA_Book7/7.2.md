## Proposition 7.2
**Statement:** Given two numbers not prime to one another, to find their greatest common measure.

**Step 1.** Let the given numbers be A and B, not prime to one another, and arrange them so that A ≥ B. If B measures A, including the case A = B, take B as the required measure. It measures both, and no common measure can exceed B: a measure unequal to the number it measures is a part of it and therefore less. Hence only the case in which B does not measure A remains. [D7.3, CN5]

**Step 2.** Use continual subtraction, the direct route treated by VII.1. At each stage, if the lesser measures the greater, stop. Otherwise, writing the greater as X and the lesser as Y, subtract Y from X and continue with Y and the positive remainder R = X − Y, arranging them in descending order. This process must terminate: every subtraction strictly decreases the total number of units in the pair, and the original pair contains only finitely many units. Denote the terminal pair by H and G, where G measures H. [7.1, D7.2]

**Step 3.** The terminal lesser quantity G is not an unit. For if an unit were reached, our stopping rule ensures that throughout the preceding continual subtraction no remainder had measured the quantity before it. By VII.1, the original numbers would then be prime to one another, contrary to the hypothesis. Thus G is a number greater than an unit. [7.1, D7.12]

**Step 4.** G measures both original numbers. To see this, retrace the subtractions. At the terminal stage, G measures H and itself. At any preceding stage, X = Y + R. If G measures Y and R, these quantities consist of whole copies of G; adding their copies shows that G also measures X. Thus measurement by G passes backward through every subtraction, until it follows that G measures A and B. [D7.5, CN2]

**Step 5.** Let C be any common measure of A and B. Follow the subtractions forward. Whenever C measures X and Y, subtracting the whole copies of C composing Y from those composing X leaves whole copies of C composing the positive remainder R = X − Y. Hence C measures both members of the next pair. Continuing through all the subtractions, C therefore measures G. [D7.5, CN3]

**Step 6.** Since C measures G, either C = G or C is a part of G and is less than G. Consequently no common measure of A and B is greater than G. By Step 4, G itself is a common measure; it is therefore their greatest common measure. The prescribed continual subtraction finds it, as required. [D7.3, CN5]
