## Proposition 7.8
**Statement:** If a number be the same parts of a number that a number subtracted is of a number subtracted, the remainder will also be the same parts of the remainder that the whole is of the whole.

**Step 1.** Let A be the same parts of B that C is of D, and let C be subtracted from A and D from B. Write R = A − C and S = B − D. We use the direct route of subtracting corresponding equal groups, without introducing ratios. Unpacking “the same parts,” there are the same counts m < n and positive measures u, v such that
A = mu,  B = nu,  C = mv,  D = nv.
Here mu means m equal groups of size u, and similarly for the other expressions; a group may consist of a single unit. Thus A comprises m of the n equal parts of B, and C comprises m of the n equal parts of D. [D7.2, D7.3, D7.4]

**Step 2.** Since C < A, we have v < u. Indeed, if v equalled u, their m-fold sums would be equal; if v exceeded u, the m groups of size v would contain the m groups of size u together with a positive excess. Both alternatives contradict C < A. Let w = u − v, which is therefore positive. [CN2, CN5]

**Step 3.** From each of the m groups of size u composing A, remove one group of size v. The total removed is precisely C. Equal groups have had equal groups subtracted, so each remainder is w, and consequently R consists of m groups of size w. Likewise, removing v from each of the n groups of size u composing B removes precisely D and leaves S consisting of n groups of size w. Hence
R = mw,  S = nw.
These are statements about repeated addition and subtraction, not an appeal to any multiplication or proportion theorem. [D7.2, CN2, CN3]

**Step 4.** The remainder R does not measure S. For if S consisted of k copies of R, it would consist of km groups of size w. Since it also consists of n such groups and w is positive, the counts must agree: n = km; otherwise the larger collection would contain the smaller with a positive excess. But then B, consisting of n groups of size u, would consist of k copies of A, each containing m such groups. Thus A would measure B, contrary to the hypothesis that A is parts of B. [D7.2, D7.3, D7.4, CN2, CN5]

**Step 5.** Therefore R comprises m of the n equal parts of S and is parts, rather than a measuring part, of S. These are exactly the same parts as the m of n equal parts which A comprises of B. Hence the remainder is the same parts of the remainder that the whole is of the whole. [D7.3, D7.4]
