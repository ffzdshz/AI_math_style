## Proposition 7.20
**Statement:** The least numbers of those which have the same ratio with them measure those which have the same ratio the same number of times, the greater the greater and the less the less.

**Step 1.** Let A, B be the least numbers having the same ratio as C, D, with A the greater and B the less, corresponding respectively to C and D. Thus A:B = C:D, and by their leastness A ≤ C and B ≤ D. We must show that C and D contain A and B respectively the same number of times. [D7.20]

**Step 2.** We take the alternation route, since it directly compares each least number with its corresponding number. By alternation, A:C = B:D. If A = C, then A measures C once, and the equality of ratios means that B likewise measures D once; the conclusion follows. Hence suppose A < C. [7.13, D7.20]

**Step 3.** By 7.4, A is either a part or parts of C. Suppose it is parts, rather than a part. Since A:C = B:D, B is the same parts of D. Accordingly, let E and F be the corresponding equal subparts: for whole-number counts m, n with 1 < m < n, we have A = mE, C = nE, B = mF, and D = nF. This expresses precisely that A and B comprise the same number of the corresponding parts of C and D; m cannot be one, since then A would measure C. [7.4, D7.3, D7.20]

**Step 4.** Now E measures C exactly n times, and F measures D exactly n times. Hence E:C = F:D, and, by alternation, E:F = C:D. But E < A and F < B, since each is a proper part of its respective whole. We have therefore found smaller numbers having the ratio C:D, contrary to the leastness of A and B. Thus the supposition that A is parts of C is impossible. [D7.3, D7.20, 7.13, CN5]

**Step 5.** Consequently A is a part of C, so A measures C. Since A:C = B:D, B is the same part of D and therefore measures D exactly as many times as A measures C. Thus the least numbers measure every pair having the same ratio the same number of times, the greater measuring the greater and the less the less. [7.4, D7.3, D7.20]
