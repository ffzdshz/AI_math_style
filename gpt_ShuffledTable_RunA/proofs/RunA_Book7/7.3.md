## Proposition 7.3
**Statement:** Given three numbers not prime to one another, to find their greatest common measure.

**Step 1.** Let A, B, C be the three given numbers. Since they are not prime to one another, they have a common measure H other than the unit. We use repeated subtraction directly on the three numbers, choosing this route because it preserves their common measures throughout. [D7.12]

**Step 2.** Begin with the triple A, B, C. Whenever its entries are not all equal, choose two unequal entries X > Y and replace X by X − Y, leaving the other two entries unchanged.

This operation preserves exactly the common measures of the triple. Indeed, if M measures all three entries, then X and Y consist of whole collections of copies of M; subtracting the copies making up Y from those making up X leaves a whole collection of copies of M. Thus M measures the new triple. Conversely, if M measures the new triple, it measures X − Y and Y, and therefore measures their sum X; hence it measures the preceding triple. In particular, H remains a common measure after every operation, so every entry remains a number at least as large as H. [D7.5, CN3, CN2, CN5]

**Step 3.** Continue this operation whenever two entries are unequal. At each operation the total number of units in the three entries decreases by the positive number Y. The initial total contains only finitely many units, and the entries remain positive. Consequently the operation cannot continue indefinitely. Since it can be performed whenever the entries are not all equal, it must finish with three equal entries, say D, D, D. [D7.2]

**Step 4.** D measures each entry of the final triple. Reversing the operations reconstructs each replaced entry by adding the retained smaller entry to the remainder. Since a sum of whole collections of copies of D is again a whole collection of copies of D, D measures every entry at each preceding stage. Thus D measures A, B, and C. Moreover, H measures D by the forward preservation in Step 2, so D is greater than the unit. [D7.5, CN2, CN5]

**Step 5.** Let M be any common measure of A, B, and C. By the forward preservation in Step 2, M measures every successive triple and therefore measures D. Hence D consists of one or more copies of M: either D = M, or D is greater than M. No common measure of the given numbers can therefore exceed D. Since D itself measures all three, D is their greatest common measure, and the finite construction above finds it. [D7.5, CN3, CN5]
