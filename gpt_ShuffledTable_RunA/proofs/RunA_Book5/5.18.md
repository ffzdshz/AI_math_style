## Proposition 5.18
**Statement:** If magnitudes be proportional separando, they will also be proportional componendo.

**Step 1.** Let A exceed B and C exceed D, and suppose that (A−B):B = (C−D):D. Put X=A−B and Y=C−D. Thus X:B=Y:D, and we must show (X+B):B=(Y+D):D, or A:B=C:D. These are precisely separation and composition of the ratios. [D5.15, D5.14]

**Step 2.** Choose the direct equimultiple route, which does not require magnitudes belonging to the two different ratios to be of the same kind. Let m and n be arbitrary positive whole-number multipliers. Using the earlier subtraction-of-multiples rule, we have mA−mB=mX and mC−mD=mY; hence mA=mX+mB and mC=mY+mD. [D5.5, 5.1]

**Step 3.** If n≤m, then mA>mB≥nB and mC>mD≥nD, since each whole exceeds its proper part. Thus mA and mC both exceed nB and nD respectively. [CN5]

**Step 4.** Suppose instead that n>m. Set E=nB−mB and F=nD−mD. These remainders are respectively B and D themselves, or equimultiples of B and D, with the same multiplier n−m. Since X:B=Y:D, the equimultiple definition therefore says that mX and mY alike exceed, equal, or fall short of E and F respectively. [5.6, D5.5]

**Step 5.** Add mB to both magnitudes in the comparison of mX with E, and add mD to both magnitudes in the comparison of mY with F. Each comparison is preserved: equality is preserved by addition, and a strict inequality is preserved because its positive excess remains. Consequently mA=mX+mB and mC=mY+mD alike exceed, equal, or fall short of nB=E+mB and nD=F+mD respectively. [CN2, CN5]

**Step 6.** In either case, the arbitrary equimultiples mA and mC have the same comparison with nB and nD respectively. Therefore A:B=C:D by the definition of equal ratios. Thus magnitudes proportional separando are also proportional componendo. [D5.5, D5.14]
