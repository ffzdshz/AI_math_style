## Proposition 5.20
**Statement:** If there be three magnitudes, and others equal to them in multitude, which taken two and two are in the same ratio, and if ex aequali the first be greater than the third, the fourth will also be greater than the sixth; if equal, equal; and, if less, less.

**Step 1.** Let the two triples be A, B, C and D, E, F, with A:B = D:E and B:C = E:F. We use ratios with common consequents within each triple, avoiding any need to form ratios between the two triples. First, C:B = F:E: for arbitrary positive integers m,n, the hypothesis B:C = E:F makes the comparisons of nB with mC and nE with mF agree; reversing both comparisons gives agreement between mC and nB and between mF and nE. This is precisely the equimultiple criterion for C:B = F:E. [D5.5]

**Step 2.** Suppose A > C. Then A:B > C:B. [5.8] By the definition of greater ratio, there are positive integers m,n such that mA > nB but mC does not exceed nB. [D5.7] Since A:B = D:E and C:B = F:E, the equimultiple criterion gives mD > nE while mF does not exceed nE. [D5.5] Hence D:E > F:E. [D5.7] Of magnitudes having ratios to the same magnitude E, that which has the greater ratio is greater; therefore D > F. [5.10]

**Step 3.** Suppose A = C. Equal magnitudes have the same ratio to B, so A:B = C:B. [5.7] Combining this with A:B = D:E and C:B = F:E gives D:E = F:E. [5.11] Magnitudes having the same ratio to the same magnitude are equal; therefore D = F. [5.9]

**Step 4.** Suppose A < C. Then C:B > A:B. [5.8] Thus there are positive integers m,n such that mC > nB but mA does not exceed nB. [D5.7] Using C:B = F:E and A:B = D:E gives mF > nE while mD does not exceed nE. [D5.5] Consequently F:E > D:E, and therefore F > D, or D < F. [D5.7; 5.10]

Thus, upon comparing the extremes of the two triples, if the first magnitude is greater than the third, the fourth is greater than the sixth; if equal, equal; and if less, less.
