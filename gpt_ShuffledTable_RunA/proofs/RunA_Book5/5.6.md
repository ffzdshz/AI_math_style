## Proposition 5.6
**Statement:** If two magnitudes be equimultiples of two magnitudes, and any magnitudes subtracted from them be equimultiples of the same, the remainders also are either equal to the same or equimultiples of them.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be equimultiples of C and D respectively, and let X and Y, subtracted from A and B, also be equimultiples of C and D respectively. By the definition of a multiple, A consists of m copies of C and B of m copies of D, while X consists of n copies of C and Y of n copies of D. We use this equal-copy description directly, cancelling equal blocks. [D5.2]

**Step 2.** Since X is a proper part of A, n is less than m: if the counts were equal, X would equal A; if n were greater, X would contain a part equal to A. Either possibility contradicts the whole being greater than its proper part. Thus, after the first n copies are removed from each whole, the same positive number of copies remains in both. [D5.2, CN2, CN5]

**Step 3.** The block formed by the first n copies of C in A equals X, and the block formed by the first n copies of D in B equals Y, by repeated addition of equals. Subtracting these equal blocks from the respective wholes therefore gives remainders equal to A−X and B−Y. Consequently, A−X consists of the remaining copies of C, and B−Y consists of the same number of remaining copies of D. [CN2, CN3]

**Step 4.** If that remaining number is one, A−X equals C and B−Y equals D. If it is greater than one, A−X and B−Y are equimultiples of C and D respectively. Hence the remainders are either equal to the original magnitudes or equimultiples of them, as required. [D5.2]
