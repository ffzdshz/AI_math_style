## Proposition 4.5
**Statement:** About a given triangle to circumscribe a circle.

**Step 1.** Let ABC be the given triangle. Bisect AB at D and AC at E. Through D draw a straight line m perpendicular to AB, and through E draw a straight line n perpendicular to AC, producing these lines as necessary. We use their intersection as the prospective centre. [1.10, 1.11, D1.10, P2]

**Step 2.** The lines m and n meet. For suppose they were parallel. Through A draw a line ℓ parallel to m; then ℓ is also parallel to n. Applying the theorem on angles made by a transversal to AB and then to AC shows that both AB and AC are perpendicular to ℓ at A. [1.31, 1.30, 1.29]

If the rays AB and AC lay on the same side of ℓ and were distinct, one of the right angles they make with a fixed ray of ℓ would be a proper part of the other, contrary to the equality of right angles and the whole being greater than its part. If they lay on opposite sides, their adjacent right angles would make them one straight line. Thus A, B, C would be collinear, contrary to their forming a triangle. Consequently m and n are not parallel and meet at some point O. [P4, CN5, 1.14, D1.23]

**Step 3.** Join OA, OB, and OC. If O is distinct from D, compare triangles ODA and ODB: AD = DB, OD is common, and the included angles ODA and ODB are right and therefore equal. Side–angle–side is the direct choice here, since the equal included angles are already known. Hence OA = OB. If O = D, the same equality follows immediately from AD = DB. [P1, 1.10, D1.10, P4, 1.4]

**Step 4.** Likewise, if O is distinct from E, triangles OEA and OEC have AE = EC, OE common, and equal included right angles. Therefore OA = OC by side–angle–side. If O = E, this equality follows directly from AE = EC. Thus OA, OB, and OC are all equal. [1.10, D1.10, P4, 1.4, CN1]

**Step 5.** Describe the circle with centre O and distance OA. Since OA = OB = OC, its circumference passes through A, B, and C. It is therefore a circle circumscribed about triangle ABC, as required. [P3, D1.15, D4.6]
