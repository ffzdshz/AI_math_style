## Proposition 4.13
**Statement:** In a given pentagon, which is equilateral and equiangular, to inscribe a circle.

**Step 1.** Let ABCDE be the given equilateral and equiangular pentagon, with its vertices in boundary order. We use the direct angle-bisector construction, without first constructing a circumcircle. Bisect the interior angles BCD and CDE. Their inward bisectors meet at F: the two half-angles they make with CD together are less than two right angles, so the fifth postulate applies. Join FB, FE, and FA. [1.9, P5, P1]

**Step 2.** In triangles BCF and DCF, BC equals CD, CF is common, and angle BCF equals angle DCF. Therefore, by side-angle-side, angle CBF equals angle CDF. But angle CDF is half of angle CDE, and angle CDE equals angle CBA. Consequently angle CBF is half of angle CBA; subtracting it leaves angle ABF equal to angle FBC. Thus BF bisects the interior angle at B. [1.4, CN3]

**Step 3.** Similarly, in triangles CDF and EDF, CD equals DE, DF is common, and angle CDF equals angle EDF. Hence angle DEF equals angle DCF. Since angle DCF is half of angle BCD, and angle BCD equals angle DEA, EF bisects the interior angle at E. The construction places F on the inward sides of BC, CD, and DE; the interior bisectors just established at B and E also place it on the inward sides of AB and AE. Thus F lies inside the pentagon. [1.4, CN3]

**Step 4.** In triangles ABF and CBF, AB equals CB, BF is common, and angle ABF equals angle CBF. Therefore angle BAF equals angle BCF. The latter is half of angle BCD, which equals angle EAB. Since F is inside the pentagon, subtracting angle BAF from angle EAB shows that angle FAE equals angle BAF. Thus AF also bisects the interior angle at A. Accordingly, the segments from F to all five vertices bisect their respective interior angles. [1.4, CN3]

**Step 5.** Draw perpendiculars FG, FH, FI, FK, and FL to the straight lines AB, BC, CD, DE, and EA respectively. Their feet lie on the actual sides, not their extensions. Indeed, at both endpoints of each side, the segment to F makes an acute angle with that side, being half an interior angle of the pentagon. A perpendicular foot beyond an endpoint would make this acute angle an exterior angle of a triangle whose opposite interior angle at the foot is right, contrary to the exterior-angle theorem. A foot at an endpoint would likewise make the endpoint angle right rather than acute. [1.12, D1.10, 1.16]

**Step 6.** Compare the right triangles FBG and FBH. Their angles at G and H are equal right angles, their angles at B are equal because BF bisects angle ABC, and FB is their common side opposite the right angles. Hence FG equals FH. Applying the same angle-angle-side proposition to FCH and FCI, to FDI and FDK, and to FEK and FEL gives, respectively, FH = FI, FI = FK, and FK = FL. Therefore
FG = FH = FI = FK = FL. [P4, 1.26, CN1]

**Step 7.** Describe the circle with centre F and radius FG. By the equality just proved, its circumference passes through G, H, I, K, and L. [P3, D1.15]

**Step 8.** Each side of the pentagon is perpendicular, at the corresponding point on the circumference, to the diameter through that point. The perpendicular-to-diameter theorem therefore places that side, apart from its contact point, outside the circle; hence the side touches the circle. Thus the circle touches AB, BC, CD, DE, and EA at G, H, I, K, and L respectively. By the definition of an inscribed circle, it is inscribed in the given pentagon, as required. [3.16, D3.2, D4.5]
