## Proposition 4.14
**Statement:** About a given pentagon, which is equilateral and equiangular, to circumscribe a circle.

**Step 1.** Let ABCDE be the given equilateral and equiangular pentagon, with its vertices named in order. We take the direct angle-bisector route, constructing a point equally distant from all five vertices. Join AC and AD. The resulting three triangles show that the five interior angles together equal six right angles. Thus their common value, denoted by 2α, is less than two right angles. [P1, 1.32]

**Step 2.** Bisect ∠BCD and ∠CDE. The angles which these bisectors make with CD on its interior side are each α, and their sum is less than two right angles. Therefore the bisectors, produced as necessary, meet on that side at O. Join OA, OB, and OE. We have ∠BCO = ∠OCD = ∠CDO = ∠ODE = α. [1.9, P2, P5, P1]

**Step 3.** In triangle CDO, the angles OCD and CDO are equal. Hence their opposite sides are equal: OC = OD. [1.6]

**Step 4.** Compare triangles BCO and DCO. Their sides BC and DC are equal, CO is common, and their included angles BCO and DCO are equal by the bisection at C. SAS is the natural criterion here, since precisely these two sides and their included angle are given equal. Consequently OB = OD and ∠CBO = ∠CDO = α. [1.4]

**Step 5.** Since CO lies inside ∠BCD, O is on the same side of BC as A. As ∠CBO = α while ∠CBA = 2α, BO lies inside ∠CBA, and subtraction gives ∠ABO = ∠CBO. Now AB = CB, BO is common, and these included angles are equal. Therefore triangles ABO and CBO have equal bases: OA = OC. [CN3, 1.4]

**Step 6.** Compare triangles EDO and CDO. We have DE = DC, DO common, and ∠EDO = ∠CDO by the bisection at D. Therefore OE = OC. Together with the preceding equalities, this gives OA = OB = OC = OD = OE. [1.4, CN1]

**Step 7.** Describe the circle with centre O and radius OC. Since all five vertices are equally distant from O, its circumference passes through A, B, C, D, and E. It is therefore circumscribed about the given pentagon, as required. [P3, D1.15, D4.6]
