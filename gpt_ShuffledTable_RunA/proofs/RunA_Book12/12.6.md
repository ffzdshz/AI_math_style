## Proposition 12.6
**Statement:** Pyramids which are of the same height and have polygonal bases are to one another as the bases.

**Step 1.** Let the two pyramids have equal heights, and denote their solid magnitudes by P and Q and their base areas by A and B. Divide each polygonal base by nonintersecting diagonals into triangles, and join these triangles to the corresponding vertex. This partitions the pyramids into triangular pyramids P₁,…,Pₘ and Q₁,…,Qₙ, with respective base areas a₁,…,aₘ and b₁,…,bₙ. Thus P = P₁ + ⋯ + Pₘ, Q = Q₁ + ⋯ + Qₙ, A = a₁ + ⋯ + aₘ, and B = b₁ + ⋯ + bₙ. Every component has the height of its original pyramid, since its vertex and base plane are unchanged. [P1, D11.12]

**Step 2.** Use the triangular-base case directly. For every i and j, the pyramids Qⱼ and Pᵢ have equal heights and triangular bases. Therefore
Qⱼ : Pᵢ = bⱼ : aᵢ.
[12.5]

**Step 3.** Fix i. In these proportions, Pᵢ and aᵢ remain the respective consequents. Repeated application of the addition rule therefore gives
(Q₁ + ⋯ + Qₙ) : Pᵢ = (b₁ + ⋯ + bₙ) : aᵢ,
that is, Q : Pᵢ = B : aᵢ. This is the natural addition rule here because the consequents are fixed; the two bases need not have equally many triangles. [5.24]

**Step 4.** Reverse both ratios to obtain, for every i,
Pᵢ : Q = aᵢ : B.
This follows immediately from the definition of equal ratios: interchanging the two groups of equimultiples reverses their comparisons on both sides alike. [D5.5]

**Step 5.** Now Q and B are fixed consequents. Repeated application of the same addition rule yields
(P₁ + ⋯ + Pₘ) : Q = (a₁ + ⋯ + aₘ) : B.
Hence P : Q = A : B. Therefore pyramids of the same height with polygonal bases are to one another as their bases. [5.24]
