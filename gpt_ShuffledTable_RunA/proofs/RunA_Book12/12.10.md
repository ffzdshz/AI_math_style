## Proposition 12.10
**Statement:** Any cone is a third part of the cylinder which has the same base with it and equal height.

**Step 1.** Let K be the cone, S its vertex, O the centre of its circular base, and C the cylinder on that base whose upper base lies in the plane through S parallel to the lower base. Thus the solids have the same height. Inscribe a square P₀ in the base circle. For any inscribed polygon P obtained by refining this square, let Q(P) be the prism erected on P to the cylinder's height, and T(P) the pyramid with base P and vertex S. These lie inside C and K respectively. We use the same letters for solids and their magnitudes. [D11.18, D11.21, 4.6]

**Step 2.** We take the direct route through triangular prisms, since their equal-thirds division already supplies the required factor three. Join O to the vertices of P, dividing P into triangles. The corresponding triangular prisms divide Q(P), and the pyramids on those triangles with vertex S divide T(P). In the three-equal-pyramid division of each triangular prism, one constituent has its lower triangular face as base and a vertex in the upper base plane. By the equality of pyramids with equal triangular bases and equal heights, that constituent equals the corresponding pyramid with vertex S. Adding over the triangles gives
Q(P) = 3T(P).
[12.7, 12.5, CN2]

**Step 3.** Refine P as follows. For each side AB, bisect AB at E and extend the ray OE to meet the minor arc AB at M. The line OM is perpendicular to AB. The tangent at M is therefore parallel to AB. Construct the rectangle R having AB as one side and its opposite side on this tangent, with the remaining sides perpendicular to AB through A and B. The circular segment Σ between AB and the minor arc AB is a proper part of R, and triangle AMB lies inside Σ. Replace AB by AM and MB, doing this for every side to obtain the next inscribed polygon. [1.10, 3.3, 3.16, 1.28, CN5]

**Step 4.** The line EM divides R into two rectangles, and AM and BM are diagonals of these respective rectangles. Each diagonal bisects its rectangle. Consequently, when these figures are erected to the cylinder's height, the prism on AMB is half the rectangular prism on R: the corresponding diagonal planes bisect the two rectangular solids. Likewise, when they are joined to S, the pyramid on AMB is half the pyramid on R, since the paired triangular bases are equal and their pyramids have the same height. Here the triangular-base theorem suffices directly. [1.34, 11.28, 12.5, CN2]

The cylindrical piece over Σ is a proper part of the prism on R. Hence the prism on AMB, which the refinement adds, is greater than half that cylindrical piece. Similarly, the portion of the cone formed by joining S to Σ is a proper part of the pyramid on R; therefore the pyramid on AMB is greater than half that portion of the cone. [CN5]

**Step 5.** Repeat this refinement, obtaining polygons Pₙ and corresponding solids Qₙ and Tₙ. Set
Dₙ = C − Qₙ and Fₙ = K − Tₙ.
The pieces considered in Step 4 partition these respective remainders. Thus each refinement removes more than half of each remainder:
Dₙ₊₁ < Dₙ/2, and Fₙ₊₁ < Fₙ/2.
The same assertion holds for the successive magnitudes 3Fₙ. By exhaustion, Dₙ can therefore be made less than any prescribed positive solid magnitude, and so can 3Fₙ. Throughout, Step 2 gives Qₙ = 3Tₙ. [CN2, CN3, 10.1]

**Step 6.** Suppose C > 3K, and put δ = C − 3K. Choose a refinement for which Dₙ < δ. Then
Qₙ = C − Dₙ > C − δ = 3K.
But Tₙ is a proper part of K, so Qₙ = 3Tₙ < 3K, a contradiction. Therefore C is not greater than 3K. [10.1, CN3, CN5]

**Step 7.** Suppose instead that C < 3K, and put δ = 3K − C. Choose a refinement for which 3Fₙ < δ. Then
Qₙ = 3Tₙ = 3K − 3Fₙ > 3K − δ = C,
contrary to Qₙ being a proper part of C. Therefore C is not less than 3K either. It follows that C = 3K: any cone is a third part of the cylinder having the same base and equal height. [10.1, CN2, CN3, CN5]
