## Proposition 12.11
**Statement:** Cones and cylinders which are of the same height are to one another as their bases.

**Step 1.** Let K and L be cones of the same height, with circular bases A and B. We first compare the cones by inscribed polygonal pyramids, using the polygonal-base theorem directly; afterward the cone–cylinder relation will give the result for cylinders. Inscribe a square in each base and repeatedly bisect all the intervening arcs, performing the same number of bisections in both circles. Denote the resulting similar polygons by a_j and b_j, and the pyramids joining them to the respective cone vertices by P_j and Q_j, beginning with j = 0. These pyramids lie inside their cones and increase with each refinement. [4.6, 3.30, D11.12, D11.18, CN5]

**Step 2.** Similar polygons inscribed in circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters; the circles themselves have that same ratio. Hence a_j : b_j = A : B. Since P_j and Q_j have equal heights and polygonal bases, it follows that
P_j : Q_j = a_j : b_j = A : B
for every j. The polygonal-base theorem is the natural choice here because every refinement again has polygonal bases. [12.1, 12.2, 5.11, 12.6]

**Step 3.** We show explicitly that the remainders K − P_j and L − Q_j can be made smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. Consider a side UV of one of the inscribed polygons, and let M bisect the minor arc UV. Enclose the circular segment between UV and that arc in the rectangle having UV as one side and the parallel tangent at M as the opposite side. The perpendicular-bisector property of the chord and the tangent construction give this rectangle; its other sides pass through U and V perpendicular to UV. The circular segment is a proper part of the rectangle, while triangle UMV is half the rectangle, having the same base and lying between the same parallels. [3.30, 3.3, 3.16, 1.31, 1.41, CN5]

Join the rectangle and triangle to the vertex of the cone. By the equal-height polygonal-pyramid theorem, the triangular pyramid is half the rectangular pyramid. The portion of the cone joining the vertex to the circular segment is a proper part of the rectangular pyramid. Consequently the triangular pyramid is greater than half that portion of the cone. But this triangular pyramid is precisely what is added over UV when the arc is bisected. Applying this to every side shows that each refinement removes more than half the whole remainder outside the inscribed pyramid. Proposition 10.1 therefore makes each remainder smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. The same is true of any fixed multiple of either remainder, since those multiples likewise decrease by more than half at each refinement. [D11.12, D11.18, 12.6, 1.41, CN5, 10.1]

**Step 4.** Let m and n be arbitrary positive integers. By Step 2 and the definition of equal ratios, the comparison of mP_j with nQ_j is always the same as that of mA with nB. Moreover, for j > 0, Step 2 gives P_j : Q_j = P_0 : Q_0. Subtracting the corresponding parts therefore gives
(P_j − P_0) : (Q_j − Q_0) = A : B.
Thus the corresponding multiples of these increments also have the same comparison as mA and nB. [D5.5, 5.19, 5.11]

**Step 5.** Suppose mA > nB. Then δ = mP_0 − nQ_0 is positive. By Step 4, the increments satisfy m(P_j − P_0) > n(Q_j − Q_0) for j > 0, so
mP_j − nQ_j ≥ δ.
Choose a refinement for which n(L − Q_j) < δ, as Step 3 permits. Then
mK > mP_j ≥ nQ_j + δ > nQ_j + n(L − Q_j) = nL.
Hence mA > nB implies mK > nL. Interchanging the two cones and the two multipliers proves likewise that mA < nB implies mK < nL. [D5.5, 5.19, CN2, CN3, CN5, 10.1]

**Step 6.** Suppose instead that mA = nB. Step 2 gives mP_j = nQ_j for every j. If mK > nL, let ε = mK − nL. Choose a refinement for which m(K − P_j) < ε. It follows that mP_j > nL, whereas mP_j = nQ_j < nL, a contradiction. Interchanging the cones excludes mK < nL as well. Therefore mK = nL. [D5.5, 10.1, CN3, CN5]

**Step 7.** For arbitrary positive integers m and n, the multiples mK and nL therefore exceed, equal, or fall short of one another exactly as mA and nB do. By the definition of equality of ratios,
K : L = A : B.
Thus cones of the same height are to one another as their bases. [D5.5]

**Step 8.** Now let X and Y be cylinders of the same height, with bases A and B, and take the cones K and L on those bases with that height. Each cone is one third of its corresponding cylinder, so X = 3K and Y = 3L. Parts have the same ratio as their corresponding equal multiples; hence
X : Y = K : L = A : B.
Therefore cylinders also, and consequently cones and cylinders respectively, which are of the same height are to one another as their bases. [12.10, 5.15, 5.11]
