## Proposition 12.17
**Statement:** Given two spheres about the same centre, to inscribe in the greater sphere a polyhedral solid which does not touch the lesser sphere at its surface.

**Step 1.** Let O be the common centre, and let R and r be the radii of the greater and lesser spheres, with R > r. We use the planar construction 12.16 directly, with an intermediate radius to allow for polygonal approximation in two directions. Construct a mean proportional t between R and r. Then t² = Rr and r < t < R. [6.13, 6.17]

**Step 2.** Choose a plane E through O, and erect its perpendicular diameter NS. Choose a meridian great circle through NS; its centre is O and its radius is R. [11.12, D11.14, D11.16]

In this circle apply 12.16 to the concentric circles of radii R and t. Obtain an equilateral inscribed polygon P with an even number n of sides, none of whose sides meets the smaller circle. The triangles formed by O and successive sides have three corresponding sides equal; hence their central angles are equal. Thus P is regular. By copying these central angles, place a vertex at N; since n is even, the opposite vertex is S. [12.16, 1.8, 1.23]

**Step 3.** Let a be the perpendicular distance from O to any side of P. These distances are equal because the sides are equal. Each perpendicular meets its side at its midpoint, so its foot belongs to the side itself. Consequently a > t, since the sides avoid the circle of radius t. Also a < R, by the right triangle formed by O, a side’s midpoint, and an endpoint. Put k = a/R. Then

0 < k < 1, and ka = a²/R > t²/R = r.

[3.14, 3.3, 1.47; Step 1]

**Step 4.** Take the half of P running from N to S on one side of NS. In E construct n equally spaced rays from O, using the central angle of P. Rotate this half-polygon about NS into the n meridian half-planes determined by those rays. Every copied vertex lies on the greater sphere, by the defining rotation of a sphere. [1.23, D11.14]

At each nonpolar vertex-level, join successive copied vertices. They form a regular n-gon in a plane perpendicular to NS. These planes are parallel, and the polygons are similarly situated, with their centres on NS. Join corresponding vertices at consecutive levels. [11.14, 6.4]

**Step 5.** Corresponding sides of consecutive level-polygons are parallel: each is parallel to the corresponding side of the equatorial copy of P. They therefore lie in one plane and bound a trapezoidal face. At N and S the corresponding faces are triangles. These finitely many faces form a closed polyhedral solid K. [11.9, 11.7]

Here is an explicit description of its sections. Let H be a point of NS strictly between the poles, and cut K by the plane through H parallel to E. In the original meridian let Y be the point where this plane meets the polygonal chain from N to S, and write ρ = HY. The section Q is a regular n-gon centred at H, similar to P, with circumradius ρ. Indeed, a parallel section of each trapezoidal or triangular face divides its lateral edges in the same ratio; its vertices are therefore the n equally spaced rotated copies of Y. This also shows that the faces close without crossings and that O is inside K. [6.2, 6.4]

**Step 6.** Write h = OH. The point Y lies on a side of P. If F is the perpendicular foot from O to that side, then OF = a, and therefore OY² = a² + FY² ≥ a². Moreover, a chord lies within its circle, so OY ≤ R. Since HY is perpendicular to the axis,

a² ≤ h² + ρ² = OY² ≤ R².

[1.47, 3.2, D11.3]

Every point of Q is within its circumcircle, and hence has distance at most ρ from H. Its squared distance from O is consequently at most h² + ρ² ≤ R². Thus K lies wholly in the greater sphere; all its vertices lie on that sphere by Step 4. [3.2, 1.47]

**Step 7.** Since Q is similar to P, its apothem b satisfies b/ρ = a/R; hence b = kρ. A point X on any side of Q has HX ≥ b, by the perpendicular from H to that side and the right-triangle theorem. Therefore

OX² = h² + HX² ≥ h² + k²ρ² ≥ k²(h² + ρ²) ≥ k²a² > r².

Here the second inequality uses k < 1, the third uses Step 6, and the strict inequality uses ka > r from Step 3. The poles themselves also have distance R > r from O. Thus every point of the polyhedral surface is strictly outside the lesser sphere. [6.4, 1.47]

Furthermore, whenever h ≤ r, the circular section of the lesser sphere has radius s satisfying s² = r² − h². The same inequality gives b² > r² − h² = s². Hence that circle lies strictly inside the centred polygon Q. Thus K contains the lesser sphere, and its surface nowhere meets the lesser sphere. [1.47]

Accordingly K is the required polyhedral solid inscribed in the greater sphere and not touching the lesser sphere at its surface.
