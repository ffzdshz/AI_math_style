## Proposition 12.12
**Statement:** Similar cones and cylinders are to one another in the triplicate ratio of the diameters in their bases.

**Step 1.** Let C and C′ be similar cones, with vertices S and S′, base-centres O and O′, heights h and h′, radii r and r′, and base-diameters d and d′. Their axes are their heights, and similarity gives h:h′ = d:d′. Since diameters are twice radii, also r:r′ = d:d′. We take the direct route through inscribed triangular pyramids, and afterwards transfer the result to cylinders by equal multiplication. [D11.18, D11.19, D11.24, 5.15]

**Step 2.** Inscribe a square in each base-circle. Repeatedly bisect every central angle in both circles, joining the successive points obtained on their circumferences. This produces corresponding inscribed polygons with equal numbers of sides and equal corresponding central angles. Let P_k and P′_k be the pyramids having these polygons as bases and S and S′ as vertices, with k = 0 denoting the initial squares. Each refinement enlarges the inscribed pyramid. [4.6, 1.9, CN5]

**Step 3.** Divide each polygon into triangles by joining its vertices to its centre. Consider corresponding triangular pyramids S–OAB and S′–O′A′B′.

The triangles OAB and O′A′B′ are similar, because their central angles are equal and their corresponding radii proportional. The right triangles SOA and S′O′A′, and likewise SOB and S′O′B′, are similar because h:h′ = r:r′. Consequently all corresponding edges of the two triangular pyramids are proportional in the ratio r:r′. Their remaining faces SAB and S′A′B′ are therefore similar as well. Thus the pyramids are similar solids. [6.6, 6.4, 6.5, D6.1, D11.9]

By the theorem for similar triangular pyramids, every such pair has the triplicate ratio of r:r′, hence of d:d′. Summing the corresponding triangular pyramids preserves their common ratio. Accordingly, if U = P_0 and V = P′_0, then for every k,
P_k:P′_k = U:V,
and U:V is the triplicate ratio of d:d′. This uses the triangular-pyramid theorem directly, rather than introducing a separate result about similar polygonal pyramids. [12.8, D5.10, 5.12, 5.11]

**Step 4.** We show that these inscribed pyramids exhaust their cones. Consider one side AB of an inscribed polygon, and let M be the new point introduced between A and B by the next central-angle bisection. The radius OM bisects AB at right angles. Draw the tangent at M and the perpendiculars to AB through A and B. These bound a rectangle on AB, with its opposite side passing through M. The circular segment between AB and its minor arc is strictly contained in this rectangle. [1.4, 3.3, 3.16]

The rectangle is twice triangle ABM, since they have the same base and lie between the same parallels. Therefore the pyramid from S over this rectangle is twice the triangular pyramid S–ABM: their heights are equal, so their ratio is that of their bases. Here the polygonal-base theorem applies directly to the rectangle and triangle, avoiding an additional dissection. [1.41, 12.6]

The portion of C lying over the circular segment is strictly contained in that rectangular-based pyramid. Summing over all sides, the portions over the circular segments constitute C − P_k, while the pyramids S–ABM constitute P_{k+1} − P_k. Hence
C − P_k < 2(P_{k+1} − P_k).
Thus each refinement removes more than half the remaining difference between the cone and its inscribed pyramid. The identical argument applies to C′ and P′_k. [D11.18, CN5]

**Step 5.** It follows by exhaustion that C − P_k and C′ − P′_k can each be made less than any assigned positive solid magnitude. The same is true of any fixed multiple of either remainder, since its successive reductions also remove more than half. The construction can therefore be continued until any finitely many such requirements hold simultaneously. [10.1]

**Step 6.** We now establish the exact ratio, using the definition of proportional magnitudes. Let m and n be arbitrary positive integers. Since P_k:P′_k = U:V, subtraction of the initial pyramids gives, for k > 0,
(P_k − U):(P′_k − V) = U:V. [5.19]

If mU > nV, write δ = mU − nV. The definition of equal ratios gives
m(P_k − U) > n(P′_k − V),
so mP_k − nP′_k > δ. Choose k so that n(C′ − P′_k) < δ. Since C contains P_k, it follows that
mC > mP_k > nP′_k + δ > nC′.
If mU < nV, the same argument with the two cones interchanged gives mC < nC′. [D5.5, 10.1, CN5]

Finally, suppose mU = nV. Then mP_k = nP′_k for every k. If mC > nC′, choose k so that m(C − P_k) is smaller than the positive excess mC − nC′. This gives mP_k > nC′ > nP′_k, a contradiction. Interchanging the cones excludes mC < nC′. Therefore mC = nC′. [D5.5, 10.1, CN5]

Thus, for all m and n, the comparison of mC with nC′ agrees exactly with that of mU with nV. By the definition of equal ratios,
C:C′ = U:V.
By Step 3, this is the triplicate ratio of d:d′. The proposition is therefore established for similar cones. [D5.5, 5.11]

**Step 7.** Now let Z and Z′ be similar cylinders, and construct cones C and C′ on their respective bases with their respective heights. These cones are similar, because their axes and base-diameters have the same proportions as those of the cylinders. Each cylinder is three times its corresponding cone. Equal multiples preserve a ratio, so
Z:Z′ = C:C′,
which is the triplicate ratio of their base-diameters. Therefore similar cones, and likewise similar cylinders, are to one another in the triplicate ratio of the diameters in their bases. [D11.24, 12.10, 5.15]
