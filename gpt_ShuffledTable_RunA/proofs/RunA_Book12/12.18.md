## Proposition 12.18
**Statement:** Spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters.

**Step 1.** Let the spheres have solid magnitudes S and T, radii r and s, and diameters d and e. Place their centres together at O; this does not change their magnitudes [CN4]. Since the diameters are twice the radii, r:s = d:e [5.15]. We use the inscribed polyhedra of XII.17 and divide them into triangular pyramids: XII.8 is therefore the natural triplicate-ratio result for this route.

**Step 2.** Radial copies about O can be constructed by taking fourth proportional lengths on the rays [6.12]. In any triangle formed by O and two vertices, radial copying preserves the angle at O and multiplies its adjacent sides in the same ratio. The corresponding triangles are therefore equiangular, and their remaining sides have that same ratio [6.6, 6.4]. Consequently, corresponding triangular faces have proportional sides and are similar [6.5]; corresponding triangular pyramids are thus similar solid figures [D11.9].

Choose a triangular pyramid U with vertex O, and let V be its radial copy with lengths multiplied in the ratio s:r. By XII.8, U:V is the triplicate ratio of r:s, hence of d:e [12.8, 5.15]. It remains to prove S:T = U:V.

**Step 3.** Whenever P is an enclosing polyhedron about O and Q is its radial copy with lengths multiplied in the ratio s:r, triangulate the faces of P and join their triangles to O. Do the corresponding division in Q. Each pair of corresponding triangular pyramids has the ratio U:V, since each has the same triplicate ratio of corresponding lengths [12.8, 5.11]. Therefore their sums satisfy
P:Q = U:V [5.12].
The same argument applies to any radial enlargement of either polyhedron.

**Step 4.** Let k be any positive integer. Inside S take the concentric sphere of radius kr/(k+1). By XII.17, inscribe in S a polyhedron P whose boundary lies outside that smaller sphere, so that P contains it [12.17]. Let Q be the corresponding radial copy inscribed in T. Then Q contains the concentric sphere of radius ks/(k+1).

Enlarge P and Q radially by the factor (k+1)/k, obtaining P⁺ and Q⁺. These contain S and T respectively. Thus, as solid magnitudes,
P < S < P⁺, and Q < T < Q⁺ [CN5].

Put a = k³ and b = (k+1)³. By the triangular-pyramid calculation of Step 3, the ratios P⁺:P and Q⁺:Q are triplicate ratios of (k+1):k; consequently
 aP⁺ = bP, and aQ⁺ = bQ
[12.8, 5.12, D5.10, D5.5]. Hence, for every positive integer k, the constructed polyhedra satisfy
P:Q = U:V,
P < S, Q < T,
aS < bP, and aT < bQ.

**Step 5.** We shall use the following finite exhaustion calculation. Given solid magnitudes X > Y, choose a positive integer k such that
k(X − Y) > 7Y;
such a multiple exists by the Archimedean condition on magnitudes [D5.4]. Since
(k+1)³ − k³ = 3k² + 3k + 1 ≤ 7k²,
it follows, by multiplying the preceding strict inequality by k² and adding k³Y, that
k³X > (k+1)³Y.
Thus k can always be chosen so that aX > bY, with a and b as in Step 4.

**Step 6.** Take arbitrary positive integers m and n. Suppose first that mU > nV. By Step 5 choose k so that
amU > bnV,
and take the polyhedra supplied by Step 4 for this k. Because P:Q = U:V, the definition of equal ratios gives
amP > bnQ [D5.5].
The inequalities of Step 4 now yield
amS > amP > bnQ > anT.
Therefore mS > nT. Interchanging the two spheres and the corresponding pyramids proves likewise that mU < nV implies mS < nT [D5.5, CN5].

**Step 7.** Suppose instead that mU = nV. For every pair P,Q constructed in Step 4, equality of ratios gives mP = nQ [D5.5]. If mS > nT, choose k by Step 5 so that amS > bnT. But Step 4 gives
amS < bmP = bnQ < bnT,
a contradiction. If nT > mS, choose k so that anT > bmS; then, similarly,
anT < bnQ = bmP < bmS,
again a contradiction. Hence mS = nT [D5.5, CN5].

**Step 8.** For every pair of positive integers m,n, the multiples mS,nT have precisely the same comparison—greater, equal, or less—as mU,nV. Therefore S:T = U:V by the definition of equal ratios [D5.5]. Since U:V is the triplicate ratio of r:s = d:e, the spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters [12.8, 5.15].
