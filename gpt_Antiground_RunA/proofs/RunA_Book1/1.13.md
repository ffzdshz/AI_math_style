## Proposition 1.13
**Statement:** If a straight line set up on a straight line make angles, it will make either two right angles or angles equal to two right angles.

**Step 1.** Let A, B, C lie on one straight line, with B between A and C, and let BD stand on that line, making the adjacent angles ABD and DBC. If these angles are equal, each is a right angle by D1.10. This gives the first alternative.

**Step 2.** Suppose instead that the two angles are unequal. On the side of AC containing D, construct BE perpendicular to AC at B. We choose 1.11 directly because the point B is on the given line. The adjacent angles ABE and EBC are equal and are both right angles (1.11; D1.10). Consequently BE is distinct from BD. Interchanging the names A and C if necessary, we may take BD to lie between BA and BE.

**Step 3.** The adjacent angular parts coincide with their respective whole angles. Therefore
∠ABE = ∠ABD + ∠DBE,
and
∠DBC = ∠DBE + ∠EBC.
These are equalities of each whole with precisely the parts composing it (CN4).

**Step 4.** Adding ∠EBC to both sides of the first equality, and using the second equality to identify the adjoining parts ∠DBE and ∠EBC, gives
∠ABE + ∠EBC = ∠ABD + ∠DBE + ∠EBC = ∠ABD + ∠DBC
(CN2; CN1).

**Step 5.** Since ∠ABE and ∠EBC are both right angles, Step 4 shows that ∠ABD and ∠DBC together equal two right angles (D1.10; CN1). Together with Step 1, this proves that a straight line set up on a straight line makes either two right angles or angles equal to two right angles.
