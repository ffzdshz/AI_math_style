## Proposition 1.24
**Statement:** If two triangles have the two sides equal to two sides respectively, but have the one of the angles contained by the equal straight lines greater than the other, they will also have the base greater than the base.

**Step 1.** Let ABC and DEF be triangles with AB = DE, AC = DF, and ∠BAC > ∠EDF. We must prove BC > EF. (Hypotheses.)

**Step 2.** At A construct a ray inside ∠BAC making with AB an angle equal to ∠EDF; this placement is possible because ∠EDF < ∠BAC. On this ray take G such that AG = AC, extending a segment along the ray before cutting off the required length if necessary. Thus ∠BAG = ∠EDF and AG = DF. (1.23; P2; 1.3; CN1.)

**Step 3.** In triangles ABG and DEF, AB = DE, AG = DF, and the included angles ∠BAG and ∠EDF are equal. These are precisely the side–angle–side data, so 1.4 gives BG = EF. (1.4.)

**Step 4.** Path choice: bisect the excess angle ∠GAC, so that another application of side–angle–side will make the triangle inequality directly applicable. Let its internal bisector meet BC at X, and join GX. Since this bisector lies strictly inside ∠BAC, X lies between B and C. (1.9; P2; P1.)

**Step 5.** In triangles AGX and ACX, AG = AC, AX is common, and ∠GAX = ∠XAC by the bisection. Hence GX = CX. (1.4.)

**Step 6.** If G lies on BC, it lies between B and C, since the ray AG lies strictly inside ∠BAC. Consequently BG < BC, the whole being greater than its part. (CN5.) If G does not lie on BC, then B, G, and X form a triangle, and therefore BG < BX + XG. (1.20.) But XG = XC by Step 5, so BX + XG = BX + XC by addition of equals; and BX + XC = BC because X lies between B and C. Thus BG < BC in this case also. (CN2; Step 4.)

**Step 7.** By Step 3, BG = EF, while Step 6 gives BC > BG. Substituting the equal base EF for BG yields BC > EF. Therefore the triangle with the greater angle contained by the respectively equal sides has the greater base, as required. (Steps 3 and 6.)
