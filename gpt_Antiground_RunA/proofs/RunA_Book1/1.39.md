## Proposition 1.39
**Statement:** Equal triangles which are on the same base and on the same side are also in the same parallels.

**Step 1.** Let ABC and DBC be equal triangles on the same base BC, with A and D on the same side of BC. Through A draw a straight line ℓ parallel to BC. It suffices to show that D also lies on ℓ. [Hypothesis; 1.31]

**Step 2.** Suppose that D does not lie on ℓ. The straight line BD must meet ℓ: otherwise BD would be parallel to ℓ, and hence parallel to BC, since BC is also parallel to ℓ. This is impossible because BD and BC meet at B. Produce BD as necessary, and denote its intersection with ℓ by E. [D1.23; 1.30; P2]

**Step 3.** Since ℓ passes through A and never meets BC, E is on the same side of BC as A, and therefore as D. Consequently E lies on the ray from B through D. Also E differs from D by the supposition in Step 2. Join CE. [D1.23; hypothesis; P1]

**Step 4.** Triangles ABC and EBC are on the same base BC and between the same parallels BC and ℓ; therefore they are equal. The direct path is the same-base theorem 1.37: an equal-base generalization is unnecessary because the base has not changed. [1.37]

**Step 5.** Triangle DBC equals triangle ABC by hypothesis, and triangle EBC equals triangle ABC by Step 4. Hence triangles DBC and EBC are equal. [CN1]

**Step 6.** But E and D are distinct points on the same ray from B. If E lies between B and D, triangle EBC is a proper part of triangle DBC; if D lies between B and E, triangle DBC is a proper part of triangle EBC. In either case the whole is greater than its part, contradicting Step 5. [Step 3; CN5]

**Step 7.** Thus D lies on ℓ. Both triangles therefore have their base on BC and their opposite vertices on ℓ, which is parallel to BC. They are consequently in the same parallels, as required. [Steps 1–6; D1.23]
