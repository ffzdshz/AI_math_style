## Proposition 11.3
**Statement:** If two planes cut one another, their common section is a straight line.

**Step 1.** Let the distinct planes α and β cut one another, and let S be their common section. Choose distinct points A and B on S and join AB. The segment AB lies in each plane, since its endpoints lie in each plane (P1, D1.7). Produce AB to the straight line ℓ (P2). The whole line ℓ remains in both planes, since a straight line cannot lie partly in a plane and partly outside it (11.1).

**Step 2.** Suppose, for contradiction, that S contains a point C not on ℓ. Join AC and produce it to the straight line m. As in Step 1, m lies in both planes (P1, P2, D1.7, 11.1). Choose D on ℓ beyond B (P2). Thus B and D are distinct points of ℓ, neither lying on m.

**Step 3.** Since the planes are distinct, choose a point X in α but not in β, interchanging their names if necessary. Draw and produce the straight lines XB and XD; both lie in α (P1, P2, D1.7, 11.1). They are distinct: otherwise their common line would contain the segment BD in β, and 11.1 would force X also to lie in β.

**Step 4.** The line XB cannot meet m. For if it met m at E, then E and B would be distinct points of β. Consequently the segment BE would lie in β (D1.7), and the whole straight line XB would lie in β (11.1), contradicting the choice of X. The same reasoning, with D in place of B, shows that XD cannot meet m (D1.7, 11.1).

**Step 5.** Thus XB and XD are both parallel to m: they lie with m in α and do not meet it (D1.23). We now use 1.30 directly, since two lines parallel to the same line have arisen: it gives XB parallel to XD. But XB and XD meet at X, contrary to the definition of parallel straight lines (D1.23). This is the contradiction.

**Step 6.** Therefore S has no point outside ℓ. Conversely, the whole line ℓ belongs to both planes by Step 1 (11.1). Hence their common section S is exactly the straight line ℓ. Therefore, if two planes cut one another, their common section is a straight line.
