## Proposition 11.2
**Statement:** If two straight lines cut one another, they are in one plane, and every triangle is in one plane.

**Step 1.** Let the distinct straight lines ℓ and m cut one another at O, and choose a point Q on m different from O. Then Q is not on ℓ. Through Q draw a straight line n parallel to ℓ (1.31). This is the chosen route because the parallel construction supplies a common plane by the definition of parallel lines, rather than presupposing a plane containing ℓ and m.

**Step 2.** Since n and ℓ are parallel, they lie in one plane Π (D1.23). Thus O, which lies on ℓ, and Q, which lies on n, both belong to Π. Their straight join OQ lies in Π (P1, D1.7). This join is a nonzero segment of m.

**Step 3.** A straight line cannot have one part in a plane and another part elevated above it (11.1). Since the segment OQ of m lies in Π, the whole straight line m lies in Π. Therefore the two intersecting straight lines ℓ and m lie in one plane.

**Step 4.** Now let ABC be any triangle. Its side-lines AB and AC intersect at A, so the argument of Steps 1–3 places both in one plane Σ. In particular, B and C lie in Σ; hence their straight join BC also lies in Σ (P1, D1.7).

**Step 5.** All three sides AB, BC, and CA therefore lie in Σ, and the trilateral figure they bound—the triangle ABC—is in that plane (D1.19). Thus two intersecting straight lines are in one plane, and every triangle is in one plane.
