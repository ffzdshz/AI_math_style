## Proposition 11.14
**Statement:** Planes to which the same straight line is at right angles will be parallel.

**Step 1.** Let Π and Σ be distinct planes, and let the straight line l be perpendicular to both, meeting them at A and B respectively. A perpendicular is not contained in the plane to which it is perpendicular (D11.3); consequently, by 11.1, l meets each plane only at its respective foot.

**Step 2.** The feet A and B are distinct. For otherwise, let their common point be O. Choose two intersecting straight lines r and s through O in Π, and a straight line t through O in Σ not contained in Π; such a choice is possible because the planes are distinct (P1, 11.1, 11.3). By D11.3, l is perpendicular to all three lines. Therefore r, s, and t lie in one plane by 11.5. That plane must be Π: otherwise its common section with Π would contain the two intersecting lines r and s, contrary to 11.3. This would put t in Π, a contradiction. Hence A ≠ B.

**Step 3.** Suppose that Π and Σ meet, and take a common point C. By Steps 1–2, C cannot lie on l. Join AC and BC (P1). Since their endpoints belong to the respective planes, AC lies in Π and BC lies in Σ (11.1).

**Step 4.** The noncollinear points A, B, and C form a triangle lying in one plane (11.2). Since AB lies on the common perpendicular l, while AC lies in Π and BC lies in Σ, both ∠CAB and ∠ABC are right angles (D11.3).

**Step 5.** But any two angles of a triangle are together less than two right angles (1.17), whereas these two angles are themselves two right angles. This is a contradiction. The direct two-angle bound of 1.17 is the natural choice here; no calculation of the full triangle-angle sum is needed.

**Step 6.** Thus Π and Σ do not meet. Planes which do not meet are parallel (D11.8). Therefore planes to which the same straight line is at right angles are parallel.
