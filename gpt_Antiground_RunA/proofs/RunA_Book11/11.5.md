## Proposition 11.5
**Statement:** If a straight line be set up at right angles to three straight lines which meet one another, at their common point of section, the three straight lines are in one plane.

**Step 1.** Let OA, OB, and OC be the three given straight lines meeting at O, and let OD be perpendicular to each at O. The intersecting straight lines OA and OB lie in a plane Π. [Hypothesis; 11.2]

**Step 2.** Since OD is perpendicular to both OA and OB at their intersection, OD is perpendicular to Π. We use 11.4 directly to obtain perpendicularity to the plane, rather than reconstructing its proof. [11.4]

**Step 3.** Suppose, for a contradiction, that OC does not lie in Π. The intersecting lines OD and OC lie in a plane Σ, distinct from Π. These planes meet at O, and their common section is a straight line g through O. [11.2; 11.3]

**Step 4.** Since g lies in Π and passes through O, OD is perpendicular to g. Both g and OC lie in Σ. Choose a point E on g so that the ray OE lies on the opposite side of OD from the ray OC. Then ∠DOE is right, and ∠DOC is right by hypothesis. [D11.3; hypothesis]

**Step 5.** Thus, in Σ, the rays OE and OC lie on opposite sides of OD and make with it adjacent angles together equal to two right angles. Therefore OE and OC are in one straight line. Here 1.14 applies directly to the adjacent right angles, so no separate uniqueness theorem for perpendiculars is needed. [P4; 1.14]

**Step 6.** Consequently OC is part of the same straight line as g. Since g lies in Π, that straight line cannot have another part outside Π; hence OC also lies in Π, contrary to the supposition. Therefore OA, OB, and OC are all in one plane. [11.1]
