## Proposition 5.1
**Statement:** If a magnitude be the same multiple of a magnitude that a part subtracted is of a part subtracted, the remainder will also be the same multiple of the remainder that the whole is of the whole.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be the whole magnitudes, and let C and D be the parts subtracted from A and B respectively. Suppose A is the same multiple of B that C is of D. Thus, for some common number n of repetitions, A consists of n copies of B, and C consists of n copies of D. We use this equal-copy decomposition and direct subtraction, rather than a ratio argument. [Hypothesis; D5.2]

**Step 2.** Denote the n copies composing A by B₁, …, Bₙ. In each Bᵢ take the corresponding part Dᵢ equal to D, and call the remainder Rᵢ. This uses the decomposition of B into D and its remainder in each copy of B. Since Bᵢ equals B and Dᵢ equals D, each Rᵢ equals the remainder B − D. [D5.2; CN3]

**Step 3.** The parts D₁, …, Dₙ are respectively equal to the n copies of D composing C, because each corresponding pair is equal to D. Adding these equalities shows that the marked-off parts taken together equal C. [CN1; CN2, repeatedly]

**Step 4.** The pieces R₁, …, Rₙ together are what remains of A after those marked-off parts have been removed. Since their total removal equals C, their total remainder equals A − C: equal magnitudes have been subtracted from equal wholes. [CN3]

**Step 5.** Therefore A − C consists of n pieces, each equal to B − D. Consequently A − C is n times B − D, which is the same multiple that A is of B. Hence the remainder is the same multiple of the remainder that the whole is of the whole. [Steps 2 and 4; D5.2]
