## Proposition 5.2
**Statement:** If a first magnitude be the same multiple of a second that a third is of a fourth, and a fifth also be the same multiple of the second that a sixth is of the fourth, the sum of the first and fifth will also be the same multiple of the second that the sum of the third and sixth is of the fourth.

**Step 1.** Denote the first through sixth magnitudes by A, B, C, D, E, F, respectively. We use the direct route of counting equal parts, as supplied by the definition of a multiple. By the first hypothesis, A consists of some number m of parts each equal to B, while C consists of the same number m of parts each equal to D. [Hypothesis; D5.2]

**Step 2.** By the second hypothesis, E consists of some number n of parts each equal to B, while F consists of the same number n of parts each equal to D. The number n need not equal m. [Hypothesis; D5.2]

**Step 3.** Add the decomposition of E to that of A, and the decomposition of F to that of C. Addition of equals to equals shows that A + E equals the sum of the m copies of B followed by the n copies of B, while C + F equals the sum of the m copies of D followed by the n copies of D. Thus each combined list has m + n parts, all equal to its respective measuring magnitude. [CN2]

**Step 4.** Consequently, B measures A + E exactly as many times as D measures C + F, namely m + n times. Therefore the sum of the first and fifth is the same multiple of the second that the sum of the third and sixth is of the fourth, as required. [D5.2]
