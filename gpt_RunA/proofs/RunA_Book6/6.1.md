## Proposition 6.1
**Statement:** Triangles and parallelograms which are under the same height are to one another as their bases.

**Step 1.** Let T and U be the areas of two triangles whose bases have lengths b and c and whose heights are equal. Superpose their equal perpendicular heights on the same segment AO; this changes neither their areas nor their base lengths (D6.4; CN4). Their base-lines coincide in a straight line ℓ, since both are perpendicular to AO at O (1.14). Through A draw the parallel p to ℓ (1.31). Both triangles are therefore between ℓ and p.

**Step 2.** Let m and n be arbitrary positive integers. Starting from O along one ray of ℓ, lay off successive segments OX₁, X₁X₂, …, Xₘ₋₁Xₘ, each equal to b. Independently, along the same ray, lay off OY₁, Y₁Y₂, …, Yₙ₋₁Yₙ, each equal to c. These constructions are made by producing the line and repeatedly cutting off a segment equal to the given base (P2; 1.3). Join A to all the division points (P1). By the definition of a multiple, OXₘ = mb and OYₙ = nc (D5.2).

**Step 3.** Every triangle on one of the successive b-segments with vertex A equals T: its base equals b, and it lies between the same parallels ℓ and p as the original triangle. Likewise, every triangle on one of the successive c-segments with vertex A equals U (1.38). The first collection partitions triangle AOXₘ, and the second partitions triangle AOYₙ. Adding these equal areas therefore gives area(AOXₘ) = mT and area(AOYₙ) = nU (CN2; D5.2).

**Step 4.** Compare OXₘ and OYₙ. If OXₘ is greater, Yₙ lies between O and Xₘ, so triangle AOYₙ is a proper part of triangle AOXₘ; hence mT > nU (CN5). If the segments are equal, Xₘ and Yₙ coincide, so the triangles coincide and mT = nU (CN4). If OXₘ is less, the same proper-part argument with the triangles interchanged gives mT < nU (CN5). Thus mT and nU have exactly the same comparison as mb and nc.

**Step 5.** Since m and n were arbitrary, the equimultiple definition of equal ratios gives T:U = b:c (D5.5). This direct route makes no assumption that the bases have a common measure. The assertion for triangles is proved.

**Step 6.** Now let P and Q be the areas of two parallelograms with respective bases b and c and equal heights. Use diagonal bisection, since it supplies the required half-triangles directly: draw a diagonal in each parallelogram (P1), and let T′ and U′ be the areas of the resulting triangles having the specified bases. Each diagonal bisects its parallelogram, so P = 2T′ and Q = 2U′ (1.34). These triangles have the respective bases b and c and retain the common height of the parallelograms (D6.4).

**Step 7.** Equimultiples have the same ratio as the original magnitudes, so P:Q = (2T′):(2U′) = T′:U′ (5.15). By Step 5, T′:U′ = b:c. Consequently P:Q = b:c (5.11). Therefore triangles of the same height are to one another as their bases, and parallelograms of the same height are likewise to one another as their bases.
