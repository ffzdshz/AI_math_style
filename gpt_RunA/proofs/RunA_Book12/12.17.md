## Proposition 12.17
**Statement:** Given two spheres about the same centre, to inscribe in the greater sphere a polyhedral solid which does not touch the lesser sphere at its surface.

**Step 1.** Let O be the common centre, and let R and r be the radii of the greater and lesser spheres, respectively, so that R > r > 0 (D11.14, D11.16). Construct the mean proportional s between R and r; thus s² = Rr and r < s < R (6.13, 6.17; CN5). The natural preceding construction is 12.16: we shall use the same polygon both around an axis and in meridian sections. The mean proportional supplies the margin needed for these two directions. Products below denote rectangles, and squares denote squares on the indicated lengths.

**Step 2.** In a plane E through O, describe concentric circles of radii R and s (P3). By 12.16, inscribe in the greater circle an equilateral polygon G having an even number 2m of sides, with its sides wholly outside the lesser circle. Equal chords give equal successive arcs and equal central angles; consequently G is regular, and opposite vertices are endpoints of diameters (3.28, 3.27). Its sides have one common perpendicular distance a from O (1.12, 3.14). Therefore a > s. A perpendicular from O bisects its side, so the right triangle formed with a vertex gives a < R (3.3, 1.47; CN5). Write k = a/R; thus 0 < k < 1.

**Step 3.** Erect through O the diameter NS perpendicular to E (11.12), and label the vertices of G consecutively V₀, …, V₂ₘ₋₁. In each meridian half-plane bounded by NS and containing OVⱼ, copy the half of G running between two opposite vertices, placing those vertices at N and S. This is done by copying its central angles (1.23). Denote its successive vertices by A₀,ⱼ = N, A₁,ⱼ, …, Aₘ,ⱼ = S. These are finitely many positions of the same polygonal semicircle during the rotation defining the greater sphere; hence every Aᵢ,ⱼ lies on that sphere (D11.14; CN4).

For each fixed i, the corresponding vertices have the same height on NS and the same distance ρᵢ from it. If Hᵢ is their common perpendicular foot on NS, they therefore form a regular polygon in the plane through Hᵢ perpendicular to NS, with centre Hᵢ and circumradius ρᵢ. These assertions follow directly from the congruent meridian positions and their perpendiculars (1.12; D11.14; CN4). At i = 0 and i = m the polygon reduces to a pole.

**Step 4.** Fix adjacent meridians, indexed j and j + 1, taking indices cyclically. Let M be the midpoint of VⱼVⱼ₊₁, and let B be the plane through NS and OM (3.3, 11.2). For each nondegenerate ring, let Uᵢ be the midpoint of Aᵢ,ⱼAᵢ,ⱼ₊₁. The corresponding midpoint radius HᵢUᵢ lies in B and is perpendicular to that ring edge (3.3). The right triangles HᵢUᵢAᵢ,ⱼ and OMVⱼ are similar, since the rings have the same central angles. Hence
HᵢUᵢ / ρᵢ = OM / OVⱼ = a/R = k
(6.4). At a pole put Uᵢ equal to that pole; the equality HᵢUᵢ = kρᵢ then still holds.

**Step 5.** Each nondegenerate ring edge Aᵢ,ⱼAᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ is perpendicular to B. Indeed, it is perpendicular to HᵢUᵢ, and also to the line through Uᵢ parallel to NS: this latter line is perpendicular to the ring plane (1.31, 11.8; D11.3). These two intersecting lines lie in B, so 11.4 applies. Two consecutive ring edges are therefore parallel (11.6), and hence coplanar (D1.23). Joining corresponding endpoints produces a plane trapezium; at either pole it produces a plane triangle (P1, 11.2). Perform these joins for every pair of consecutive rings and every pair of adjacent meridians.

**Step 6.** The resulting trapezia and polar triangles form a closed polyhedral surface. Indeed, between consecutive ring planes, every section parallel to E is a regular polygon with corresponding vertices on the meridian edges; this follows from parallel-line proportions (6.2). These sections occur successively from N to S, and their interiors contain the intervening part of the axis. Thus the faces bound a solid K, and O is in its interior (D11.1, D11.2).

Moreover, K is inscribed in the greater sphere. All its vertices lie on that sphere by Step 3. A segment joining two points of the sphere lies inside it, by applying 3.2 in the plane through those points and O (11.2; D11.14). The same holds for two points already inside, by extending their line to the sphere (P2, 3.2). The filled ring polygons and the filled layers between them consist of such segments. Consequently the whole solid lies within the greater sphere.

**Step 7.** Consider any face between the two meridians fixed in Step 4, and let P be any point of that face. Its perpendicular projection onto B is a point Q of the segment UᵢUᵢ₊₁. To see this directly, draw through P the line parallel to the ring edges (1.31). Parallel-line proportions show that the midpoint of this section of the trapezium or triangle lies on UᵢUᵢ₊₁; for a parallelogram the same assertion follows from its equal opposite sides (6.2, 1.34). That line is perpendicular to B, since it is parallel to a line perpendicular to B (11.8). Its midpoint is therefore Q. If P already lies in B, take Q = P. Since O lies in B,
OP² = OQ² + PQ²,
and consequently OP ≥ OQ (D11.3, 1.47).

**Step 8.** In B, copy the original polygonal semicircle from N to S, on the side containing the Uᵢ, and call its corresponding vertices Cᵢ (1.23; CN4). Thus Cᵢ has foot Hᵢ on NS and HᵢCᵢ = ρᵢ. By Step 4,
HᵢUᵢ = k·HᵢCᵢ.
Accordingly, UᵢUᵢ₊₁ is obtained from CᵢCᵢ₊₁ by reducing all distances perpendicular to NS in the fixed ratio k, while preserving their heights.

More explicitly, for every Q on UᵢUᵢ₊₁ there is a point Y on CᵢCᵢ₊₁ with the same perpendicular foot T on NS and with TQ = k·TY. If the original side meets NS when produced, this follows by applying the intercept theorem and similar triangles with that intersection as vertex (6.2, 6.4). If the side is parallel to NS, it follows from the equal opposite sides of the resulting parallelograms (1.34).

**Step 9.** The side CᵢCᵢ₊₁ has perpendicular distance a from O, because it belongs to a congruent copy of G (3.14; CN4). Dropping the perpendicular from O to that side and applying 1.47 gives OY² ≥ a² (1.12, 1.47). Applying 1.47 also to the perpendiculars at T, and using 0 < k < 1, gives
OQ² = OT² + TQ²
= OT² + k²TY²
≥ k²OT² + k²TY²
= k²OY²
≥ k²a².
Here the inequality follows by comparing a square with the smaller similar square (CN5). Hence, together with Step 7, OP ≥ OQ ≥ ka.

**Step 10.** Since a > s and s² = Rr,
ka = a²/R > s²/R = r.
The strict comparison follows by comparing the squares and then rectangles of the same altitude R (6.17, 6.1; CN5). Thus every point P on every face of K satisfies OP > r, including all edges and vertices.

Since O is inside K, no radius of the lesser sphere can leave K: its first exit would be a boundary point at distance at most r, contradicting the inequality just proved (D11.2). Therefore the lesser sphere lies strictly inside K, and no part of the surface of K touches it. The solid K is the required polyhedral solid inscribed in the greater sphere.
