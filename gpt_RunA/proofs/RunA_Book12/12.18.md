## Proposition 12.18
**Statement:** Spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters.

**Step 1.** Let the spheres be S and T, with diameters a and b. Place their centres together at O; this does not change their magnitudes (CN4). Let A and B be similarly placed, concentric cubes with edges a and b. They contain the respective spheres: the perpendicular projection of a radius cannot exceed that radius (D11.14, D11.17, 1.47). By 11.33, A:B is the triplicate ratio of a:b, in the sense of D5.10. We shall prove S:T = A:B. Our route uses the inscribed polyhedra of 12.17 and their subdivision into triangular pyramids, for which 12.8 is directly applicable. Small cubical shells will control the approximation error without assuming a formula for spherical volume.

**Step 2.** Consider a centre-enclosing polyhedron P inscribed in S, and form its radial copy Q in T: each vertex is moved along its ray from O to the surface of T. The corresponding distances from O are in the ratio a:b, since radii have the same ratio as their doubles, the diameters (5.15). Triangulate the faces of P and join their vertices to O (P1), thereby dividing P into triangular pyramids; make the corresponding subdivision of Q. In corresponding triangles having O as a vertex, the included angles are equal and the containing sides proportional, so the triangles are equiangular by 6.6, and their remaining sides are proportional by 6.4. Consequently, corresponding base triangles also have proportional sides and are equiangular by 6.5. Thus the corresponding pyramids are similar (D6.1, D11.9).

**Step 3.** By 12.8, every pair of corresponding pyramids has the triplicate ratio of a:b, hence the ratio A:B by 11.33 and 5.11. Applying 5.12 to these equal ratios gives P:Q = A:B. Alternately, by 5.16,
P:A = Q:B.
The same calculation applies to a radial enlargement of any such polyhedron and the corresponding enlargement of its concentric cube. The use of 5.12 is direct here because all corresponding pairs already have the same ratio.

**Step 4.** Fix a positive solid magnitude E and a positive integer k. We first construct a cube A⁺, concentric with A and slightly larger, such that k(A⁺ − A) < E. Repeatedly bisect a twice, obtaining h_j = a/4^j (1.10), and let A_j have edge a + h_j. After placing congruent copies of A_j and A at a common corner, their difference divides into three rectangular boxes with dimensions
h_j × (a + h_j) × (a + h_j),
a × h_j × (a + h_j),
a × a × h_j.
For j ≥ 1, each is smaller than a box with dimensions 2a × 2a × h_j. Let V_j be the sum of three such bounding boxes. Therefore A_j − A < V_j (CN2, CN3, CN4, CN5). Dividing their heights into four equal parts shows V_{j+1} is one quarter of V_j (CN2, CN4). Thus the sequence kV_j repeatedly removes three quarters of the preceding magnitude; by 10.1, choose j with kV_j < E. Put h = h_j and A⁺ = A_j. Then k(A⁺ − A) < E.

**Step 5.** Construct a⁻ such that (a + h):a = a:a⁻ (6.11), and take the concentric inner sphere S⁻ of diameter a⁻ (D11.14, D11.17). By 12.17, inscribe in S a polyhedron P enclosing S⁻ without its boundary touching S⁻. Enlarge P radially in the ratio (a + h):a, obtaining P⁺. This enlargement carries S⁻ onto S and A onto A⁺. Hence P is contained in S, while P⁺ contains S. By the calculation in Steps 2–3,
P⁺:P = A⁺:A.

**Step 6.** Separating this proportion and then alternating gives
(P⁺ − P):(A⁺ − A) = P:A
(5.17, 5.16). Since P < A, the definition of equal ratios implies P⁺ − P < A⁺ − A (CN5, D5.5). Also S − P < P⁺ − P, because S is contained in P⁺ (CN3, CN5). Consequently,
k(S − P) < k(A⁺ − A) < E
(CN2). We have therefore shown, for either sphere and any prescribed E and k, how to choose an inscribed polyhedron whose omitted magnitude, multiplied by k, is less than E.

**Step 7.** Let m and n be arbitrary positive integers. Suppose mS > nA, and put E = mS − nA. By Steps 4–6 choose P inscribed in S with m(S − P) < E. Subtraction gives mP > nA (CN3). Its radial copy Q is inscribed in T, and P:A = Q:B by Step 3. Thus mQ > nB by D5.5; since Q < T, it follows that mT > nB (CN2, CN5). Interchanging the spheres proves the converse. Therefore, for every m and n,
mS > nA if and only if mT > nB.

**Step 8.** Suppose now that mS = nA. Step 7 excludes mT > nB. If mT < nB, let Δ = nB − mT. Choose a positive integer r with rΔ > T (D5.4). Since mS = nA,
(rm + 1)S > rnA.
But
(rm + 1)T = rnB − rΔ + T < rnB
(CN2, CN3, CN5), contradicting Step 7 with the multipliers rm + 1 and rn. Therefore mT = nB. Interchanging the spheres proves the converse equality implication. Together with Step 7, this also shows that mS < nA if and only if mT < nB.

**Step 9.** Thus arbitrary equimultiples of S and T alike exceed, equal, or fall short of the corresponding equimultiples of A and B. By D5.5, S:A = T:B. Alternating gives S:T = A:B (5.16). Since A:B is the triplicate ratio of a:b (11.33, D5.10), the spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters, as required.
