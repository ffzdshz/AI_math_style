## Proposition 12.2
**Statement:** Circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters.

**Step 1.** Let C and D denote the areas of the two circles, and let S and T denote the areas of the squares on their respective diameters (D1.17; 1.46). Path choice: use 12.1 directly, since it already gives the required diameter-square ratio for similar inscribed polygons, and pass from polygons to circles by exhaustion (10.1), verifying the equimultiple criterion D5.5.

**Step 2.** In each circle inscribe a square whose vertices are the endpoints of perpendicular diameters (4.6). Repeatedly bisect every angle between consecutive vertex-radii, extend each bisector to the circumference, and join consecutive circumference points (1.9; P1). Write P_j and Q_j for the polygonal areas after j refinements, with P_0 and Q_0 the squares. Corresponding central angles are equal initially, being right angles, and remain equal under the matching bisections (P4; 1.9). Central-angle bisection is chosen because it preserves this equality directly. All polygonal sides lie within their circles (3.2), and each refinement enlarges the preceding polygon.

**Step 3.** At any fixed stage, corresponding triangles formed by two consecutive vertex-radii have equal included angles and proportional sides about those angles: all radii within either circle are equal (D1.15). These triangles are therefore equiangular by 6.6, and their corresponding sides are proportional by 6.4. Consequently all corresponding polygonal sides have the common ratio of the two radii. Adding the adjacent triangle angles at corresponding vertices gives equal polygonal angles (CN2). Thus P_j and Q_j are similar polygons (D6.1), and 12.1 gives, at every stage,

P_j : Q_j = S : T.

**Step 4.** Consider one side AB of either polygon, with centre O. Let the bisector of angle AOB meet AB at H and the minor arc AB at M. Since OA = OB, the triangles OAH and OBH are congruent by 1.4; hence their adjacent angles at H are equal, so OM is perpendicular to AB (D1.15; D1.10).

Through M draw a parallel to AB, and through A and B draw parallels to OM, forming a rectangle R above AB (1.31; 1.29). Its side through M is perpendicular to the radius OM and therefore touches the circle at M (3.16). The circular segment K bounded by AB and its minor arc is contained in R (D3.6): the tangent bounds it above; moreover, for any point X on the open minor arc AB, angles XAB and ABX lie in segments greater than semicircles and are acute (3.31), placing X between the perpendiculars through A and B. Thus K is a proper part of R, and its area is less than that of R (CN5).

**Step 5.** Triangle AMB lies in K, since its bounding chords lie within the circle (3.2). It has the same base AB as R and lies between the same parallels, so R is twice triangle AMB (1.41). Since K is smaller than R, triangle AMB is greater than half of K. Inserting M therefore removes more than half of this segment from the polygonal remainder. The same holds for every side. Adding these comparisons over the finitely many segments shows that each refinement removes more than half of the total remainder (CN2; CN3; CN5). Thus, putting

E_j = C − P_j and F_j = D − Q_j,

we have E_(j+1) < E_j/2 and F_(j+1) < F_j/2.

**Step 6.** Consequently, for every positive area ε and every fixed positive integer h, a sufficiently advanced stage satisfies hE_j < ε; likewise a sufficiently advanced stage satisfies hF_j < ε. Indeed, taking h copies preserves the fact that more than half is removed at each refinement (CN2), so 10.1 applies directly to these multiple remainders.

**Step 7.** Take arbitrary positive integers m and n. Suppose first that mC > nD. By Step 6 choose j such that

mE_j < mC − nD.

Then

mP_j = mC − mE_j > nD > nQ_j

(CN2; CN3; CN5). Since P_j : Q_j = S : T by Step 3, the defining equimultiple criterion yields mS > nT (D5.5).

**Step 8.** If instead mC < nD, choose j such that

nF_j < nD − mC

by Step 6. Then

nQ_j = nD − nF_j > mC > mP_j

(CN2; CN3; CN5). The same polygonal proportion therefore yields mS < nT (D5.5).

**Step 9.** Finally suppose mC = nD. If mS > nT, the Archimedean condition in D5.4 supplies a positive integer h such that

h(mS − nT) > nT,

and hence hmS > (h + 1)nT. But hmC = hnD < (h + 1)nD, so Step 8, applied to the integers hm and (h + 1)n, gives hmS < (h + 1)nT, a contradiction.

If mS < nT, choose h such that h(nT − mS) > mS (D5.4). Then hnT > (h + 1)mS. But (h + 1)mC > hmC = hnD, so Step 7 gives (h + 1)mS > hnT, again a contradiction. These additions, subtractions, and strict comparisons follow from CN2, CN3, and CN5. Therefore mC = nD implies mS = nT.

**Step 10.** For every pair of positive integers m and n, the multiples mC and nD accordingly exceed, equal, or fall short of one another exactly as mS and nT do. Hence C : D = S : T by D5.5. Therefore circles are to one another as the squares on their diameters.
