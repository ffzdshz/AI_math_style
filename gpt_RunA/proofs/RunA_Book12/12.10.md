## Proposition 12.10
**Statement:** Any cone is a third part of the cylinder which has the same base with it and equal height.

**Step 1.** Let K be the given cone, with vertex V and circular base D having centre O. Let Z be the cylinder on D whose height equals the perpendicular height VO of the cone. We also use K and Z for their solid magnitudes, and write 3K for three copies of K added together. We shall prove Z = 3K by exhausting both solids with inscribed polyhedra; the factor three will come from the triangular-prism division, not from any comparison theorem about cones or cylinders. [D11.18, D11.21]

**Step 2.** For a convex polygon F in the base plane, denote by p(F) the pyramid with base F and vertex V, and by q(F) the perpendicular prism with base F and height VO. [D11.12, D11.13]

The division of a triangular prism into three equal pyramids includes a pyramid whose base is a triangular face of the prism and whose vertex belongs to the opposite face; its height is therefore the prism's height. [12.7] Any pyramid on that same triangular base with the same height equals this pyramid, by the equal-height comparison for triangular pyramids. [12.5, D5.5] Consequently q(F) = 3p(F) when F is triangular. Triangulating any convex polygon F and adding the corresponding equalities gives
q(F) = 3p(F). [P1, CN2]
Here 12.5 is the naturally first comparison: the pieces being compared have triangular bases.

**Step 3.** Inscribe a square F₀ in D. [4.6] Refine it repeatedly as follows. For each side AB of the current polygon, draw OH perpendicular to AB; H is the midpoint of AB. [1.12, 3.3] Produce OH to meet the minor arc AB at M, and replace AB by AM and MB. [P2, P1] Thus a triangle AMB is added over every old side. Its sides lie within the circle. [3.2]

Write Fₙ for the successive polygons, Kₙ = p(Fₙ), and Zₙ = q(Fₙ). Each polygon contains O and is inscribed in D. Therefore its pyramid and prism are contained strictly in the cone and cylinder respectively, and Step 2 gives
Zₙ = 3Kₙ, Kₙ < K, Zₙ < Z. [D11.18, D11.21, CN5]

**Step 4.** Consider one side AB and the point M introduced over it. Let S be the circular segment bounded by AB and its minor arc, and let T be the triangle AMB. Through M draw a parallel to AB, and through A and B draw parallels to OM. These lines, together with AB, enclose a rectangle R. [1.31, 1.29] Its side through M is perpendicular to the radius OM and hence is tangent to the circle. [3.16]

The rectangle contains S. To verify the lateral bounds explicitly, take a point X of S off OM and let E be its perpendicular projection onto OM. [1.12] Since X is on the side of AB away from O, OE ≥ OH. Also OX ≤ OA, since X belongs to the circular disk. [D1.15, CN5] By Pythagoras,
EX² + OE² = OX² ≤ OA² = AH² + OH²,
so EX ≤ AH. Thus X lies between the sides through A and B; the tangent through M supplies the upper bound. Points on OM satisfy these bounds immediately. [1.47, CN3, CN5, 3.16]

The containment S ⊂ R is strict, while T lies within S. [3.2, 3.16] Moreover, the rectangle and triangle have the same base AB and lie between the same parallels, so
R = 2T. [1.41]

**Step 5.** Let W be the portion of the cone formed by joining V to all points of S, and let U be the portion of the cylinder standing perpendicularly over S. Because S is strictly contained in R,
W < p(R), U < q(R). [D11.18, D11.21, CN5]

The pyramids p(R) and p(T) have equal height. The polygonal-base comparison, applied to R = 2T, therefore gives
p(R) = 2p(T). [12.6, D5.5]
By Step 2, q(R) = 3p(R) and q(T) = 3p(T), whence q(R) = 2q(T). [CN2] Consequently
W < 2p(T), U < 2q(T).
Thus the added pyramid p(T) removes more than half of the conical remainder over S, and the added prism q(T) removes more than half of the cylindrical remainder over S.

**Step 6.** The circular segments over all sides of Fₙ partition the part of D outside Fₙ. Correspondingly, their conical portions partition K − Kₙ, and their cylindrical portions partition Z − Zₙ. Upon refinement, the triangles described in Step 3 add exactly their pyramids and prisms. Adding the comparisons of Step 5 therefore shows that more than half of each entire remainder is removed at every refinement. [D11.18, D11.21, CN2, CN3]

Hence each of the sequences of positive magnitudes
Z − Zₙ and 3(K − Kₙ)
is reduced by more than half at each step. By exhaustion, each can be made smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. [CN2, 10.1]

**Step 7.** Suppose first that Z > 3K. Apply Step 6 to the positive difference Z − 3K, choosing n such that
Z − Zₙ < Z − 3K.
Subtracting the remainders from Z gives Zₙ > 3K. [CN3] But Zₙ = 3Kₙ by Step 3, and Kₙ < K, so Zₙ < 3K. [CN2, CN5] This is a contradiction. Therefore Z is not greater than 3K.

**Step 8.** Suppose instead that Z < 3K. Apply Step 6 to the positive difference 3K − Z, choosing n such that
3(K − Kₙ) < 3K − Z.
Subtracting from 3K gives 3Kₙ > Z. [CN2, CN3] But 3Kₙ = Zₙ, and Zₙ < Z because the inscribed prism is a proper part of the cylinder. [CN5] This too is a contradiction. Therefore Z is not less than 3K.

**Step 9.** Since Z is neither greater nor less than 3K, Z = 3K. Thus the cone measures the cylinder exactly three times: any cone is a third part of the cylinder having the same base and equal height. [D5.1]
