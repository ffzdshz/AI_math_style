## Proposition 7.39
**Statement:** To find the number which is the least that will have given parts.

**Step 1.** Let the given parts be those named by the numbers d₁, …, dᵣ: thus, for example, a third is named by three. A number measured by every dᵢ has every prescribed part, by 7.37; conversely, a number having every prescribed part is measured by every dᵢ, by 7.38. We must therefore construct the least number measured by all these numbers. [7.37, 7.38]

**Step 2.** Put L₁ = d₁. Successively, for each k from 2 through r, construct Lₖ as the least number measured by both Lₖ₋₁ and dₖ, using 7.34. Let L = Lᵣ. If only one part is given, simply take L = d₁. Path choice: the two-number construction is the first applicable construction and, applied successively, handles any finite list of prescribed parts. [7.34]

**Step 3.** Each Lₖ is measured by d₁, …, dₖ. Indeed, this holds for L₁. At each subsequent stage, Lₖ is measured by Lₖ₋₁ and by dₖ. Every earlier dᵢ measures Lₖ₋₁ and consequently also measures Lₖ: replacing each equal copy of Lₖ₋₁ by its equal copies of dᵢ expresses the whole Lₖ as equal copies of dᵢ. Thus every dᵢ measures L, so L has every prescribed part. [7.34, D7.5, CN2, 7.37]

**Step 4.** Let M be any number having every prescribed part. Every dᵢ measures M, by 7.38. Hence L₁ measures M. Whenever Lₖ₋₁ measures M, both Lₖ₋₁ and dₖ measure M; therefore their least common measured number Lₖ also measures M, by 7.35. Applying this successively shows that L measures M. [7.38, 7.35]

**Step 5.** Since L measures M, either M equals L or M contains more than one equal copy of L and is therefore greater than L. Thus no number less than L has all the prescribed parts. Together with Step 3, this proves that L is the least number having the given parts, as required. [D7.5, CN5]
