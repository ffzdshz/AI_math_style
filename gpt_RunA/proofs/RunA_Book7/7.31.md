## Proposition 7.31
**Statement:** Any composite number is measured by some prime number.

**Step 1.** Let A be a composite number. By D7.13, A has a numerical measure B; as a proper part of A, B is less than A (D7.3, CN5). Here a numerical measure is greater than the unit, since a number is a multitude of units (D7.2).

**Step 2.** Path choice: choose the least numerical measure of A by finite inspection. Inspect the numbers consisting of two units, three units, and so on, up to the numbers less than A. This inspection is finite because A is a multitude of units (D7.2), and it encounters a measure by Step 1. Let p be the first measure encountered. Thus p measures A, and no number greater than the unit and less than p measures A.

**Step 3.** Suppose p is not prime. Then p has a numerical proper measure q, rather than being measured by the unit alone (D7.11). Consequently q is greater than the unit and less than p (D7.2, D7.3, CN5).

**Step 4.** Since p measures A, A consists of a whole number of copies of p; since q measures p, each such copy consists of a whole number of copies of q (D7.5, D7.15). Replace each copy of p in A by its equal collection of copies of q. Adding these equal collections preserves the whole (CN2), so A consists entirely of copies of q. Hence q measures A (D7.5, D7.15). This contradicts the choice of p in Step 2, since q is a number less than p.

**Step 5.** Therefore p has no numerical proper measure. The unit measures p because p is composed of units (D7.2); thus p is prime (D7.11). Since p measures A by Step 2, the arbitrary composite number A is measured by a prime number, as required.
