## Proposition 7.27
**Statement:** If two numbers be prime to one another, and each by multiplying itself make a certain number, the products will be prime to one another; and, if the original numbers by multiplying the products make certain numbers, the latter will also be prime to one another [and this is always the case with the extremes].

**Step 1.** Let A and B be the given numbers prime to one another. Write A₁ = A and B₁ = B, and define successive products by Aᵣ₊₁ = A·Aᵣ and Bᵣ₊₁ = B·Bᵣ. These are successive multiplications in the sense of D7.15. Primality to one another is symmetric, since it means having the unit alone as a common measure. [D7.15, D7.12]

**Step 2.** Path choice: use 7.24, the earlier product rule, throughout, because it applies unchanged at every successive multiplication. Initially B₁ is prime to A by hypothesis. Whenever Bᵣ is prime to A, both B and Bᵣ are prime to A; therefore their product Bᵣ₊₁ is prime to A. Starting from B₁ and repeating this inference shows that every Bₙ is prime to A. The rule also applies when the two factors are equal, as at the first multiplication. [7.24]

**Step 3.** Fix any positive index n. By Step 2 and symmetry, A is prime to Bₙ. Thus A₁ is prime to Bₙ. Whenever Aᵣ is prime to Bₙ, both A and Aᵣ are prime to Bₙ, so their product Aᵣ₊₁ is prime to Bₙ. Repeat this inference until reaching Aₙ. Consequently Aₙ and Bₙ are prime to one another. [Step 2, D7.12, 7.24]

**Step 4.** Taking n = 2, the products A₂ = A·A and B₂ = B·B are the squares of the original numbers and are prime to one another. Taking n = 3, the products A₃ = A·A₂ and B₃ = B·B₂ are their cubes and are likewise prime to one another. Since n in Step 3 was arbitrary, the corresponding successive powers—the extremes at every stage—are always prime to one another. This proves the proposition. [Step 3, D7.18, D7.19]
