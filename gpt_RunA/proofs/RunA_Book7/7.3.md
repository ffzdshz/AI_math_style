## Proposition 7.3
**Statement:** Given three numbers not prime to one another, to find their greatest common measure.

**Step 1.** Let A, B, C be the three given numbers. Since they are not prime to one another, some number E, not merely the unit, measures all three. Our path is to reduce A and B while preserving their common measures, and then apply 7.2, the immediately available two-number construction, to the reduced number and C. [D7.12]

**Step 2.** Starting with the pair A, B, whenever its entries are unequal, replace the greater by its excess over the less. This preserves exactly their common measures: a measure of both old entries measures the excess, because subtracting two collections of copies of that measure leaves a collection of such copies; conversely, a measure of the excess and the unchanged entry measures their sum, which is the old greater entry. Thus both the subtraction and its reversal preserve common measures. [D7.2, D7.5, CN3, CN2]

**Step 3.** Continue until the two entries are equal, and call their common value D. This process terminates: the sum of the entries is a finite multitude of units and strictly decreases at each subtraction, while both entries remain positive. Moreover, E continues to measure both entries by Step 2, so the final D is a number, not a unit. [D7.2, CN5; Step 2]

**Step 4.** Since D measures each of the two equal final entries once, reversing the subtractions shows that D measures A and B. Conversely, every common measure of A and B measures D, by following the subtractions forward. In particular, E measures D. These conclusions also hold immediately if A and B were equal at the outset. [D7.2, D7.5, CN2, CN3; Step 2]

**Step 5.** E measures both D and C; therefore D and C are not prime to one another. By 7.2, find their greatest common measure G. This application includes the cases in which D and C are equal or one measures the other. [D7.12, 7.2; Steps 1 and 4]

**Step 6.** G measures D, and D measures A and B. Each of A and B therefore consists of copies of D, each of which in turn consists of copies of G; consequently G measures A and B. It also measures C by its construction. Thus G is a common measure of all three given numbers. [D7.5, CN2, 7.2; Steps 4 and 5]

**Step 7.** Let H be any common measure of A, B, C. Since H measures A and B, it measures D by Step 4; it also measures C. Hence H cannot exceed G, the greatest common measure of D and C. Therefore G is the greatest common measure of A, B, C, and it has been found. [7.2; Steps 4–6]
