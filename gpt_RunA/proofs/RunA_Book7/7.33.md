## Proposition 7.33
**Statement:** Given as many numbers as we please, to find the least of those which have the same ratio with them.

**Step 1.** Let A₁, …, Aₙ be the given numbers. Choose the common-measure route: cancel one greatest common measure, then establish minimality using the two-number results. To find this measure for an arbitrary finite list, test the unit and every number not exceeding the smallest Aᵢ, retaining those which measure every Aᵢ. Each test is performed by repeated addition until the relevant Aᵢ is reached or exceeded. There are finitely many candidates, and the unit always succeeds; therefore select the greatest successful candidate, d. No greater candidate can measure the smallest entry. [D7.1, D7.2, D7.15, CN5]

**Step 2.** For each i, take Bᵢ to be the number of times d measures Aᵢ, retaining the unit if this count is one. Thus Aᵢ = dBᵢ. Simultaneous multiplication by d preserves ratios, so Bᵢ:Bⱼ = Aᵢ:Aⱼ for every pair of indices. This follows from 7.17 when the factors are numbers; cases involving the unit follow directly from the definition of numerical proportion. [D7.15, 7.17, D7.20]

**Step 3.** No number greater than the unit measures all the Bᵢ. For if e > 1 did so, write Bᵢ = eKᵢ. Grouping the repeated additions shows that Aᵢ = (de)Kᵢ; hence de would measure every Aᵢ. But de > d, since it contains more than one copy of d, contradicting the choice of d. [D7.15, CN2, CN5; Step 1]

**Step 4.** Let C₁, …, Cₙ be any other list having the same corresponding ratios as the given list. Then Bᵢ:Bⱼ = Cᵢ:Cⱼ. If some Bⱼ is the unit, these proportions say that each Cᵢ consists of Cⱼ copies of Bᵢ. Consequently Cᵢ ≥ Bᵢ for every i, and the constructed list is least in this case. [Step 2, CN1, D7.20, D7.15, CN5]

**Step 5.** It remains to consider the case in which every Bᵢ is greater than the unit. Suppose, for a contradiction, that some Cₖ < Bₖ. Alternating Bₖ:Bᵢ = Cₖ:Cᵢ gives
Bₖ:Cₖ = Bᵢ:Cᵢ
for every i. Alternation is supplied by 7.13; if a unit occurs among the Cᵢ, the same conclusion follows directly by counting the multiples specified in D7.20. [7.13, D7.20]

**Step 6.** Reduce the pair Bₖ, Cₖ by its greatest common measure g. If Cₖ is the unit, put g equal to the unit; likewise do so if the two numbers are prime to one another. Otherwise construct g by 7.2. Write Bₖ = gp and Cₖ = gq. Since Bₖ > Cₖ, we have p > q ≥ 1. Moreover, no number greater than the unit measures both p and q: such a measure h would make gh > g a common measure of Bₖ and Cₖ, exactly as in Step 3. Thus, when q is a number, p and q are prime to one another. [7.2, D7.12, D7.15, CN2, CN5; Step 3]

**Step 7.** If q is the unit, Bₖ is p times Cₖ. The proportions in Step 5 therefore make every Bᵢ p times Cᵢ. Thus p > 1 measures every Bᵢ, contradicting Step 3. [D7.20]

If q is greater than the unit, apply 7.21: p and q are the least numbers in their ratio because they are prime to one another. This is the naturally applicable least-ratio result, since coprimality has just been established. Also p:q = Bₖ:Cₖ = Bᵢ:Cᵢ, by simultaneous multiplication and Step 5. Hence 7.20 implies that p measures every Bᵢ. Again p > 1, contradicting Step 3. [7.21, 7.17, CN1, 7.20]

**Step 8.** Therefore no corresponding entry Cₖ can be smaller than Bₖ. The constructed B₁, …, Bₙ have the same ratios as the given numbers and are no greater, entry by entry, than any other list with those ratios. They are consequently the required least numbers. [Steps 2, 4–7]
