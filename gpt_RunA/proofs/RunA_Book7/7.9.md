## Proposition 7.9
**Statement:** If a number be a part of a number, and another be the same part of another, alternately also, whatever part or parts the first is of the third, the same part, or the same parts, will the second also be of the fourth.

**Step 1.** Let A be a part of B, and C the same part of D. Thus B consists of a certain finite number k of copies of A, while D consists of exactly k copies of C: this is what it means for A and C to measure B and D as the same part. [D7.2, D7.3]

**Step 2.** Consider whatever part or parts A is of C. These are the two possibilities when A is less than C. Our direct route is to add corresponding copies of A and C repeatedly, using the addition theorem appropriate to the case. [7.4]

**Step 3.** Suppose first that A is a part of C. Adding A to A and C to C gives sums having that same part-relation. Whenever sums of equally many copies have this relation, adding one further A and one further C preserves it, since the added numbers have the same part-relation as the preceding sums. Repeating this application until k copies have been included shows that B is the same part of D that A is of C. We use 7.5 because this is precisely its single-part case. [7.5]

**Step 4.** Suppose instead that A is parts of C. Adding A to A and C to C gives sums having those same parts. Whenever sums of equally many copies have this relation, adding one further A and one further C preserves it. Repeating until k copies have been included shows that B is the same parts of D that A is of C. Here 7.6 is the applicable addition theorem because the relation is one of parts. [7.6]

**Step 5.** Therefore, alternately, whatever part or parts the first number A is of the third C, the same part or the same parts the second B is of the fourth D, as required. [7.5, 7.6]
