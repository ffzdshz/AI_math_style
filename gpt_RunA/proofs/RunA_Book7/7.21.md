## Proposition 7.21
**Statement:** Numbers prime to one another are the least of those which have the same ratio with them.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be numbers prime to one another. Thus the unit alone is their common measure. We must show that A and B are the least numbers having their ratio. [D7.12]

**Step 2.** Choose the least numbers C and D having the ratio A:B, with corresponding terms in the same order. Such a least choice exists because descent through smaller numbers, which are finite multitudes of units, must terminate. [D7.2] We take the direct least-pair route: by 7.20, C measures A and D measures B the same number of times. [7.20]

**Step 3.** Suppose that C and D are not respectively equal to A and B. Their common measurement count cannot then be one, since measuring once would give C = A and D = B. Let E be the number consisting of as many units as this common count. Thus E is greater than the unit, and the unit measures E as many times as C measures A and as D measures B. [D7.2; 7.20]

**Step 4.** Apply 7.15 to the unit measuring E and C measuring A: alternately, the unit measures C as many times as E measures A. Hence E measures A. Apply 7.15 again to the unit measuring E and D measuring B: the unit measures D as many times as E measures B. Hence E also measures B. This uses 7.15 directly because its hypothesis is exactly equality of measurement counts. [7.15]

**Step 5.** Therefore E, which is greater than the unit, is a common measure of A and B, contrary to their being prime to one another. Consequently C = A and D = B. Since C and D were chosen least in this ratio, A and B are the least numbers having the same ratio with them. [D7.12; Steps 2–4]
