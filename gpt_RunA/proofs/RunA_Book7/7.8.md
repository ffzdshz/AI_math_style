## Proposition 7.8
**Statement:** If a number be the same parts of a number that a number subtracted is of a number subtracted, the remainder will also be the same parts of the remainder that the whole is of the whole.

**Step 1.** Let A be the same parts of B that C is of D, with C subtracted from A and D from B. Write E = A − C and F = B − D. Thus C < A and D < B; moreover, A < B and A does not measure B, since A is parts of B. [Hypothesis; D7.4]

**Step 2.** Express the same-parts hypothesis by its equal measuring pieces: there are positive quantities u and v, each a number or a unit, and the same counts r < s, such that A consists of r copies of u and B of s copies of u, while C consists of r copies of v and D of s copies of v. This says precisely that each smaller number comprises the same number of equal parts of its corresponding greater number. We use these decompositions and subtract corresponding pieces directly; this is the direct common-notion route, without introducing proportionality theorems. [Hypothesis; D7.1; D7.2; D7.3; D7.4; CN3]

**Step 3.** Since A > C and both contain r corresponding pieces, u > v. For if u = v, repeated addition would give A = C; if u < v, the r copies of u would form a proper part of the r copies of v, giving A < C. Both contradict A > C. Let w be the positive remainder after v is removed from u. [CN2; CN5; D7.2]

**Step 4.** Remove one copy of v from each of the r copies of u composing A. The total removed is C, and each remaining piece is w; consequently E consists of r copies of w. Likewise, removing one copy of v from each of the s copies of u composing B removes exactly D and leaves F consisting of s copies of w. These equalities follow by adding the corresponding equal pieces and subtracting equal totals. [CN2; CN3]

**Step 5.** Thus w measures F exactly s times, and E comprises r of these equal measuring pieces. Since r < s, E < F. The pieces composing E therefore have exactly the same count and the same measuring relation to F as the pieces composing A have to B. [D7.2; D7.3; CN5]

**Step 6.** To verify that E is parts, rather than a single measuring part, of F, suppose E measured F exactly k times. By Step 4, F would then consist both of s copies and of kr copies of the positive quantity w. These counts must be equal: otherwise the collection with fewer copies would be a proper part of the other and could not equal it. Hence s = kr. But B, consisting of s copies of u, would consequently consist of k copies of A, each containing r copies of u. This would make A measure B, contrary to Step 1. Therefore E does not measure F; and, by Step 5, E is the same parts of F that A is of B. The remainder is therefore the same parts of the remainder that the whole is of the whole. [D7.2; D7.3; D7.4; CN2; CN5]
