## Proposition 7.12
**Statement:** If there be as many numbers as we please in proportion, then, as one of the antecedents is to one of the consequents, so are all the antecedents to all the consequents.

**Step 1.** Let the given proportional pairs be A₁:B₁, A₂:B₂, …, Aₙ:Bₙ, and put S = A₁ + ⋯ + Aₙ and T = B₁ + ⋯ + Bₙ. By D7.20, the antecedents have throughout the same numerical relation to their corresponding consequents: the same multiple, the same part, or the same parts. We use these numerical descriptions directly, choosing the addition rule matching each case rather than passing to ratios of magnitudes. [D7.20]

**Step 2.** Suppose each Aᵢ is the same part of Bᵢ. By 7.5, A₁ + A₂ is that same part of B₁ + B₂. Applying 7.5 again to these sums and the next pair preserves the same part; continuing through the finite list shows that S is the same part of T that any Aᵢ is of Bᵢ. [7.5]

**Step 3.** Suppose instead that each Aᵢ is the same parts of Bᵢ. Proposition 7.6 gives that A₁ + A₂ is those same parts of B₁ + B₂. Repeating this application with each successive pair shows that S is the same parts of T that any Aᵢ is of Bᵢ. This is precisely the parts case, so 7.6 is the corresponding addition rule. [7.6]

**Step 4.** Suppose each Aᵢ is the same m-fold multiple of Bᵢ. Write each Aᵢ as the sum of m copies of Bᵢ. Adding these equal decompositions and grouping corresponding copies gives S as the sum of m copies of B₁ + ⋯ + Bₙ, that is, of T. Hence S is the same m-fold multiple of T. If the common relation is equality, then Aᵢ = Bᵢ for every i, and adding these equalities gives S = T. [D7.15; CN2; D7.20]

**Step 5.** Thus, in every case, S has to T the same numerical relation that any chosen antecedent Aᵢ has to its corresponding consequent Bᵢ. By the definition of numerical proportion, Aᵢ:Bᵢ = S:T. Therefore, as one of the antecedents is to its consequent, so are all the antecedents together to all the consequents together. [D7.20]
