## Proposition 7.28
**Statement:** If two numbers be prime to one another, the sum will also be prime to each of them; and, if the sum of two numbers be prime to any one of them, the original numbers will also be prime to one another.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be the two numbers, and let S = A + B. Path choice: use the definition of numbers prime to one another directly, excluding any common measure other than the unit, rather than passing to ratios (D7.12).

**Step 2.** Suppose A and B are prime to one another. Let X be either of them and Y the other, so that S = X + Y. Suppose S and X are not prime to one another. Then some number D other than the unit measures both S and X (D7.12). Thus S and X each consist of whole copies of D, allowing a single copy when the measured number equals D (D7.2, D7.5). Since S is greater than X (CN5), remove from the copies constituting S as many copies as constitute X. The remaining copies constitute S − X = Y (CN3). Consequently D measures Y as well as X, contradicting that A and B are prime to one another (D7.12). Therefore S is prime to X; since X was either summand, S is prime to each of A and B (D7.12).

**Step 3.** Conversely, suppose S is prime to one summand, which we call X, and call the other summand Y. If X and Y were not prime to one another, some number D other than the unit would measure both (D7.12). Each would therefore consist of whole copies of D (D7.2, D7.5). Adjoining the copies constituting X to those constituting Y gives whole copies of D constituting X + Y = S (CN2). Hence D would measure both S and X, contrary to their being prime to one another (D7.12). Thus the original numbers A and B are prime to one another.

**Step 4.** Therefore, if two numbers are prime to one another, their sum is prime to each; and if their sum is prime to either one, the original numbers are prime to one another, as required (D7.12).
