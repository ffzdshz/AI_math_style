## Proposition 7.24
**Statement:** If two numbers be prime to any number, their product also will be prime to the same.

**Step 1.** Let A and B each be prime to C, and let P be the product AB. Suppose, for contradiction, that P and C are not prime to one another. Then a number d, not merely the unit, measures both P and C. [D7.15, D7.12]

**Step 2.** Since C and A are prime to one another and d measures C, d and A are prime to one another. The direct path is to apply 7.23 first, because the supposed common measure is already a divisor of C. [7.23]

**Step 3.** The number A measures P, by the definition of multiplication and commutativity of multiplication. [D7.15, 7.16] Therefore d cannot equal P: otherwise A would measure both A and d, contrary to their being prime to one another. [D7.12] Since d measures P but is not equal to P, the multitude of repetitions of d making P is a number e, rather than the unit. Thus de = P. [D7.2, D7.15, 7.16]

**Step 4.** The products AB and de are equal, since both equal P. [CN1] Apply the converse of 7.19 directly to these equal products, with the four numbers ordered as A, d, e, B. It follows that A:d = e:B. This converse is the natural choice because the equality of cross-products is already established. [7.19]

**Step 5.** Since A and d are prime to one another, they are the least numbers having their ratio. [7.21] Consequently, in the proportion A:d = e:B, they measure the corresponding numbers e and B respectively. In particular, d measures B. [7.20]

**Step 6.** Thus the number d measures both B and C, contradicting the hypothesis that B and C are prime to one another. Hence P and C have no common measure other than the unit: the product AB is prime to C, as required. [D7.12]
