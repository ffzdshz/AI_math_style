## Proposition 7.2
**Statement:** Given two numbers not prime to one another, to find their greatest common measure.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be the given numbers, not prime to one another, and arrange them so that A is not less than B. If A = B, take B. If A > B and B measures A, again take B. In either case B measures both numbers, and no number greater than B can measure B: a measure occurs as one or more whole copies within what it measures. Thus B is the greatest common measure in these cases. [D7.2, D7.3, CN5]

**Step 2.** Suppose, then, that A > B and B does not measure A. Path choice: use successive subtraction, the procedure addressed directly by the immediately preceding proposition, 7.1. Starting with the pair (A, B), proceed as follows. For a current pair (x, y), with x > y, stop if y measures x. Otherwise subtract y repeatedly from x until a positive remainder z less than y is left. Record that x consists of the removed copies of y together with z, and continue with the pair (y, z). Each such subtraction is possible by removing the corresponding units; since y does not measure x, the final remainder is not empty. [7.1, D7.2, D7.3, CN3, CN5]

**Step 3.** This procedure must terminate. Whenever it continues, the lesser member strictly decreases, losing at least one unit; there cannot be more such decreases than there are units in B. Nor can the procedure first terminate with an unit: if no preceding remainder measures its predecessor until an unit is left, 7.1 says that A and B are prime to one another, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence it terminates with a number d, not an unit, which measures the preceding number C. [D7.2, CN5, 7.1, D7.12]

**Step 4.** The number d measures both original numbers. At the terminal pair it measures C and itself. Now retrace the recorded stages. At any stage, the earlier greater number x consists of some whole copies of y together with the remainder z. If d measures y and z, replace each of those copies of y, and z itself, by their constituent copies of d. Adding these copies shows that d measures x also. Applying this reasoning successively backwards from (C, d) proves that d measures A and B. [D7.2, D7.3, CN2]

**Step 5.** Let e be any number measuring both A and B. Follow the same subtraction procedure forwards. Whenever e measures x and y, both consist of whole copies of e. Subtracting y from x removes exactly the copies of e constituting y, so the remainder also consists of whole copies of e. Consequently e continues to measure every remainder throughout the procedure. In particular, e measures the terminal number d. [D7.2, D7.3, CN3]

**Step 6.** Since e measures d, it cannot be greater than d: one copy equals d, or several copies compose d and each is less than the whole. Thus every common numerical measure of A and B is at most d. By Step 4, d is itself a common measure, so d is their greatest common measure. The prescribed finite procedure therefore finds the required greatest common measure. [D7.3, CN5]
