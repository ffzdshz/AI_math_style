## Proposition 3.2
**Statement:** If on the circumference of a circle two points be taken at random, the straight line joining the points will fall within the circle.

**Step 1.** Let A and B be distinct points on the circumference. Draw AB, find the centre O, and join OA and OB. By the definition of a circle, O is inside the circle and OA = OB. [P1, 3.1, D1.15, D1.16]

**Step 2.** Take any point X strictly between A and B. If X = O, it is already inside the circle; otherwise join OX. First consider the case in which O, A, B are collinear. Since OA = OB, O must lie between A and B: otherwise one of these equal segments would be a proper part of the other. Thus X lies between O and one of the endpoints, and OX is shorter than the corresponding radius. Consequently OX < OA. [P1, D1.15, CN5]

**Step 3.** Now suppose O is not on AB. Triangle OAB is isosceles, so its base angles are equal. Since X lies between A and B, this gives ∠OAX = ∠OBX. [D1.15, 1.5]

**Step 4.** In triangle OAX, the side AX continues through X towards B. Therefore the exterior angle ∠OXB is greater than the opposite interior angle ∠OAX, and hence greater than ∠OBX. We choose the exterior-angle inequality as the natural first route because only a strict comparison, not an angle-sum calculation, is needed. [1.16, 1.5]

**Step 5.** In triangle OXB, the greater angle ∠OXB is subtended by the greater side OB. Thus OB > OX; since OB = OA, we again have OX < OA. [1.19, D1.15]

**Step 6.** This strict inequality places X inside the circle. Indeed, if X were on or outside the circumference, the segment from the interior point O to X would meet the circumference at some point Y at or before X. Then OY ≤ OX, whereas the definition of the circle gives OY = OA > OX, a contradiction. Thus every point strictly between A and B is inside the circle, while A and B themselves are on its circumference. Therefore the straight line joining the two points falls within the circle. [D1.15, D1.16, CN5]
