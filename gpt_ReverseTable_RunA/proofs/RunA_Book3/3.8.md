## Proposition 3.8
**Statement:** If a point be taken outside a circle and from the point straight lines be drawn through to the circle, one of which is through the centre and the others are drawn at random, then, of the straight lines which fall on the concave circumference, that through the centre is greatest, while of the rest   the nearer to that through the centre is always greater than the more remote, but, of the straight lines falling on the convex circumference, that between the point and the diameter is least, while of the rest the nearer to the least is always less than the more remote, and only two equal straight lines will fall on the circle from the point, one on each side of the least.

**Step 1.** Let P be the point outside the circle. Find its centre O, and draw and extend PO to meet the circumference first at A and afterwards at B. Thus P, A, O, B occur in that order, and AB is a diameter. Draw the joins required below. All radii of the circle are equal. For any other secant ray from P, call its first intersection the convex, or near, endpoint and its second intersection the concave, or far, endpoint. On either side of PO, the nearer ray means the one making the smaller angle with PO. [3.1, P1, P2, D1.17, D1.15]

**Step 2.** Let X be any point of the circumference not on PO. In triangle POX, the triangle inequality gives
PX < PO + OX = PO + OB = PB.
It also gives
PO < PX + OX.
But PO = PA + AO and AO = OX; removing the equal radii therefore gives PA < PX. Moreover, PA < PB because PA is a proper part of PB. Consequently PB is uniquely greatest and PA uniquely least among the segments from P to the circumference. [1.20, D1.15, CN3, CN5]

**Step 3.** Consider two nonaxial secant rays on the same side of PO, with PC nearer to PO than PD, and let C and D be their far endpoints. Since PC lies inside angle OPD, it meets OD at a point K between O and D. The point K is inside the circle, so the far endpoint C lies beyond K on the ray from P. Hence OC lies between OB and OD, and angle POC contains angle POD as a proper part. Thus angle POC is greater than angle POD. In triangles POC and POD, PO is common and OC = OD. Apply I.24 directly: its equal-side hypotheses are already supplied by the common side and the radii, and the greater included angle gives PC > PD. This proves the required ordering of the concave endpoints on either side of PO. [D1.15, CN5, 1.24]

**Step 4.** Let E and F be the near endpoints of those same rays, respectively. The ray PE lies inside angle OPF and meets OF at a point L between O and F. Since L is inside the circle and E is the first intersection with the circumference, E lies between P and L. Thus E is strictly inside triangle POF. Use I.21 here, since its nested-triangle configuration occurs directly: the two segments PE and EO, drawn from the ends of side PO to the interior point E, satisfy
PE + EO < PF + FO.
Since EO = FO, cancellation gives PE < PF. Therefore, among the convex endpoints on either side, the nearer ray to PA gives the smaller segment. [D1.15, 1.21, CN3, CN5]

**Step 5.** Let X be any nonaxial point of the circumference. At O, on the opposite side of the line PO, construct a ray OY such that angle POY equals angle POX, and let Y be its intersection with the circumference. The triangles POX and POY have PO common, OX = OY, and their included angles equal. Hence PX = PY, and angle OPX equals angle OPY. Thus every nonaxial segment has an equal mate on the other side of PA, equally inclined to it. Applying this construction to both intersections of a secant places their mates on the same opposite ray with their respective distances from P unchanged; the near endpoint therefore remains near, and the far endpoint remains far. Consequently, if two rays being compared lie on opposite sides of PA, replace one by its equal mate and apply Step 3 or Step 4 on a single side. The required strict orderings therefore hold without restriction to one side. [1.23, D1.15, 1.4, CN5]

**Step 6.** For uniqueness, use I.7, the direct same-side criterion. If distinct circumference points X and Z on the same side of PO satisfied PX = PZ, then also OX = OZ, since both are radii. On the fixed base PO there would therefore be two distinct vertices on the same side with both corresponding sides equal, contrary to I.7. Thus an equal length can occur at most once on each side. Step 5 supplies exactly one equal mate on the opposite side for every nonaxial segment, while Step 2 excludes any mate for either axial extreme. Hence no three segments from P to the circle are equal: each nonaxial length occurs exactly twice, once on each side of the least segment PA. Together with Steps 2–5, this proves all the assertions. [D1.15, 1.7]
