## Proposition 3.10
**Statement:** A circle does not cut a circle at more points than two.

**Step 1.** Suppose, for a contradiction, that two circles cut one another at three distinct points A, B, C. Let their centres be O and P. Since the circles cut one another, O ≠ P. Their radii give OA = OB = OC and PA = PB = PC. [D1.15, D1.16, 3.5]

**Step 2.** Join O to P and each centre to A, B, C, and produce OP in both directions. We use I.7 directly: the equal radii supply precisely its two pairs of equal sides on the common base OP. We therefore seek two common points strictly on the same side of this line. [P1, P2, 1.7]

**Step 3.** The points A, B, C are not collinear. Otherwise one would lie between the other two; but the segment joining those other two points lies within either circle, so its intermediate point cannot also lie on the circumference. [3.2]

**Step 4.** None of A, B, C lies on the line OP. For suppose one of them, X, did. By Step 3, another, Y, lies off OP. Also X differs from both centres, since each centre lies within its own circle. Of the three distinct collinear points O, P, X, one lies between the other two; consequently one of the lengths OP, OX, PX equals the sum of the other two. But OX = OY and PX = PY, since X and Y belong to both circumferences. Thus one side of the nondegenerate triangle OPY equals the sum of its other two sides, contrary to I.20. [D1.15, D1.16, 1.20]

**Step 5.** Since A, B, C all lie off OP, two of them must lie on the same side of it; call them A and B. On the same base OP and the same side of it, the segments OA, PA meet at A, while OB, PB meet at the distinct point B, with OA = OB and PA = PB. This is impossible by I.7. The supposition is therefore false: **a circle does not cut a circle at more points than two.** [1.7]
