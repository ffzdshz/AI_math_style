## Proposition 3.16
**Statement:** The straight line drawn at right angles to the diameter of a circle from its extremity will fall outside the circle, and into the space between the straight line and the circumference another straight line cannot be interposed; further the angle of the semicircle is greater, and the remaining angle less, than any acute rectilineal angle.

**Step 1.** Let AB be a diameter of the circle, O its centre, and let the straight line through B perpendicular to AB have ray BT on the side of a chosen semicircle. Thus ∠OBT is right. The angle of this semicircle at B is the curvilinear angle between BA and its circumference; the remaining angle is between that circumference and BT. [D1.17, D1.18, D1.10, D3.7]

**Step 2.** Suppose the perpendicular met the circumference again at C, and join OC. Since OB = OC, the angles OBC and BCO are equal. The equal radii make the isosceles-triangle theorem the immediate route here. But ∠OBC is right, so both these angles would be right, contrary to the theorem that any two angles of a triangle are together less than two right angles. Thus the perpendicular has no second point on the circumference. Nor can it enter the circle: if it did, its continuation would meet the circumference again. This applies to both rays of the perpendicular, which therefore lies outside the circle except at B. [P1, P2, D1.15, D1.10, 1.5, 1.17]

**Step 3.** Consider any ray r from B strictly between BO and BT. Draw OF perpendicular to the straight line containing r. Its foot F lies on r: if F lay on the opposite ray, the angle OBF would be greater than a right angle, which, together with the right angle at F, would contradict the two-angle bound for a triangle. In the right-angled triangle OBF, ∠BOF is less than a right angle. Therefore the side OB opposite the right angle is greater than OF. Consequently F is inside the circle. Indeed, if F were on or outside it, OF would meet the circumference at G on or before F, giving OF ≥ OG = OB, a contradiction. [1.12, D1.10, D1.12, 1.17, 1.19, D1.15, CN5]

**Step 4.** Continue r through the interior point F until it meets the circumference at C. The chord BC lies within the circle. Hence every ray strictly between BO and BT has an initial portion inside the chosen semicircle. Any straight line interposed at B between BT and the circumference would have to follow such a ray while remaining outside the circle, which is impossible. No straight line can therefore be interposed in that space. [P2, 3.2; Step 3]

**Step 5.** Let α be any acute rectilineal angle. At B construct ∠OBR equal to α, toward BT. Because α is less than a right angle, BR lies strictly between BO and BT. By Step 4, its initial portion lies inside the semicircle. Thus ∠OBR is a proper part of the curvilinear angle between BA and the circumference. The angle of the semicircle is therefore greater than α, and hence greater than every acute rectilineal angle. [1.23, D1.12, D3.7, CN5; Step 4]

**Step 6.** Again let α be any acute rectilineal angle, and construct ∠TBS equal to α, toward BO. The ray BS also lies strictly between BT and BO and initially lies inside the semicircle. The circumference therefore lies, near B, between BT and BS, so the remaining curvilinear angle between BT and the circumference is a proper part of ∠TBS. It is consequently less than α, and hence less than every acute rectilineal angle. Together with Steps 2 and 4, this proves all the assertions. [1.23, D1.12, CN5; Step 4]
