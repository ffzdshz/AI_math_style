## Proposition 3.7
**Statement:** If on the diameter of a circle a point be taken which is not the centre of the circle, and from the point straight lines fall upon the circle, that will be greatest on which the centre is, the remainder of the same diameter will be least, and of the rest  the nearer to the straight line through the centre is always greater than the more remote, and only two equal straight lines will fall from the point on the circle, one on each side of the least straight line.

**Step 1.** Let AB be the diameter, O the centre, and P the given point distinct from O. Label the endpoints so that A, P, O, B occur in this order. Thus PB passes through the centre, and PA is the remainder of the diameter. Join P and O to the points of the circumference as needed. All straight lines from O to the circumference are equal. [D1.17, P1, D1.15]

**Step 2.** Let X be any point of the circumference other than A and B. In triangle POX, the sum PO + OX is greater than PX. But OX = OB, and PO + OB = PB. Therefore PB > PX. This proves the required comparison with the line through the centre directly by the triangle inequality. [1.20, D1.15]

**Step 3.** In the same triangle, PO + PX > OX. Since OX = OA = OP + PA, we have PO + PX > OP + PA. Removing the common length OP gives PX > PA. Consequently every intermediate line is greater than PA and less than PB; in particular, PB is greatest and PA least. [1.20, D1.15, CN3]

**Step 4.** First compare two lines on the same side of AB. Let PX be nearer to PB than PY, so that their endpoints occur in the order B, X, Y, A along that semicircle. Then OY lies inside angle POX, and therefore ∠POX > ∠POY, the whole angle being greater than its part. In triangles POX and POY, OP is common and OX = OY. Hence PX > PY by the included-angle comparison. This is the natural direct comparison here, because the two triangles already have two corresponding sides equal. Thus, on either side, the nearer line to PB is greater than the more remote. [CN5, D1.15, 1.24]

**Step 5.** For any X other than A and B, construct at O, on the opposite side of AB, an angle POY equal to angle POX, producing its second side to meet the circumference at Y. Now OP is common, OX = OY, and the included angles POX and POY are equal. Therefore triangles POX and POY are equal by side-angle-side: in particular, PX = PY and ∠OPX = ∠OPY. Since PO and PB have the same direction from P, these two equal lines have equal angular departures from PB, on opposite sides of the diameter—and hence one on each side of PA. [1.23, P2, D1.15, 1.4]

**Step 6.** The nearer-greater comparison also holds for lines on opposite sides. Indeed, replace one of them by its equal counterpart from Step 5 on the side of the other. This replacement preserves both its length and its angular departure from PB. The comparison is then exactly the same-side comparison of Step 4. [1.4, 1.24]

**Step 7.** There cannot be a third line equal to PX. Any other line on X's side is either nearer to PB or more remote than PX, and so is unequal to PX by Step 4. Any other line on Y's side is similarly unequal to PY, and hence to PX. The two diameter portions are excluded by the strict inequalities of Steps 2 and 3. Thus every length attained by an intermediate line is attained by exactly two lines, one on each side of PA. Together with the preceding comparisons, this proves that the line through the centre is greatest, the remaining diameter portion is least, and among all the others the nearer to the greatest is always greater than the more remote. [1.20, 1.24, 1.4]
