## Proposition 3.6
**Statement:** If two circles touch one another, they will not have the same centre.

**Step 1.** Let two distinct circles Γ and Δ touch at A. By the definition of touching circles, A belongs to both circumferences. We use the defining equality of radii directly; this route requires no distinction between internal and external contact. [D3.3]

**Step 2.** Suppose, for a contradiction, that both circles have the same centre O. Join OA. Since O is the centre of each circle, it lies inside each, and OA is a radius of both. [D1.15, D1.16, P1]

**Step 3.** Since the circles are distinct, choose a point B on one circumference but not on the other, calling the former Γ and the latter Δ. Draw OB and produce it as necessary. The ray from O through B meets the circumference of Δ at some point C: it starts inside the figure bounded by that circumference and meets its boundary on passing out. Since B is not on Δ, B and C are distinct. [D1.15, P1, P2]

**Step 4.** In Γ, the radii OB and OA are equal. In Δ, the radii OC and OA are equal. Therefore OB = OC, since things equal to the same thing are equal to one another. [D1.15, CN1]

**Step 5.** But B and C are distinct points on the same ray from O. Thus either OB is a proper part of OC, or OC is a proper part of OB. In either case the whole is greater than the part, contradicting OB = OC. Hence the supposed common centre cannot exist: if two circles touch one another, they will not have the same centre. [CN5]
