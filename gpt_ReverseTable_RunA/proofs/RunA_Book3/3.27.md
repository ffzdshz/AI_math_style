## Proposition 3.27
**Statement:** In equal circles angles standing on equal circumferences are equal to one another, whether they stand at the centres or at the circumferences.

**Step 1.** Let two equal circles have equal circumferences (arcs) AB and CD. Choose any point X on the remaining circumference of the first circle and any point Y on the remaining circumference of the second. Join XA, XB, YC, and YD, so that ∠AXB and ∠CYD stand on the given arcs. [P1] We prove equality at the circumferences first, using the immediately preceding proposition III.26; III.20 will then give equality at the centres by doubling.

**Step 2.** Suppose ∠AXB and ∠CYD are unequal. Interchanging the circles if necessary, let ∠AXB be the smaller. At Y, on YC and toward YD, construct ∠CYE equal to ∠AXB, and produce its second side to meet the circle again at E. Since the constructed angle is smaller than ∠CYD, its second side lies strictly inside that angle; hence E lies strictly within the specified arc CD, and arc CE is a proper part of arc CD. [1.23, P2]

**Step 3.** The equal angles ∠AXB and ∠CYE stand in equal circles, so the arcs AB and CE on which they stand are equal. [3.26] But arc AB equals arc CD by hypothesis; therefore arc CE equals arc CD. [CN1] This is impossible, because arc CE is a proper part of arc CD, and the whole is greater than the part. [CN5] Thus ∠AXB = ∠CYD. Since X and Y were arbitrary points on the remaining circumferences, the required equality holds for angles at the circumferences.

**Step 4.** Find the centres O and P of the two circles, and join OA, OB, PC, and PD. [3.1, P1] The angle at O standing on arc AB is double ∠AXB, and the angle at P standing on arc CD is double ∠CYD. [3.20] Since ∠AXB = ∠CYD, adding each angle to itself gives equal doubles. [CN2] Consequently the corresponding angles at the centres are also equal. Therefore, in equal circles, angles standing on equal circumferences are equal to one another, whether they stand at the centres or at the circumferences.
