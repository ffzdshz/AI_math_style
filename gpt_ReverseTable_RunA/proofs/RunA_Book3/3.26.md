## Proposition 3.26
**Statement:** In equal circles equal angles stand on equal circumferences, whether they stand at the centres or at the circumferences.

**Step 1.** Let two equal circles have centres O and P, and let α and β be the circumferences cut off between endpoints A, B and C, D respectively. Choose X and Y on the complementary circumferences; if the given angles are at the circumferences, use their vertices as X and Y. Join the chords, radii, and sides of the angles AXB and CYD. These inscribed angles stand on α and β respectively. [P1; D3.8; D3.9]

**Step 2.** By III.20, the central angles standing on α and β are respectively double ∠AXB and ∠CYD. Consequently, whether the given equal angles are central or inscribed, both corresponding pairs are equal. Indeed, equal magnitudes have equal doubles, and equal doubles have equal halves: an inequality between the halves would persist upon doubling. [3.20; CN2; CN5]

**Step 3.** Since the circles are equal, OA = PC and OB = PD. The ordinary included angles between these pairs of radii are also equal. For minor intercepted circumferences they are the central angles already compared; for major intercepted circumferences they are the remainders after subtracting those equal central angles from a full turn. Thus, by I.4, AB = CD. If the endpoints are diametrically opposite, the triangle argument is unnecessary: AB and CD are equal diameters directly. [D3.1; CN3; 1.4]

**Step 4.** Consider the segments bounded by AB and the circumference AXB, and by CD and the circumference CYD. Since ∠AXB = ∠CYD, these segments are similar. Their bases AB and CD are equal, so III.24 makes the segments equal, and hence their corresponding curved boundaries AXB and CYD are equal. This is the direct equal-base segment route: III.24 applies precisely to the similarity and equal bases just established. [D3.6; D3.11; 3.24]

**Step 5.** The whole circumferences of the given equal circles are equal. Subtract from them the equal complementary circumferences AXB and CYD. The remaining circumferences α and β are therefore equal. Thus, in equal circles, equal angles stand on equal circumferences, whether the angles are at the centres or at the circumferences. [D3.1; CN3]
