## Proposition 6.6
**Statement:** If two triangles have one angle equal to one angle and the sides about the equal angles proportional, the triangles will be equiangular and will have those angles equal which the corresponding sides subtend.

**Step 1.** Let ABC and DEF be triangles such that ∠BAC = ∠EDF and AB:AC = DE:DF. At E, on ED, construct a ray in the half-plane containing F which makes with ED an angle equal to ∠ABC. [1.23]

**Step 2.** The angles ∠BAC and ∠ABC together are less than two right angles, since the three angles of triangle ABC equal two right angles and the whole is greater than its part. Thus the interior angles made by DE with the constructed ray and with DF are together less than two right angles. These rays therefore meet on the side of DE containing F; call their intersection G. Consequently ∠EDG = ∠BAC and ∠DEG = ∠ABC. [1.32, CN5, P5]

**Step 3.** The angle sums of triangles ABC and DEG each equal two right angles. Subtracting their respective equal angles gives ∠DGE = ∠ACB, so these triangles are equiangular. The direct route is now to apply the proportionality theorem for equiangular triangles, since it immediately supplies the needed comparison:
AB:AC = DE:DG. [1.32, CN3, 6.4]

**Step 4.** By hypothesis AB:AC = DE:DF, while Step 3 gives AB:AC = DE:DG. Ratios equal to the same ratio are equal to one another; hence DE:DG = DE:DF. Magnitudes to which the same magnitude has the same ratio are equal, so DG = DF. [5.11, 5.9]

**Step 5.** In triangles DEG and DEF, DE is common, DG = DF, and ∠EDG = ∠EDF because DG and DF lie on the same ray. By side–angle–side, ∠DEG = ∠DEF and ∠DGE = ∠DFE. Combining these equalities with those already established gives ∠ABC = ∠DEF and ∠ACB = ∠DFE. Together with the given ∠BAC = ∠EDF, this proves that ABC and DEF are equiangular. Their equal angles are precisely those subtended by the corresponding sides: BC and EF, AC and DF, and AB and DE, respectively. [1.4, CN1]
