## Proposition 12.14
**Statement:** Cones and cylinders which are on equal bases are to one another as their heights.

**Step 1.** Let X and Y be cylinders on equal bases B and B′, with heights h and k respectively. Choose the parallel-cut route, since it exhibits both prescribed heights directly without a comparison of their sizes. Continue X coaxially beyond its upper base through the additional height k, obtaining one cylinder T. The plane of the former upper base divides T into X and an additional cylinder Z. By the rectangular generation of a cylinder, Z has a base equal to B, and the axes of X and Z have lengths h and k respectively. [D11.21, D11.22, D11.23]

**Step 2.** The cylinders Z and Y have the same height k. Therefore, by the equal-height theorem,
Z:Y = B:B′.
But B = B′. In the definition of equal ratios, take each multiplier to be one: since the third and fourth magnitudes are equal, the first and second must also be equal. Hence Z = Y. [12.11, D5.5]

**Step 3.** The plane dividing T into X and Z is parallel to its opposite base planes. Thus the parallel-cut theorem gives
X:Z = h:k.
Since Z = Y, equal magnitudes have the same ratio to them from X; consequently
X:Y = X:Z = h:k.
The proposition is therefore established for cylinders. [12.13, 5.7, 5.11]

**Step 4.** Now let K and L be cones on equal bases, with heights h and k. Take cylinders X and Y having respectively the same bases and heights as these cones. Each cone is a third part of its corresponding cylinder, so X and Y are respectively the triple multiples of K and L. Parts have the same ratio as their corresponding equal multiples; hence
K:L = X:Y.
By Step 3, X:Y = h:k, and therefore K:L = h:k. Thus cones to cones, and cylinders to cylinders, on equal bases are to one another as their heights. [12.10, 5.15, 5.11]
