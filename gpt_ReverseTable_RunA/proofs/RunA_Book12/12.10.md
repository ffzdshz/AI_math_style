## Proposition 12.10
**Statement:** Any cone is a third part of the cylinder which has the same base with it and equal height.

**Step 1.** Let K be the cone, with vertex V, circular base D, and height h; let C be the cylinder with the same base and height. We also use K and C for their magnitudes. For a convex polygon Q in the base plane, write P(Q) for the pyramid with vertex V and base Q, and R(Q) for the prism erected perpendicularly on Q to height h. If Q lies inside D, then P(Q) lies inside K and R(Q) inside C. We take the direct route through these polygonal solids and exhaustion. [D11.18, D11.21]

**Step 2.** For every such polygon Q,
R(Q) = 3P(Q).
Indeed, divide Q into triangles and divide its prism and pyramid accordingly. By XII.7, each triangular prism is three times a pyramid on its triangular base with a vertex in the opposite base. By XII.5, replacing that vertex by V does not change the pyramid’s magnitude, since its base and height remain unchanged. Adding over the triangles gives the asserted identity. XII.5 is the natural comparison here because the individual bases are triangular. [12.7, 12.5, CN2]

**Step 3.** Inscribe a square Q₀ in D. Refine its sides, and subsequently every side of each resulting polygon, as follows. For a side AB, bisect AB at N. If O is the centre of D, extend ON to meet the minor arc AB at M. Since ON is perpendicular to AB, the line through M parallel to AB is tangent to the circle. Erect a rectangle on AB whose opposite side lies on this tangent and whose other sides are parallel to OM. The circular segment between AB and its minor arc lies properly inside this rectangle, while triangle ABM lies inside the segment. Replace AB by AM and MB. Repeating this construction produces nested inscribed polygons Q₀, Q₁, Q₂, … . [4.6, 1.10, 3.3, 1.31, 3.16]

**Step 4.** For each side AB in this construction, triangle ABM is half the enclosing rectangle, because they have the same base and lie between the same parallels. Consequently, by XII.6, the pyramid with vertex V and base ABM is half the pyramid with vertex V and that rectangular base. Here XII.6 directly applies because the comparison includes a rectangular base. By Step 2, the corresponding triangular prism is likewise half the rectangular prism. [1.41, 12.6, CN2]

The curved portion of K over the circular segment is properly contained in the rectangular pyramid. Therefore the triangular pyramid added in the refinement is greater than half that curved portion. Likewise, the curved portion of C over the segment is properly contained in the rectangular prism, so the triangular prism added is greater than half that portion. Adding these inequalities over all sides shows that each refinement removes more than half the total remainder, both in the cone and in the cylinder. [CN2, CN5]

**Step 5.** Put Pₙ = P(Qₙ) and Rₙ = R(Qₙ). Thus Rₙ = 3Pₙ, and the positive remainders satisfy
K − Pₙ₊₁ < ½(K − Pₙ),
C − Rₙ₊₁ < ½(C − Rₙ).
By X.1, each sequence of remainders can be made smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. The same holds for 3(K − Pₙ), since tripling preserves the more-than-half removal at every refinement. [10.1, CN2, CN3]

**Step 6.** Suppose C > 3K. By Step 5, choose n such that
C − Rₙ < C − 3K.
Subtracting gives Rₙ > 3K. But Pₙ is contained in K, so Step 2 gives Rₙ = 3Pₙ < 3K, a contradiction. Hence C is not greater than 3K. [CN2, CN3, CN5]

**Step 7.** Suppose C < 3K. By Step 5, choose n such that
3(K − Pₙ) < 3K − C.
Subtracting gives 3Pₙ > C. But Rₙ is contained in C, and Step 2 gives 3Pₙ = Rₙ < C, again a contradiction. Hence C is not less than 3K either. Therefore C = 3K: any cone is a third part of the cylinder having the same base and equal height. [CN2, CN3, CN5]
