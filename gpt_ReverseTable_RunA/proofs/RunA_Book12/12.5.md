## Proposition 12.5
**Statement:** Pyramids which are of the same height and have triangular bases are to one another as the bases.

**Step 1.** Let P and Q be pyramids of the same height with triangular bases A and B, respectively; letters also denote their magnitudes. We use the direct prism comparison of XII.4 and exhaustion, verifying the desired proportion by the equimultiple definition rather than assuming an auxiliary fourth proportional solid. Apply XII.3 to both pyramids, and repeatedly apply the same subdivision to every remaining pyramid, proceeding to the same depth on both sides. Let X_k and Y_k be the total prisms removed from P and Q after k stages. The collections contain equally many corresponding prisms, so XII.4 gives
A:B = X_k:Y_k
at every stage. The prism totals strictly increase and remain proper parts of their respective pyramids. [12.3, 12.4, CN5, D5.5]

**Step 2.** Write R_k = P − X_k and S_k = Q − Y_k for the total remainders. At each further stage, more than half of every remaining pyramid is removed as prisms. Consequently, more than half of each total remainder is removed. By X.1, R_k and S_k can each be made smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. The same holds for any fixed multiples mR_k and nS_k: their successive reductions likewise remove more than half. Thus all subsequent choices of sufficiently advanced stages require only finite exhaustion, not a passage to a limit. [12.3, 10.1]

**Step 3.** Fix arbitrary positive integers m and n, and first suppose mA > nB. Definition V.5 gives mX_1 > nY_1; let d = mX_1 − nY_1, so d is positive. For k > 1, the ratios X_k:Y_k and X_1:Y_1 are the same by V.11. Subtracting the first prism totals from the later totals, V.19 gives
(X_k − X_1):(Y_k − Y_1) = X_k:Y_k = A:B.
Definition V.5 therefore gives
m(X_k − X_1) > n(Y_k − Y_1).
Adding back the first prism totals shows that
mX_k ≥ nY_k + d
for every k ≥ 1, with equality when k = 1 and strict inequality thereafter. [5.11, 5.19, D5.5, CN2]

**Step 4.** Choose a stage k for which nS_k < d, as permitted by Step 2. Since X_k is a proper part of P, Step 3 yields
mP > mX_k ≥ nY_k + d > nY_k + nS_k = nQ.
Thus mA > nB implies mP > nQ. [10.1, CN2, CN5]

**Step 5.** Suppose instead that mA = nB. Definition V.5 gives mX_k = nY_k at every stage. If mP > nQ, put e = mP − nQ and choose k with mR_k < e. Then
mX_k = mP − mR_k > mP − e = nQ > nY_k,
contradicting mX_k = nY_k. If nQ > mP, the same argument with the pyramids interchanged, choosing nS_k smaller than nQ − mP, gives the opposite contradiction. Hence mP = nQ. [D5.5, 10.1, CN3, CN5]

**Step 6.** Finally, if mA < nB, apply Steps 3–4 with the pyramids and their corresponding multiples interchanged; this gives mP < nQ. Therefore, for every pair of positive integers m and n, mA and nB have exactly the same comparison—greater, equal, or less—as mP and nQ. By Definition V.5, A:B = P:Q. Thus pyramids of the same height having triangular bases are to one another as their bases. [D5.5]
