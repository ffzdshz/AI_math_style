## Proposition 12.17
**Statement:** Given two spheres about the same centre, to inscribe in the greater sphere a polyhedral solid which does not touch the lesser sphere at its surface.

**Step 1.** Let O be the common centre, and let R and r be the greater and lesser radii, with R>r. Choose a plane E through O and an axis through O perpendicular to E. A plane containing this axis will be called a meridian plane. The definition of a sphere, together with the right-triangle theorem, shows that its central sections are circles and that its surface consists of points at its radius from O. Construct s=(R+r)/2. Applying II.5 to a segment divided into lengths R and r gives
s²=Rr+((R−r)/2)²>Rr.
Thus r<s<R. [11.12, D11.14, 1.47, 1.10, 2.5]

**Step 2.** In a meridian plane apply XII.16 to the concentric circles of radii R and s. Obtain an equilateral inscribed polygon P with an even number n of sides, enclosing and not touching the circle of radius s. This is the direct finite-polygon route: XII.16 supplies the approximation needed for the construction. The triangles joining O to successive vertices have equal corresponding sides, so their central angles are equal. Hence P is regular. Perpendiculars from O bisect its equal sides and have a common length a. Since the circle of radius s is strictly inside P, a>s. Put k=a/R. Then 0<k<1 and
ka=a²/R>s²/R>r.
[12.16, 1.8, 3.3, 1.47]

**Step 3.** Orient P so that two opposite vertices N and S lie on the axis. In E construct a congruent regular n-gon Q, centred at O, with circumradius R and apothem a. Its successive radii determine n equally spaced meridian half-planes. Take the half of the boundary of P running from N to S on one side of the axis, and copy it into each of these half-planes by turning it about the axis. Every copied vertex lies on the greater sphere. At each intermediate height, join the n corresponding vertices in their cyclic order; they form a regular horizontal polygon. At N and S the corresponding vertices coincide. [1.23, D11.14]

**Step 4.** Join corresponding vertices on consecutive horizontal polygons. Corresponding horizontal sides are parallel, since all these polygons have the same orientation and differ only in size and height. Thus each pair of corresponding sides bounds a plane trapezium; the faces adjoining N and S are triangles. These finitely many faces form a closed polyhedral solid K, with O in its interior.

More precisely, let H be any point on the axis between N and S, and let Y be the point at the height of H on the chosen half-boundary of P. Write t=HY. The section of K through H parallel to E is the copy of Q centred at H and reduced in the ratio t:R. Indeed, a section parallel to the bases of each trapezium divides its two meridian sides in the same proportion, so its endpoints have exactly the distances from the axis prescribed by Y. This also describes the whole solid without overlap or gaps. [11.7, 6.2]

**Step 5.** The solid K is inscribed in the greater sphere. Its vertices already lie on that sphere. To verify that its remaining points lie inside, take any point X of K and use the horizontal section just described. Put z=OH. That section is contained in the circle with centre H and radius t, so HX≤t. Also, Y lies on an inscribed polygon side, whence OY≤R. Therefore
OX²=z²+HX²≤z²+t²=OY²≤R².
Here the two decompositions are right-triangle decompositions because the axis is perpendicular to the horizontal section. [3.2, D11.3, 1.47]

**Step 6.** Now let X be any boundary point of K other than a pole, and retain H, Y, t and z from its horizontal section. The point X lies on a side of that section polygon. By similarity with Q, the perpendicular distance from H to the line of this side is kt. Consequently HX≥kt. Likewise, Y lies on a side of P whose line is at perpendicular distance a from O, so OY≥a. Thus
HX²≥k²t²,
and
z²+t²=OY²≥a².
These inequalities follow by dropping the perpendiculars and applying the right-triangle theorem. [3.3, 6.4, 1.47]

**Step 7.** Since 0<k<1, the preceding inequalities give
OX²=z²+HX²
≥z²+k²t²
≥k²(z²+t²)
≥k²a²
>r²,
the last inequality following from ka>r in Step 2. Hence OX>r. The poles also satisfy this inequality, since ON=OS=R>r. Every point of the polyhedral surface is therefore strictly outside the lesser sphere. [1.47]

**Step 8.** Since O is inside K, the lesser sphere is wholly inside K: otherwise a segment from O to a point of that sphere lying outside K would meet the polyhedral boundary at distance at most r, contradicting Step 7. Thus K is a polyhedral solid inscribed in the greater sphere whose surface does not touch the lesser sphere, as required. [D11.14]
