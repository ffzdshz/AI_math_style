## Proposition 12.2
**Statement:** Circles are to one another as the squares on the diameters.

**Step 1.** Let A and B denote the areas of the two circles, and let U and V denote the squares on their respective diameters. Inscribe a square in each circle. Their central angles are equal, hence right angles: the triangles formed by consecutive vertices and the centre have equal radii and equal bases. Repeatedly bisect every central angle and join consecutive endpoints of the resulting radii. Denote the successive inscribed polygons by P₀, P₁, … and Q₀, Q₁, …, also using these letters for their areas. Corresponding polygons have the same number of sides and equal corresponding central angles. [4.6, D1.15, 1.8, 1.9]

**Step 2.** The central triangles are isosceles. Since corresponding central angles are equal, their corresponding base angles are equal, and therefore corresponding central triangles are equiangular. Their corresponding chords have the same ratio as the radii. Consequently, corresponding polygons have equal angles and proportional corresponding sides, and are similar. Proposition 12.1 therefore gives, at every stage j,
Pⱼ : Qⱼ = U : V.
We use 12.1 directly because it supplies precisely the polygon ratio required; the remaining task is to pass from these polygons to the circles by exhaustion and the definition of equal ratios. [1.5, 1.32, 6.4, D6.1, 12.1]

**Step 3.** Consider a side XY of one of these polygons, with centre O. Let the bisector of angle XOY meet XY at N and the circumference at M. The triangles OXN and OYN are congruent, so XN = NY; hence OM is perpendicular to XY. Draw through M a parallel to XY, and through X and Y parallels to OM, forming a rectangle on XY. Its opposite side is perpendicular to OM and therefore lies outside the circle, touching it at M. The minor circular segment on XY, whose central angle is at most a right angle, is strictly contained in this rectangle. The triangle XMY is half the rectangle, since it has the same base and lies between the same parallels. Thus XMY is greater than half that circular segment. Adding all such triangles therefore removes more than half the entire area remaining between the polygon and its circle. [1.4, 3.3, 1.31, 1.29, 3.16, 1.41, CN5]

**Step 4.** Put Eⱼ = A − Pⱼ and Fⱼ = B − Qⱼ. Step 3 gives
Eⱼ₊₁ < ½Eⱼ and Fⱼ₊₁ < ½Fⱼ.
The same inequalities hold for any fixed positive-integer multiples of these remainders. Consequently, by 10.1, given any positive area H and any positive integer r, sufficiently many refinements give rEⱼ < H; likewise sufficiently many give rFⱼ < H. This is a finite exhaustion argument, not an assumption that the polygons eventually equal the circles. Sums, differences and multiples here are operations on areas. [10.1, CN2, CN3]

**Step 5.** Let m and n be arbitrary positive integers. Suppose first that mA > nB. By Step 4 choose j such that
m(A − Pⱼ) < mA − nB.
Then mPⱼ > nB > nQⱼ. Since Pⱼ : Qⱼ = U : V, the definition of equal ratios implies mU > nV.

Similarly, if mA < nB, choose j such that
n(B − Qⱼ) < nB − mA.
Then nQⱼ > mA > mPⱼ, so the same definition implies mU < nV. Thus both strict comparisons between multiples of the circles give the corresponding strict comparisons between multiples of the diameter-squares. [10.1, D5.5, CN2, CN3, CN5]

**Step 6.** Now suppose mA = nB. If mU > nV, let H = mU − nV. By the Archimedean condition for magnitudes having a ratio, choose a positive integer k such that kH > V. Adding the appropriate equal magnitudes gives
(km)U > (kn + 1)V.
But mA = nB gives
(km)A < (kn + 1)B.
Applying the strict-less conclusion of Step 5 to the positive integers km and kn + 1 yields
(km)U < (kn + 1)V,
a contradiction. Therefore mU cannot exceed nV. [D5.4, CN2, CN3, CN5]

**Step 7.** If instead mU < nV, choose a positive integer k such that
k(nV − mU) > U.
Then
(km + 1)U < (kn)V,
whereas mA = nB gives
(km + 1)A > (kn)B.
The strict-greater conclusion of Step 5 now gives the opposite comparison between those multiples of U and V, again a contradiction. Hence, whenever mA = nB, necessarily mU = nV. [D5.4, CN2, CN3, CN5]

**Step 8.** For every pair of positive integers m and n, the multiples mA and nB therefore alike exceed, are alike equal to, or alike fall short of mU and nV in the corresponding comparisons. By the definition of equal ratios,
A : B = U : V.
Thus circles are to one another as the squares on their diameters. [D5.5]
