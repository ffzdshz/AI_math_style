## Proposition 12.11
**Statement:** Cones and cylinders which are of the same height are to one another as their bases.

**Step 1.** Let C and D be cones of the same height, and let A and B denote their circular bases, regarded as magnitudes. We first prove C:D = A:B. This is the direct route because XII.6 applies to polygonal pyramids inscribed in the cones; afterward XII.10 transfers the result to cylinders. [12.6, 12.10]

**Step 2.** Inscribe a square in each base, then repeatedly bisect every intervening arc and join consecutive division points. Perform corresponding subdivisions in the two circles. At stage k the resulting polygons a_k and b_k are similar. Join their vertices to the respective cone vertices, obtaining inscribed pyramids P_k and Q_k. These pyramids increase with k and have the same height. By XII.6 their ratio is a_k:b_k. By XII.1 and XII.2, both a_k:b_k and A:B equal the ratio of the squares on the two diameters. Consequently, at every stage,
P_k:Q_k = a_k:b_k = A:B.
[4.6, 3.30, D11.12, 12.6, 12.1, 12.2, 5.11]

**Step 3.** Consider a chord XY of one of these polygons, and let M bisect its minor arc. We describe the next subdivision explicitly. If O is the circle's centre, then XM = YM by III.29. The triangles OXM and OYM give equal angles at O by I.8. Writing H for the intersection of OM with XY, I.4 applied to OXH and OYH gives XH = HY; hence OM is perpendicular to XY by III.3. The perpendicular to OM at M is tangent to the circle by III.16. It is therefore parallel to XY. Together with XY and the perpendiculars at X and Y, it bounds a rectangle R containing the circular segment between XY and its minor arc. The triangle XMY has the same base and lies between the same parallels as R, so R is double that triangle. [3.29, 1.8, 1.4, 3.3, 3.16, 1.41]

**Step 4.** Join the rectangle R, the triangle XMY, and the circular segment to the vertex S of the cone. The solid over the circular segment is precisely the corresponding portion left outside the current inscribed pyramid. It is properly contained in the pyramid over R. By XII.6, the pyramid over R is double the pyramid over XMY, since their bases have that ratio and their heights are equal. Thus the triangular pyramid added at this subdivision is greater than half the corresponding remaining portion of the cone.

Adding over all the intervening arcs shows that each subdivision removes more than half the entire remainder. Therefore, by X.1, C−P_k and D−Q_k can each be made smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. The same holds for any fixed positive integral multiple of either remainder, because those multiples also decrease by more than half at each subdivision. [D11.18, 12.6, CN5, 10.1]

**Step 5.** Take arbitrary positive integers m and n, and suppose first that mA > nB. Since P_0:Q_0 = A:B, the definition of equal ratios gives mP_0 > nQ_0. Put Δ = mP_0−nQ_0, a positive solid magnitude.

For k>0, the wholes P_k, Q_k and the subtracted parts P_0, Q_0 have the same ratio. Hence, by V.19,
(P_k−P_0):(Q_k−Q_0) = A:B.
The definition of equal ratios therefore gives
m(P_k−P_0) > n(Q_k−Q_0),
and consequently mP_k−nQ_k > Δ. Choose k large enough that n(D−Q_k) < Δ, as Step 4 permits. Then
mC > mP_k > nQ_k+Δ > nD.
Thus mA > nB implies mC > nD. [D5.5, 5.19, 5.11, 10.1, CN5]

**Step 6.** If mA < nB, interchange the two cones and apply the argument of Step 5 with multipliers n and m. It follows that nD > mC, or mC < nD. [D5.5, 5.19, 10.1]

**Step 7.** Suppose instead that mA = nB. Then mP_k = nQ_k at every stage, by the definition of equal ratios. If mC > nD, choose k so that
m(C−P_k) < mC−nD.
This gives mP_k > nD > nQ_k, contradicting mP_k = nQ_k. If mC < nD, choose k so that
n(D−Q_k) < nD−mC.
This gives nQ_k > mC > mP_k, the same contradiction. Thus mC = nD. [D5.5, 10.1, CN5]

**Step 8.** For every pair of positive integers m,n, the multiples mC,nD therefore exceed, equal, or fall short of one another exactly as mA,nB do. By the definition of equal ratios,
C:D = A:B.
Thus cones of the same height are to one another as their bases. [D5.5]

**Step 9.** Now let U and V be cylinders of the same height, with bases A and B. Take cones C and D on those respective bases and with that same height. By XII.10, U = 3C and V = 3D. Equal multiples have the same ratio as their corresponding parts, so
U:V = C:D = A:B.
Hence cylinders also are to one another as their bases. Therefore cones, and likewise cylinders, which are of the same height are to one another as their bases. [12.10, 5.15, 5.11]
