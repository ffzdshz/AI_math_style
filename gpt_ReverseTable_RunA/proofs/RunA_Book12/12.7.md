## Proposition 12.7
**Statement:** Any prism which has a triangular base is divided into three pyramids equal to one another which have triangular bases.

**Step 1.** Let the prism have triangular bases ABC and DEF, with corresponding vertices A,D; B,E; and C,F. Its lateral faces ABED, BCEF, and CADF are parallelograms. [D11.13]

**Step 2.** Join AF, BF, and AE, and cut the prism by the planes ABF and AEF. The section ABF cuts off the triangular pyramid F–ABC; the remainder is the pyramid with vertex F and quadrilateral base ABED. The section AEF divides this remainder into the triangular pyramids F–ABE and F–ADE. Thus the three pieces are
T₁ = ABCF, T₂ = ABEF, and T₃ = ADEF.
They fill the prism without overlapping interiors, meeting along the cutting faces ABF and AEF. [P1, D11.12]

**Step 3.** In the parallelogram BCEF, the diagonal BF divides the area into equal triangles BCF and BEF. Regard T₁ and T₂ as pyramids with these respective bases and common vertex A. Their bases lie in the same plane, so their heights are equal. By XII.5 their magnitudes are proportional to their equal bases; hence T₁ = T₂. This is the direct triangular-base route: choosing bases on a lateral face gives a common vertex and equal heights immediately, so no polygonal-base generalization is needed. [1.34, 12.5]

**Step 4.** In the parallelogram ABED, the diagonal AE divides the area into equal triangles ABE and ADE. Regard T₂ and T₃ as pyramids with these respective bases and common vertex F. Again their bases lie in the same plane and their heights are equal. XII.5 therefore gives T₂ = T₃. [1.34, 12.5]

**Step 5.** Since T₁ = T₂ and T₂ = T₃, the three pyramids are equal to one another. Together with the division in Step 2, this proves that any prism with a triangular base is divided into three equal pyramids having triangular bases. [CN1]
