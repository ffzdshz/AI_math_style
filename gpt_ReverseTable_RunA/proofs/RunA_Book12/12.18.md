## Proposition 12.18
**Statement:** Spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters.

**Step 1.** Let the two spheres have solid magnitudes S and T and diameters d and e. Write R for the triplicate ratio of d to e. We use the polyhedral route: 12.17 supplies inscribed polyhedra, and 12.8 applies directly to the triangular pyramids obtained by joining their triangulated faces to the centre. No formula for the magnitude of a sphere is assumed. [D11.14, D11.17, D5.10]

**Step 2.** Consider a convex polyhedron containing the centre O, and another obtained by multiplying every distance from O in the same ratio. Such corresponding distances can be constructed by 6.12. For any two vertices, the triangles formed with O have proportional sides about their common angle; hence their corresponding edges are proportional. Consequently corresponding triangular faces are similar, as are the triangular pyramids having vertex O and those faces as bases. [6.12, 6.6, 6.4, 6.5, D11.9]

By 12.8, every pair of corresponding triangular pyramids has the triplicate ratio of the corresponding radial distances. By 5.12, their sums—the two polyhedra—have that same ratio. In particular, corresponding polyhedra inscribed in our two spheres have ratio R, since their radii have ratio d:e. Also, enlarging a polyhedron by a rational linear factor a multiplies its solid magnitude by a³; this is merely numerical notation for the triplicate ratio. [12.8, 5.12, D11.17, D5.10]

**Step 3.** We show that an inscribed polyhedron can leave less than any assigned positive solid magnitude E outside it. Let the sphere in question have magnitude W and radius r. Choose a positive integer N such that NE > 7W. This is possible by D5.4. Within the sphere draw the concentric sphere of radius Nr/(N+1). By 12.17, inscribe a polyhedron P in the original sphere which surrounds the smaller sphere without touching its surface. Enlarge P about the centre by the factor (N+1)/N, obtaining P⁺. Since the smaller sphere is thereby carried onto the original sphere, P⁺ surrounds the original sphere. [D5.4, D11.14, 6.12, 12.17]

Thus P < W < P⁺. By Step 2,
P⁺ − P = (3/N + 3/N² + 1/N³)P ≤ 7P/N < 7W/N < E.
Therefore W − P < E. Here rational coefficients denote parts and multiples of magnitudes; no scaling assertion about sphere magnitudes has been used. [12.8, 5.12, D5.10, CN5, CN3]

**Step 4.** Fix an inscribed polyhedron U in the first sphere that surrounds its centre, and let V be its corresponding radial copy in the second sphere. By Step 2, U:V = R. We shall verify the definition of equality of ratios for S:T and U:V. [12.17, 6.12, 12.8, 5.12, D5.5]

Let m and n be any positive integers, and suppose mS > nT. By Step 3, choose an inscribed polyhedron P in the first sphere with
S − P < (mS − nT)/m.
Then mP > nT. Its corresponding copy Q in the second sphere satisfies Q < T, so mP > nQ. Step 2 gives P:Q = R = U:V; hence, by equality of ratios, mU > nV. [CN2, CN3, CN5, 5.11, D5.5]

**Step 5.** If instead mS < nT, apply Step 4 with the spheres interchanged and with n and m in place of m and n. It follows that nV > mU, or mU < nV. Thus both strict comparisons between arbitrary multiples of the spheres are preserved for the corresponding multiples of U and V. [D5.5; Step 4]

**Step 6.** Suppose now that mS = nT. If mU > nV, choose a positive integer k such that k(mU − nV) > V. Then kmU > (kn+1)V. But kmS < (kn+1)T, so Step 5 gives the opposite inequality, a contradiction. [D5.4, CN2, CN5]

If mU < nV, choose k such that k(nV − mU) > U. Then (km+1)U < knV. But (km+1)S > knT, so Step 4 again gives the opposite inequality. Therefore mU = nV. [D5.4, CN2, CN5]

**Step 7.** For every pair of positive integers m,n, the comparison of mS with nT agrees exactly with that of mU with nV. Therefore S:T = U:V by D5.5. Since U:V is the triplicate ratio of d:e, the spheres are to one another in the triplicate ratio of their respective diameters. [D5.5, 5.11, D5.10]
