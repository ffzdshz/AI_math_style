## Proposition 12.12
**Statement:** Similar cones and cylinders are to one another in the triplicate ratio of the diameters in their bases.

**Step 1.** Let C and D be similar cones, with vertices S and T, base centres O and U, and base diameters d and e. Their axes are proportional to their diameters; their base radii therefore also have the ratio d:e. Write R for the triplicate ratio of d:e. We prove the assertion for cones by exhaustion with triangular pyramids, since their similarity supplies precisely the required ratio, and then pass to cylinders. [D11.18, D11.24, 5.15, D5.10]

**Step 2.** In each base draw two perpendicular diameters, choosing corresponding radii in the two circles. Repeatedly bisect every angle between consecutive radii, and join consecutive endpoints on the circles. This central-angle construction keeps the two inscribed polygons corresponding at every stage. Let P_j and Q_j be the pyramids joining the resulting polygons to S and T respectively. These pyramids increase as j increases. [1.11, 1.9]

Divide each polygon into triangles by joining its vertices to its centre. The resulting triangular pyramids correspond in pairs, of the form SOA_iA_{i+1} and TUB_iB_{i+1}. The corresponding base triangles have equal included central angles and proportional radii. The corresponding meridian triangles SOA_i and TUB_i have equal right angles and proportional axes and radii. Thus their corresponding sides are proportional in the ratio d:e. Consequently all six corresponding edges of each pair of triangular pyramids have that ratio; their corresponding triangular faces are similar, and the triangular pyramids are similar solids. [6.6, 6.4, 6.5, D11.9]

**Step 3.** Each pair of corresponding triangular pyramids is therefore in the triplicate ratio of d:e, namely R. Adding the antecedents and consequents gives
P_j:Q_j = R
at every stage. This is the direct reason for using triangular pyramids as the approximating solids. [12.8, 5.12]

**Step 4.** We show that each refinement removes more than half the solid still omitted from either cone. Consider consecutive vertices A,B of one polygon, and let M be the new vertex obtained by bisecting the central angle AOB. The bisecting radius OM is perpendicular to AB: the triangles on either side of it, formed with its intersection with AB, are equiangular and have equal corresponding sides. The line through M parallel to AB is consequently tangent to the circle. [6.6, 6.4, 3.16]

The minor circular segment bounded by AB and its arc lies strictly inside the rectangle having AB as one side and its opposite side on this tangent. The triangle AMB is half that rectangle. Join the rectangle, the triangle, and the circular segment to the cone's vertex. The rectangular pyramid contains the entire omitted solid over that segment, while the triangular pyramid over AMB is half the rectangular pyramid, since their heights are equal and their bases are in the ratio two to one. Thus this newly added triangular pyramid is greater than half the omitted solid over the segment. [1.41, 12.6, D11.18, CN5]

The omitted solids over the several circular segments partition the remainder of the cone. Adding the preceding inequalities shows that each refinement removes more than half that remainder. The same argument applies to the other cone. [CN5]

**Step 5.** Hence, by exhaustion, C−P_j and D−Q_j can each be made smaller than any assigned positive solid magnitude. The same holds for any fixed positive integral multiple of either remainder, since those multiples likewise decrease by more than half at each refinement. [10.1]

**Step 6.** We now justify the passage from the approximating ratios to the cone ratio by the definition of equal ratios. Fix P_0,Q_0. Since
P_j:Q_j = P_0:Q_0,
subtracting these corresponding parts gives, for j>0,
(P_j−P_0):(Q_j−Q_0) = P_0:Q_0. [5.19]

Let m,n be arbitrary positive integers. Suppose first that mP_0>nQ_0, and put δ=mP_0−nQ_0. The equality of the preceding ratios implies
m(P_j−P_0)>n(Q_j−Q_0),
and therefore mP_j>nQ_j+δ. Choose j so that n(D−Q_j)<δ. Then
mC>mP_j>nQ_j+δ>nD.
Thus mP_0>nQ_0 implies mC>nD. Interchanging the two cones proves likewise that mP_0<nQ_0 implies mC<nD. [D5.5, 10.1, CN5]

**Step 7.** If instead mP_0=nQ_0, the equality P_j:Q_j=P_0:Q_0 gives mP_j=nQ_j at every stage. Were mC>nD, choose j such that
m(C−P_j)<mC−nD.
Then mP_j>nD>nQ_j, a contradiction. The opposite inequality mC<nD is excluded in the same way by exhausting D. Therefore mC=nD. [D5.5, 10.1, CN5]

All equimultiples of C,D consequently compare exactly as the corresponding equimultiples of P_0,Q_0. By the definition of equal ratios,
C:D = P_0:Q_0 = R.
Thus similar cones are in the triplicate ratio of their base diameters. [D5.5, 12.8, 5.12]

**Step 8.** Finally, let two similar cylinders be given, and take the cones having their respective bases and equal heights. These cones are similar, because their axes and base diameters have the same proportionality as those of the cylinders. Each cylinder is three times its corresponding cone, and equal multiples preserve a ratio. The cylinders therefore have the same ratio as the cones, namely the triplicate ratio of their base diameters. Hence similar cones and cylinders are to one another in the triplicate ratio of the diameters in their bases. [D11.24, 12.10, 5.15]
